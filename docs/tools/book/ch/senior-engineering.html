Senior engineering, technical leadership and career

What changes when an engineer becomes senior, staff or principal, or moves into management: levels and promotion, ownership and influence, balancing quality against delivery, deciding with incomplete information, proposals and design reviews, mentoring and feedback, metrics, incidents, dependencies and escalation, how different kinds of companies work, build versus buy, and long-term technical strategy. One fictional company, Lumen, runs through the chapter, together with two automotive scenarios, and every topic ends with how a senior engineer reasons about it and what to say.

1What seniority actually means

Job titles suggest that seniority is about knowing more. In practice it is about the size and vagueness of the problem you can be trusted with, and about how much of your impact arrives through other people. Everything else in this chapter follows from that one shift.

1.1Seniority is the size of the problem you can own without supervision

Ask five companies what a senior engineer is and you get five ladders, but they all measure the same three things. The first is scope: a junior engineer owns a task, a mid-level engineer a feature, a senior engineer a project or one team's system, a staff engineer a problem that spans several teams, and a principal engineer the technical direction of an organisation. The second is ambiguity: a junior engineer is given the task and usually the approach; a senior engineer is given a goal ("uploads must survive flaky mobile networks") and has to work out which problem to solve first; a staff engineer is given a problem area ("video costs are out of control") and has to find out what the problems even are. The third is time horizon: days, weeks, a quarter, a year, several years.

The phrase that matters is "without supervision". A mid-level engineer can build an excellent feature, but someone else decided that the feature was the right thing to build, checked that it fits next to the other features and noticed when the plan stopped making sense. A senior engineer does those things for their own project. When a manager says "I don't have to worry about that project", they are describing seniority more precisely than any ladder document.

This also explains why seniority is not the same as years of experience or technical depth. An engineer with fifteen years of deep expertise in codecs who still needs someone else to decide what to work on next is, in ladder terms, operating at a mid level, however impressive the code. An engineer with six years who reliably turns a vague goal into a shipped, operated system is senior.

Why it matters

Interviews for senior roles are designed around this. "Tell me about a project you led" is not a question about the technology; the interviewer is listening for who decided what, how vague the starting point was, how many teams were involved and how long the consequences lasted. An answer full of technical detail but with "my manager told us to" in the first sentence signals the level below.

1.2Leverage: your output is measured through other people

Andy Grove, who ran Intel, defined a manager's output as the output of the organisation they influence. The same definition fits senior individual contributors, and it is the second shift. A strong mid-level engineer is measured by what they build. A senior engineer is measured by what their team builds: the design review that saved a quarter of rework, the onboarding guide that made two new hires productive in a week, the flaky test suite they fixed that had been costing everyone twenty minutes a day. Grove called these high-leverage activities: things that affect many people or a long period of time for a small investment.

Leverage is easy to underestimate because it is invisible in your own commit history. Suppose a team of eight engineers each loses thirty minutes a day to a slow, flaky CI pipeline. That is four engineer-hours a day, roughly half an engineer. A senior engineer who spends two weeks fixing it has, after about a month, paid back the time and from then on adds half an engineer to the team permanently. The same two weeks spent writing features would have added two weeks of features. This arithmetic is why senior engineers spend time on things that do not look like "their" work: reviews, documentation, tooling, mentoring and the decisions that stop work from being thrown away.

Pitfall

Leverage does not mean stopping coding. Senior and staff engineers who lose contact with the code lose the judgement that makes their reviews and decisions worth anything, and they lose credibility with the engineers who do the work. The usual healthy pattern is to keep some real code on the critical path (not only small fixes), but to choose it for its leverage: the prototype that settles a design argument, the tricky core piece, the migration tool.

2Levels, ladders, tracks and staff archetypes

Companies write down what they expect at each level so that hiring, pay and promotion are not decided by who argues best. Knowing how these ladders are built tells you what evidence counts, why the step to staff feels different from every step before it, and why there is more than one way to be a staff engineer.

2.1How an engineering ladder is built

A career ladder (also called a levelling guide or career framework) is a table. The rows are levels; the columns are dimensions such as technical skill, delivery, scope and impact, collaboration and leadership, and sometimes operational excellence. Each cell describes the behaviour expected at that level, written as observable outcomes rather than personality traits: not "is a strong communicator" but "writes design documents that other teams can review without a meeting".

Ladders exist for three reasons, and all three explain how to use them. First, fairness: without a written standard, promotion goes to the people who are most visible to the most senior managers. Second, calibration across teams: the same rating should mean the same thing in the Feed team and the Media platform team (Section 3.2). Third, pay: levels map to salary bands, so the ladder is also a compensation document, which is why companies are careful with it.

Most tech companies have a separate individual contributor (IC) track and a management track, joined at a level where the two are paid roughly the same. The names and numbers differ but the shape is constant. Public data collected by sites such as levels.fyi gives the usual mapping: at Google the IC levels run L3 (new graduate), L4, L5 (senior), L6 (staff), L7 (senior staff), L8 (principal) and above; Meta uses E3 to E8 for the same steps; Amazon has SDE I, II and III (senior) followed by principal; Microsoft uses numeric bands from 59 upwards. A "senior engineer" at a twenty-person startup can correspond to anything from L4 to L6 elsewhere, which is why experienced interviewers ask about scope rather than titles.

Two details of ladders surprise people. Many companies define the senior level as a career level: you are expected to keep growing until you reach it, and staying there for the rest of your career is perfectly acceptable. Above it, promotion is no longer expected; it happens when the organisation needs work at a larger scope and you are doing it. And the higher levels are written more vaguely than the lower ones, because the work itself is less defined: "sets technical direction for a group of teams" can be done in many ways.

In practice

Read your company's ladder for your level and the next one, and for each dimension write down one piece of evidence from the last six months. Gaps show up immediately, usually in the dimensions that do not involve writing code. Many engineers have never read the document that decides their promotion.

2.2Senior, staff and principal: what really differs

The step from mid-level to senior is mostly more of the same: larger, vaguer projects, done well, with more independence. The step from senior to staff is different in kind, which is why many good senior engineers find it confusing. A senior engineer is usually measured inside one team, on a project the team already decided to do. A staff engineer is expected to work across teams, often on problems that nobody has assigned to anyone, and the main output is frequently a decision, a direction or an unblocked group of teams rather than code.

Talk track

"The difference I see between senior and staff is where the problem comes from. As a senior engineer I take a goal my team already has and make it happen well. At staff level I'm expected to notice the problems between teams that nobody has picked up, decide which of them matter, and get several teams to solve them, often without writing most of the code myself."

2.3The four staff archetypes

Will Larson interviewed staff-plus engineers at many companies for his book Staff Engineer (2021) and found that the role comes in four recurring shapes. They differ in how wide their scope is and whether they stay with one area or move from problem to problem.

The tech lead guides one team's (sometimes a few teams') technical approach and execution: scoping work, reviewing designs, unblocking people, coding some of the critical path. It is the most common shape and the natural next step from senior. The architect is responsible for the direction and quality of a critical area across many teams, for example "all storage at Lumen" or "the vehicle's perception stack"; the role needs deep domain knowledge and enough organisational standing to say no. The solver goes deep on one difficult, high-stakes problem at a time (a performance crisis, a failing migration), fixes it and moves on, usually at the request of senior leadership. The right hand extends an executive's attention: they run organisation-wide initiatives and can speak for the leader, which is useful in large organisations and fragile anywhere else.

The archetypes help in two ways. They let you describe what you do in words a promotion committee recognises, and they prevent a common mistake: comparing yourself with a staff engineer of a different archetype and concluding you are doing the job wrong. A tech lead who writes fewer company-wide strategy documents than the architect next door is not under-performing.

Interesting detail

The solver and right-hand shapes need an organisation large enough to have many cross-cutting problems and executives whose attention is the bottleneck, so they are mostly found at large companies. At a start-up, "staff engineer" usually means a tech lead with a broader remit.

2.4Career tracks: IC, tech lead, architect, engineering manager

Every senior engineer eventually faces the question of whether to move into management. The two tracks are different jobs, not two speeds of the same job. An engineering manager is responsible for people: hiring, growth, performance, team health and the team's delivery. Their output, in Grove's sense, is the team's output, and their tools are one-to-ones, hiring decisions, feedback and organisational design. A staff engineer is responsible for technical outcomes across a scope and keeps their tools technical: designs, reviews, prototypes, proposals.

The tech lead and architect are roles rather than separate tracks at most tech companies. A tech lead is an IC with a team's technical direction as their primary responsibility; an architect is an IC with an area's direction. In automotive organisations and many large enterprises, by contrast, "architect" is often a formal position with its own title and approval authority (for example a system or software architect who signs off architecture work products in an ASPICE process), and the role is closer to a separate track.

Charity Majors' widely cited essay "The Engineer/Manager Pendulum" (2017) argues that the best leaders often swing between the two tracks: a few years of management teach you how organisations work, and a return to hands-on engineering keeps your technical judgement current. The practical point is that switching is reversible, especially early. The thing to avoid is drifting into management because it looks like the only way up, which is the reason companies built the IC track in the first place.

Pitfall

"Tech lead manager" (one person as both tech lead and people manager of a team) works for small teams and burns people out on larger ones, because both halves expand to fill the week. If you take such a role, agree with your manager which half gives when time runs out. Usually it should be the coding.

Talk track

"I'd choose management if what I want to grow is the team: hiring, developing people, shaping how the organisation works. I'd stay on the IC track if what I want to grow is the size of the technical problems I can solve. Both are leadership roles; they differ in what you're accountable for."

2.5Titles travel badly between companies

Titles are local. A start-up gives "senior" or "lead" titles early because it cannot compete on salary, and a "principal engineer" at a fifty-person company may own less scope than a senior engineer at a large company. Automotive suppliers often have long title ladders (engineer, senior engineer, expert, senior expert, chief expert) whose steps do not line up with tech-company levels, and German companies frequently map IC levels to collective-agreement pay groups rather than to a public ladder. When you change company or are interviewed, describe your scope (how many teams, how much ambiguity, how long the consequences last) instead of relying on the title, and expect the new company to level you on that.

3Performance reviews and promotion

Performance reviews decide pay and promotion, and the process has a structure that is rarely explained to the people being reviewed. Knowing what happens in the room you are not in tells you what evidence to collect and when.

3.1How a review cycle works

Most tech companies run a review cycle once or twice a year with the same five steps. You write a self-review: what you did, what impact it had, with links and numbers. Several peers (often three to six, chosen together with your manager) write feedback on how you worked with them. Your manager drafts an assessment and a proposed rating against the expectations of your level. Managers then meet in calibration to compare their proposed ratings across teams. Finally the outcome is communicated: rating, pay adjustment, promotion decision and a development plan.

The self-review is the document that matters most and is usually written worst. People list activities ("worked on the transcoding migration, reviewed many PRs, helped with on-call") instead of impact ("led the migration of three of four pipelines to the shared platform; cost per transcoded minute fell 38 %; zero customer-visible incidents during cut-over"). The manager can only defend in calibration what is written down, and they have many people to defend. Write the review so that someone who has never met you could see the level of the work.

3.2Calibration: why ratings are compared across teams

Calibration exists because managers differ. One manager rates everyone "exceeds" to keep the team happy; another rates strictly because they believe high ratings should be rare. Without calibration, your rating would depend more on who your manager is than on what you did. In the meeting, managers present each person briefly, others challenge ("that sounds like solid senior work, not beyond it; what was the scope?"), and ratings are adjusted until the same rating means roughly the same thing across the group. Some companies also apply a target distribution, which forces trade-offs between teams.

Two consequences follow. First, your manager is your advocate in a room where you cannot speak, so give them the material: a crisp summary of your three most important contributions, each with scope and impact. Second, other managers and senior engineers in the room may know your work only from what they saw: the design review you ran for their team, the incident you led. Cross-team visibility is not vanity; it is evidence that calibration can use.

Pitfall

Recency bias is strong in calibration: the last two months are remembered, the first eight are not. Keep a running record (Section 3.4), and make sure your manager hears about important work when it happens, not in the review.

3.3Promotion: show the next level, then ask

Most companies promote people who are already working at the next level, not people who are expected to grow into it. The usual rule is that you demonstrate next-level scope and behaviour consistently, often for six months to a year, and the promotion recognises it. At larger companies the decision is made by a promotion committee or a calibration group of senior people outside your team, reading a written case (a "promo packet"): your manager's summary, your own description of your work, peer feedback, and links to design documents and results.

This has a practical implication: promotion is partly a project you manage. You need work whose scope is visibly at the next level, which your manager must help you get, and you need it documented. "Do great work and you'll be noticed" works only where the people deciding already see your work, which is rarely true above senior level. The reverse is also true: a promotion case that is all packaging and no substance does not survive a committee that reads the design documents.

Numbers

Typical (not universal) figures at large tech companies: review cycles once or twice a year; three to six peer reviewers; time at a level before promotion to senior often two to four years, and to staff longer and much less predictable, because it requires a staff-sized problem to exist. Several companies publish their ladders (for example the open-sourced frameworks collected at progression.fyi), which is the best source for your target employer.

3.4Scenario: preparing a promotion case

You have been a senior engineer at Lumen for two years and think the last year was staff-level work. A senior engineer reasons about this in four steps.

First, check against the ladder, not against how hard you worked. Take the staff column of the ladder and map your work to it. The transcoding migration touched four product teams and changed how all of them handle video; you wrote the proposal, ran the design review, negotiated the order of migration with each team, and led the cut-over. That is cross-team scope. Your mentoring of two engineers and your on-call improvements are valuable but are also expected of a senior engineer; they support the case but do not carry it.

Second, collect evidence that a stranger can verify. Julia Evans popularised the "brag document": a running list of what you did, why it mattered and links to proof, kept all year so you do not have to reconstruct it from memory. For the case, turn it into three to five impact stories, each with the problem, your specific role, what changed and a number: "cost per transcoded minute down 38 %", "four teams migrated with no customer-visible incident", "time to add a new output format down from three weeks to two days".

Third, find out who decides and what they look for. Ask your manager how the committee works and what failed cases lacked. Often the answer is "scope was not clearly beyond the team" or "the impact was the team's, not clearly the candidate's". Ask a staff engineer from another team to read your case; they will spot the gaps a committee would see.

Fourth, ask for feedback early, and treat a "not yet" as data. If your manager says the case is not ready, ask exactly what is missing and agree on a project that would supply it. A good manager will say something like: "the migration is clearly staff scope; what's missing is evidence that you set direction rather than executed someone else's; the storage-tier strategy next half would show that." That is a plan, not a rejection.

Talk track (to your manager)

"I'd like to be considered for staff in the next cycle. Here's how I map my last year to the staff expectations, with links. Where do you see gaps, and is there work coming up that would close them? I'd rather hear 'not yet, because…' now than in six months."

3.5What reviews reward badly: glue work and promotion-driven projects

Tanya Reilly's talk "Being Glue" (2019) named a problem every team has: the work that holds a team together (onboarding new people, noticing that two teams are building the same thing, writing the missing documentation, chasing the dependency nobody owns) is essential, mostly invisible, and often under-rewarded in reviews because it does not produce a feature with your name on it. Engineers who do a lot of it early in their career can find themselves "not technical enough" for promotion. The fix is not to stop doing glue work but to make it deliberate and visible: agree with your manager that it is part of your role, connect it to outcomes ("the onboarding guide cut time to first production change from three weeks to one"), and make sure it is not crowding out the technical work the ladder also expects.

The opposite failure is promotion-driven development: launching new systems because launches are visible, while maintenance, migrations and deletions are not. Companies that reward only launches accumulate half-finished platforms. A senior engineer can push back by framing maintenance work in the same impact language as launches ("removed the legacy pipeline, saving 0.7 engineers of on-call load and 22 % of storage cost"), and a good staff engineer helps the ladder itself reward it.

Why it matters

Understanding what reviews reward is not cynicism. It lets you choose work that is both valuable and recognised, argue for valuable work that is not yet recognised, and avoid being surprised by an outcome that was predictable from how the process works.

4Ownership, influence and cross-team work

Above mid-level, most of what you need done is done by people who do not report to you and have their own priorities. Ownership tells you what you are on the hook for; influence is how you get other teams to act without being able to tell them to.

4.1Ownership means outcomes, not tasks

"I own the upload service" can mean two very different things. The narrow meaning is that tickets about the upload service are assigned to you. The meaning a senior engineer is expected to live is that you are responsible for the outcome the service exists for: uploads that succeed on bad networks, at a cost the company can afford, with problems found before users find them. Under the second meaning, a bug in the mobile client that makes uploads fail is your problem too, even though the code belongs to another team; your job is not to fix their code but to notice, make the problem visible to the right people and make sure it gets fixed.

Ownership also includes the unglamorous long tail: operating the system after launch, writing the runbook, answering the support team's questions, deprecating the old version, and eventually deleting it. A system without an owner decays quietly until it fails loudly, and the most common organisational gap a staff engineer finds is a critical system that everybody uses and nobody owns.

In practice

Ownership is clearest when it is written down: a service catalogue entry with the owning team, an on-call rota, a channel for questions, the dashboards and the documents. If you cannot answer "who gets paged when this breaks at 3 a.m.?" for a system, ownership is not settled.

4.2Influence without authority: trust is the currency

A staff engineer cannot order another team to change its roadmap. What they can do is make it easy and attractive for that team to agree, and that depends on trust built in advance. It helps to think of trust as a bank account. You pay in by being right about technical questions (and saying so plainly when you were wrong), by helping other teams with their problems before you need anything from them, by keeping promises, and by writing clearly enough that people can check your reasoning. You withdraw when you ask a team to change its plans for your project. Engineers who arrive at another team for the first time with a large request are trying to withdraw from an empty account.

Influence also depends on understanding what the other side needs. Every team has its own goals and is measured on them. The Feed team at Lumen is not refusing to migrate to the new media platform because it is stubborn; it has a quarterly goal on feed latency, and migrating costs it three weeks with no visible benefit to that goal. Two approaches work: connect your request to their goal (the new platform produces smaller video renditions, which improves feed start-up time), or, if there is no such connection, raise the priority question to the level where both goals are owned, usually the shared director, and let that person make the trade-off.

A simple tool for a cross-team project is a stakeholder map: everyone whose support you need or whose work is affected, placed by how much power they have over the outcome and how much the change affects them.

The map is not a bureaucratic deliverable; you can draw it in five minutes on paper. Its value is that it forces two questions people skip: who could stop this project, and who will be surprised by it? On the Lumen migration, the answer to the first question includes finance, because the new pipeline changes the cloud bill, and security, because it moves user content between storage systems. Neither cares about codecs, and both can block the launch in its last week if they hear about it then.

Pitfall

Influence is sometimes confused with persuasion in meetings. Large decisions are rarely made in the meeting itself; they are made in the one-to-one conversations before it ("pre-wiring"), where people can raise objections without losing face. If the first time a director hears your proposal is in a group review, expect them to defend the status quo.

4.3Working across teams: interfaces, asks and shared plans

Cross-team work fails in predictable ways: two teams each think the other is doing a piece; a team agrees to something in a meeting that never enters its backlog; an interface changes without the consumer hearing. The remedies are equally predictable. Write the interface down as a contract (API schema, ownership of each piece, what happens on failure, how it will be versioned). Make every request to another team explicit, with what you need, why, and by when, and confirm that it is in their plan, not just in their goodwill. Keep one shared plan for the cross-team project with named owners for each piece, and review it with all teams on a fixed rhythm.

It also helps to know why such work is hard. Every team boundary is a communication boundary, and Conway's law (Section 11.1) says that systems end up shaped like the communication structure of the organisation that builds them. A cross-team project is effectively a temporary change to that structure: for a few months, the teams have to communicate as if they were one. Somebody has to own that temporary structure, and on most projects that is the staff engineer or tech lead.

4.4Communicating up: decision-ready, bottom line first

Directors and executives read many messages a day and act on few of them. A message that reaches them should be decision-ready: what is happening, what it means for the things they care about (dates, money, customers, risk), what you recommend, and what you need from them. The military habit of putting the bottom line up front (BLUF) fits well. Compare "We've been investigating the transcoding issue and found several contributing factors, including…" with "The video stories launch is at risk by two weeks. I recommend we launch on the original date with four of the six formats; the other two follow two weeks later. I need your agreement by Thursday to tell marketing."

The same rule applies to bad news: give it early, with a plan. Executives forgive problems; they do not forgive surprises, because a surprise means they could not act while acting was cheap. A status report that is green until the week before launch and then red (a "watermelon" report, green outside and red inside, described in Project management 10.1) destroys more trust than an honest amber three months earlier.

Talk track

"Here's the one-line version: we will miss the date by two weeks unless we cut two formats. My recommendation is to cut them and ship on time. The detail is in the doc; the decision I need from you is which of those two you prefer, by Thursday."

5Balancing technical excellence, delivery, risk and business

Every engineering decision trades quality, speed, risk and money against each other. Junior engineers tend to treat one of them as absolute; senior engineers make the trade-off explicit, protect the few things that must never be traded, and make sure the people who own the business consequences take the business decisions.

5.1Four forces, and the things that are not negotiable

Technical excellence (a design that will stay healthy), delivery (shipping when it matters), risk (what could go wrong and how badly) and business needs (revenue, cost, customer commitments) pull in different directions on almost every project. None of them wins automatically. A perfect design shipped after the market moved is a failure; so is a fast launch that loses users' photos.

The practical approach is to separate three kinds of quality. Internal quality is how easy the code is to change: structure, tests, documentation. It can be traded temporarily, as long as the debt is known and paid back (Section 5.2). External quality is what users experience: latency, reliability, polish. It is a business trade-off, and the business owner should make it with the engineering facts in front of them. And a small set of things are not negotiable at any deadline: security, privacy, data integrity, legal compliance and, in vehicles, functional safety. A senior engineer's job is to keep that last list short, explicit and defended, so that everything else can be traded calmly.

Why it matters

Engineers who defend every quality attribute equally lose all the arguments, because the business learns that "engineering always says no". Engineers who defend nothing ship disasters. Saying "we can cut the polish and the admin tools, but not the encryption of stored videos" is what makes the refusal credible.

5.2Technical debt is a loan; decide when to take one and when to repay

Ward Cunningham's metaphor of technical debt (1992) is useful precisely because loans are sometimes the right decision. Shipping a simpler design to meet a launch borrows time; the interest is the extra effort every later change costs until the design is fixed. Martin Fowler later sorted debt by two questions: was it taken deliberately or by accident, and prudently or recklessly? "We know this pipeline won't scale past ten thousand uploads a minute; we'll ship now and replace it before we get there" is deliberate and prudent. "We don't have time for design" is deliberate and reckless. Most debt is inadvertent: the team learned how the system should have been built only by building it.

The useful senior-level question is not "how much debt do we have?" but "where are we paying interest?" Debt in code nobody touches costs nothing; debt in the module every feature passes through costs every sprint. Spend repayment effort where the interest is highest, and make the case for it in the business's terms: "every new output format currently takes three weeks because of the pipeline's structure; two months of refactoring brings that to three days, and product has eight new formats planned this year." The mechanics of tracking and paying debt are in Software architecture 13.3 and Software quality 6.5.

5.3"No", and the better answer: "yes, if"

Senior engineers are asked for things that cannot all be done. A flat "no" ends the conversation and usually escalates it. A better answer makes the trade-off visible: "yes, we can add the live-streaming feature this quarter, if we move the storage migration to next quarter; here is what that costs" or "yes, by that date, if we drop the two least-used formats". This shows that you understand the request, puts the choice with the person who owns the priorities, and keeps the facts honest.

The classical framing is the project triangle of scope, time and resources, with quality in the middle. If one corner is fixed, another has to move. In software, "resources" moves least willingly, because adding people to a project costs time before it saves any (Section 5.4), so in practice the trade is usually scope against time.

If the date had been an internal target, the right move would be to move it once, early, with an honest new date and a confidence level ("90 % confident in 20 November"), instead of three small slips, each of which costs more trust than the last.

Can scope be cut by user value? This is usually the best lever. Look at the data: of the six renditions, two are for older devices that make up 3 % of video views. Stories can launch with four renditions, with older devices falling back to a still image plus audio for two weeks. Features behind a flag, a dark launch (the code runs in production without users seeing it) and a staged rollout by percentage of users also turn one big risky date into several small ones.

Would more people help? Fred Brooks observed in The Mythical Man-Month (1975) that adding people to a late software project makes it later. The reasons are mechanical: new people need weeks to become productive, and they take time from the people already on the project to get there; and the number of communication paths in a team grows as \(n(n-1)/2\), from 10 for five people to 45 for ten. People help only when the remaining work splits into pieces that are truly separable and there are months, not weeks, of runway. Here, one experienced engineer from the Editor team who already knows the codec library could take the thumbnail generation, which is separate. A second or third would slow things down.

What quality can be traded? The admin dashboard for transcoding jobs can wait; for the first two weeks, operations can be manual with a runbook. Monitoring and alerting cannot wait, and neither can the deletion path that removes a user's videos when they delete their account, because that is a privacy obligation.

The result is a decision you can take to the product director: launch on the date with four renditions, one extra engineer on thumbnails, manual operations for two weeks, and the remaining two renditions two weeks later. The decision is theirs, because the trade-off is between users on older devices and the campaign; your job was to make it a clear choice with honest numbers.

Talk track (to the product director)

"We're three weeks behind on transcoding. If we keep all six renditions, we miss the campaign. My recommendation: launch on the date with four renditions, which cover 97 % of video views; older devices get a fallback for two weeks. I'd borrow one engineer from Editor for thumbnails. I need your decision on the fallback by Friday so we can tell marketing and support."

Pitfall

The most expensive mistake in a slipping project is hoping. Teams often know they are behind weeks before they say so, because each person hopes to catch up. A senior engineer creates the conditions where "we're behind" is said early: plans with visible milestones, demos instead of percentage-complete estimates, and a reaction to bad news that rewards the messenger.

6Making decisions with incomplete information

The decisions that reach senior engineers are the ones where the facts are incomplete, people disagree and waiting has a cost. Better decisions come less from cleverness than from a few habits: classify the decision before spending effort on it, price the information you could still get, and write down what would make you change your mind.

6.1Not deciding is also a decision

When a decision is hard, it is tempting to ask for more analysis. Sometimes that is right; often it is a way of avoiding the discomfort of being wrong. Waiting has two costs that are easy to overlook: the cost of delay (the value lost while nothing happens: the feature not shipped, the team blocked, the cost still accumulating) and the fact that the default option is being chosen implicitly. A team that "hasn't decided yet" between two storage designs is, in practice, continuing to build on the old one.

The opposite failure is deciding big, irreversible questions on instinct because "we need to move fast". The skill is to know which kind of decision you are facing.

6.2Classify first: reversibility and impact

In his 2015 shareholder letter, Jeff Bezos distinguished "Type 1" decisions, which are consequential and irreversible or nearly so (one-way doors), from "Type 2" decisions, which are reversible (two-way doors), and warned that large organisations tend to use the heavy Type 1 process for everything and become slow. Combining reversibility with impact gives a simple map of how much process each decision deserves.

Two refinements make the map more useful. First, reversibility is often something you can design in. A database migration is a one-way door if you switch over in one night and a two-way door if you run both systems in parallel with a flag and compare their results. Part of a senior engineer's contribution is turning one-way doors into two-way doors, so that more decisions can be taken quickly. Second, the cost of reversal grows with time: choosing a message queue is reversible in week one and nearly irreversible after fifty services depend on it. Decisions should be revisited while they are still cheap to change. The architectural side of this is in Software architecture 13.2.

Bezos also wrote (2016) that most decisions should be made with around 70 % of the information you wish you had, because waiting for 90 % usually means being slow. The number is a rule of thumb, not a law; the useful idea is that you should be about as uncomfortable as the decision is reversible.

6.3Techniques that make uncertain decisions better

Write the assumptions down. Most decisions rest on a few beliefs about the future: "video volume will triple", "the vendor's price will stay flat". Listing them shows which ones the decision is sensitive to and gives you something to check later.

Run a pre-mortem. Gary Klein's technique (Harvard Business Review, 2007): before committing, the team imagines that it is a year later and the decision has failed badly, and everyone writes down why. Imagining that a failure has already happened makes people far better at finding reasons for it than asking "what could go wrong?", which invites optimism; Klein cites research finding that this "prospective hindsight" increased the ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30 %. The output is a list of risks to mitigate or monitor.

Buy information cheaply. A time-boxed spike or prototype, a load test, a conversation with a team that used the technology, or a small experiment in production can often turn a guess into a measurement. Section 6.4 shows how to tell whether that information is worth its price.

Set a revisit trigger. Write down what evidence would make you reopen the decision: "if p99 transcoding latency exceeds two minutes or the cost per minute exceeds the budget for two consecutive months". This lets the team commit fully now while keeping the decision honest, and it stops decisions from being reopened every time someone new is unhappy with them.

Separate the decision from the outcome. Annie Duke calls judging a decision by its outcome "resulting". A good decision can turn out badly (the vendor went bankrupt, which nobody could reasonably have predicted) and a bad one well. Review decisions by asking whether the reasoning was sound given what was known, otherwise teams learn to avoid any decision that might be blamed.

6.4Is the spike worth it? The value of information

At Lumen, two designs compete for the new transcoding job scheduler. You are about 70 % confident that design A is right. A two-week spike would tell you much more, but it would also delay the whole team by two weeks. Should you decide now or investigate first? The answer depends mostly on how expensive it would be to reverse the decision if it turns out wrong, and the figure below makes that dependency visible.

The model is deliberately simple. Deciding now costs, on average, the probability of being wrong times the cost of reversing: with 70 % confidence and a four-team-week reversal, that is 0.3 × 4 = 1.2 team-weeks. Running the spike costs its two weeks of delay plus the risk that remains afterwards (the model assumes the spike removes three quarters of the doubt), 2 + 0.3 × 4 / 4 = 2.3 team-weeks. Decide now. If reversing would cost half a year of a team (26 team-weeks), deciding now costs 7.8 and the spike 3.95: investigate. The break-even is where the two are equal, at a reversal cost of \(4s / (3(1-p))\), about 8.9 team-weeks for these numbers. At 90 % confidence it moves out to about 27 team-weeks: when you are fairly sure, only the very expensive decisions justify the delay.

Real decisions are not this tidy, but the structure holds. Information is worth buying when the decision is expensive to reverse and you are genuinely unsure and the information is cheap compared with being wrong. When any of the three is missing, decide.

Numbers

From the model (reproduced by experiments/check_interactive.js): 70 % confidence, 2-week spike: decide now below about 9 team-weeks of reversal cost. 60 % confidence, 1-week spike: the spike pays above about 3.3 team-weeks. 90 % confidence, 2-week spike: decide now up to about 27 team-weeks.

6.5Traps, and how to commit after disagreeing

A few biases distort technical decisions often enough to name. Sunk cost: continuing a failing approach because of what has already been spent ("we've put eight months into this framework"); only future costs and benefits matter. Anchoring: the first estimate or option mentioned shapes everything after it, which is why it helps to ask for independent estimates before discussing them. HiPPO (the highest-paid person's opinion): the most senior person speaks first and the discussion ends; senior people who want honest input should speak last. Confirmation bias: looking for evidence that supports the option you already like; the pre-mortem and a deliberately assigned critic counter it.

When a decision is taken against your recommendation, the professional response is disagree and commit: state your disagreement clearly once, make sure it is recorded with your reasoning, and then execute the decision as if it were your own. The phrase comes from Intel and was popularised by Bezos's 2016 letter. It is not the same as staying silent, and it does not apply to the non-negotiables of Section 5.1: if a decision is unsafe, illegal or unethical, the right response is escalation (Section 11.3), not commitment.

Talk track

"I still think the queue-based design is the better long-term choice, for the reasons in the doc, and I'd like that recorded. But the decision is made, and I'm fully behind it. If we see the trigger we agreed, latency above two minutes at peak, I'll raise it again with data."

7Design reviews, technical proposals and alignment

Past a certain size, a team cannot decide anything important in one meeting: the people affected are too many, in different time zones and with different context. Written proposals and structured reviews are how technical decisions scale, and running them well is one of the most visible senior and staff skills.

7.1Why decisions move into writing

A written proposal (called an RFC, request for comments, a design document or a technical proposal, depending on the company) does four things a meeting cannot. It scales: twenty reviewers can read and comment in parallel, at their own time, without a meeting that suits nobody. It forces clarity: vague ideas that sound convincing in a conversation fall apart when written as a sequence of claims. It includes the quiet: people who would not interrupt a confident speaker will leave a sharp written comment. And it leaves a record: a year later, the new engineer can find out why the system is the way it is.

The mechanics of writing a design document and the ADR format are covered in Software quality 7.2 and 9.2. This chapter is about the social process around them, which decides whether a good proposal is adopted.

7.2What a proposal has to contain

Proposals that get decided share a structure. They start with the problem and its context, in terms the reader can verify ("transcoding cost per minute rose 40 % in a year; four teams run separate pipelines"). They state goals and non-goals; the non-goals prevent the review from turning into a discussion of everything. They present options, usually two or three real ones plus "do nothing", evaluated against the same criteria. They give a recommendation and are honest about its weakest point. They describe the rollout and the way back (migration, flags, how to reverse it). And they end with open questions, so reviewers know where their input is most needed.

The most common weakness is a proposal that is really an announcement: one option described in detail, the alternatives mentioned in a sentence each so they can be dismissed. Experienced reviewers notice immediately and respond by reopening the alternatives, which makes the review longer and more hostile. A proposal that takes the alternatives seriously, and admits where they are better, is trusted and decided faster.

7.3The life of a proposal, step by step

The document is the visible part. What decides whether it works is the process around it, which has six stages. Step through them.

Two roles must be clear before stage 3. The decider (approver) is the one person who makes the call if the group does not agree; without one, a review with disagreement ends in a stalemate or in whoever is loudest. Frameworks such as DACI (driver, approver, contributors, informed) or RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) exist mainly to make this one name explicit. And the driver, usually the author, is responsible for moving the decision forward: chasing reviewers, summarising discussions, calling the meeting.

Pitfall

A design review held when the design is finished is either a rubber stamp or a fight, because changing anything now costs weeks. Review early, when the document has options and questions rather than answers, and the reviewers' input can still change the outcome cheaply.

7.4Scenario: an architecture disagreement settled through an RFC

The Upload & capture team and your media platform team disagree about the new transcoding pipeline. Upload wants an event-driven design: each stage (virus scan, transcoding, thumbnailing, tagging) listens for an event on a message bus, does its work and publishes the next event. Your team wants a workflow engine: a central orchestrator that calls each stage and keeps the state of every job. The argument has run for three weeks in chat threads and has started to become personal.

Stage 1: find the goals behind the positions. You talk one-to-one with the Upload tech lead and with two engineers on your own team. Upload's real concern is independence: with an orchestrator, every new stage needs a change in the platform team's code, and they have waited weeks for platform changes before. Your team's real concern is visibility and recovery: when a job fails halfway, support needs to see where it stopped, and retries must not transcode the same video twice. Written down, these are not contradictory goals: (1) teams can add a stage without waiting for another team, (2) every job's state is visible in one place, (3) retries are safe (idempotent). Both tech leads agree to these three goals before any design is discussed. Most of the heat goes out of the argument at this point, because each side's goal is now in the document.

Stage 2: options against the agreed goals. Option A (pure events, called choreography) meets goal 1 well and goal 2 poorly: the state of a job is spread across many services. Option B (central orchestration) meets goals 2 and 3 well and goal 1 poorly, unless stages can be registered without code changes. Option C combines them: the orchestrator runs the core pipeline and keeps job state, stages are registered by configuration, and the orchestrator publishes events so that other teams can react to finished jobs without asking anyone. You recommend C and write down its weakest point: the platform team now operates a workflow engine, a new on-call burden.

Stages 3 to 5: review and decision. Reviewers from four teams comment for a week. Security asks how the event payloads are authorised; finance asks for a cost estimate, which you add. In the meeting, the only open question is whether the Upload team can run its own stage without depending on the platform team's deployment schedule; a stage-registration API settles it. The director of platform engineering, named as approver in advance, decides for option C. The Upload tech lead's remaining concern about the operational burden of the workflow engine is recorded in the decision record, with a revisit trigger: if engine incidents cause more than two customer-visible outages in two quarters, the choice is reopened.

Why it worked

Nobody won the original argument. The proposal moved the discussion from positions (events versus orchestrator) to interests (independence, visibility, safe retries), found an option that served the interests, and gave the remaining disagreement a legitimate home: the record and the trigger. This is the same move negotiators call "separate interests from positions", applied to architecture.

7.5Running a good design review

A design review is a structured critique, and its tone is set by the most senior people in it. Critique the design, never the author ("this path loses jobs if the orchestrator restarts" rather than "you forgot restarts"). Ask questions that reveal risk rather than display knowledge: What happens when this dependency is slow rather than down? How do we roll back? What does on-call see at 3 a.m.? What does this cost at ten times today's volume? What will we regret in two years? Separate blocking concerns from preferences, and say which is which.

As the senior person in the room, your most useful habits are to speak late (so that others' views are heard before yours anchors them), to summarise the decision and the open actions at the end, and to protect the author when the review becomes a pile-on. Design reviews are also where junior engineers learn how senior engineers think; reviewing a design out loud ("I'm worried about this because last year…") is mentoring at scale. The checklist side of design reviews is in Software quality 9.3.

7.6When alignment fails: escalate together

Some disagreements cannot be settled by the people having them, because they are really about priorities that neither side owns. Escalating is then correct, and there is a good and a bad way to do it. The bad way is for each side to complain separately to its own manager, so that the managers inherit a fight with two incompatible versions of the facts. The good way is a joint escalation: both sides write one short document together, with the shared facts, the point of disagreement, the options and each side's recommendation, and take it to the person who owns both priorities. Writing it together often resolves the disagreement before it is sent, and when it does not, the decider gets a fair picture and can decide in one meeting.

8Mentoring, delegation and feedback

A senior engineer's impact multiplies through other people, which means helping them grow, handing them real responsibility and telling them honestly how they are doing. These are skills with structure, not personality traits, and the structure can be learned.

8.1Mentoring, coaching and sponsorship are different things

The three words are often used interchangeably, but they describe different help. A mentor shares experience and advice: "when I led my first migration, I underestimated the long tail; plan a month for the last 10 % of users". A coach asks questions that help the other person find their own answer: "what would have to be true for this estimate to hold?" Coaching is slower but builds judgement, so it suits people who already have some experience in the area. A sponsor uses their own standing to create opportunities: suggesting someone to lead the next project, mentioning their work in calibration, inviting them to the meeting where the decision is made.

Sponsorship is the most powerful of the three and the least talked about. Research popularised by Sylvia Ann Hewlett (Forget a Mentor, Find a Sponsor, 2013) found that people with sponsors were noticeably more likely to ask for and receive stretch assignments and pay rises, and that people from under-represented groups tended to receive plenty of mentoring but less sponsorship. For a staff engineer, sponsoring is cheap and high-leverage: you are already in the rooms where opportunities are handed out.

In practice

A good mentoring relationship has a goal and a rhythm: "get ready to lead a cross-team project in six months", meeting every two weeks, with the mentee setting the agenda. Without a goal, mentoring drifts into pleasant conversations that change nothing.

8.2Delegation: hand over outcomes, at the right level

Senior engineers under-delegate for understandable reasons: it is faster to do it yourself, you know you would do it well, and handing over feels like losing control of the quality. Each reason is true in the short term and wrong in the long term. If you do everything that matters, you become the bottleneck of your team (Section 14.3), nobody else learns to do it, and you cannot take on the larger scope your next level requires.

Delegation works when the level of hand-over matches the person's experience with that particular task. Andy Grove called this task-relevant maturity and stressed that it is per task, not per person. Management 3.0 (Jurgen Appelo) makes the levels explicit as seven steps from "tell" to "delegate"; situational leadership (Hersey and Blanchard) groups them into directing, coaching, supporting and delegating styles.

Most delegation failures are level mismatches. The tech lead thinks they delegated at level 7 ("you own the retry design"), the engineer thinks it is level 3 (they will be asked, then overruled), and both are frustrated when the tech lead rewrites the design in review. Naming the level ("this is a 5: I'll give you my view, but it's your decision") fixes it. Delegate outcomes rather than tasks ("uploads must survive a train going through a tunnel" rather than "add exponential back-off to the upload client"), agree on check-in points proportional to the risk, and let people make mistakes that are recoverable. If you cannot let a mistake happen, delegate at a lower level instead of taking the work back halfway.

8.3Feedback that people can use

Feedback is information about the effect of someone's behaviour, given so that they can choose what to do with it. Two simple tools cover most situations. The situation–behaviour–impact (SBI) model from the Center for Creative Leadership keeps feedback factual: "In yesterday's design review (situation), you interrupted Priya three times while she was explaining the retry design (behaviour); she stopped contributing, and we never heard her concern about duplicate jobs, which turned out to be right (impact)." It avoids judgements about character ("you're dismissive") that people can only argue with.

Kim Scott's Radical Candor (2017) explains why feedback so often fails: it needs both caring personally and challenging directly, and people usually drop one. The resulting four quadrants are worth knowing because each failure feels reasonable from the inside.

A few practical habits make feedback work. Give it soon, while the situation is fresh. Give criticism privately and praise specifically (specific praise teaches; "great job" does not). Make it about one thing. Ask before you tell ("how do you think the review went?"), because people often know already, and their answer tells you where to start. And ask for feedback yourself, visibly, and react well when you get it; a senior engineer who takes criticism defensively teaches the whole team that feedback is dangerous.

Code review is the feedback channel engineers use most. Label comments by weight (blocking, suggestion, nit), explain why rather than just what, and point out good solutions as well as problems. A reviewer who leaves forty nits on a junior engineer's first pull request has given feedback mainly about themselves.

8.4Scenario: a struggling team member

You are the tech lead of the media platform team. Alex, a mid-level engineer who joined four months ago, is struggling: their pull requests go through many rounds of review, two estimates were missed by weeks, and they have gone quiet in design discussions. You are not Alex's manager, but you work with them every day. How do you reason about it?

Expectations. Looking back, Alex's main task was "improve the reliability of upload retries": a goal without a definition of done, in a codebase they did not know. Nobody had said what good would look like after a month. That alone explains a lot. You sit down with Alex and write two concrete outcomes: a design for resumable uploads reviewed within three weeks, and the retry metrics dashboard live in production.

Skill. In conversation it turns out Alex had never worked with the network-condition simulator the team uses to test uploads, and had been testing by hand on a phone. That explains the slow progress and the bounced pull requests. You pair with Alex for two afternoons, split the work into smaller pull requests that can be reviewed in a day, and ask a teammate who knows the client side to be their go-to person for questions.

Context. Alex is on the on-call rota every third week, more than anyone else, because the rota was set up when the team was smaller; on-call weeks wipe out their project time. That is an environment problem, which you raise with the manager, who changes the rota.

Motivation or personal circumstances. If none of the above had explained it, or if things did not improve after a few weeks of support, the next questions (is the work a poor fit, is something happening outside work?) belong in a private conversation with the manager, who owns support and, if necessary, any formal performance process. As a tech lead you share what you observe, factually and with examples, but you do not run that process yourself.

Six weeks later, the resumable-upload design is approved and Alex presents it in the design review. Nothing about Alex changed; the expectations, the tooling knowledge and the on-call load did.

Pitfall

The two common mistakes are opposite. One is to wait, hoping it resolves itself, until the problem has grown into a formal performance issue that could have been a two-week fix. The other is to quietly take the work away from the person; it solves the deadline and tells them, and the team, that struggling leads to being sidelined.

8.5Phrasing for difficult conversations

Difficult conversations go wrong mostly in the first thirty seconds: the opening either describes something the other person can recognise, or it hands them a verdict they have to defend against. A few principles from Crucial Conversations (Patterson and colleagues) and from experienced leads help: describe what you observed rather than what you concluded; state your intent ("I want this design to pass review"); ask for their view before giving your solution; keep to one topic; and end with an agreed next step. 9Engineering metrics and misleading KPIs

Organisations want numbers to steer by, and some engineering numbers are genuinely useful. But a number that is easy to collect is often a poor proxy for what matters, and once it becomes a target, people change their behaviour to move the number instead of the thing. A senior engineer is expected to know which metrics help and to argue well against the ones that do harm.

9.1Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring

The economist Charles Goodhart observed in 1975 that statistical regularities break down once they are used for control; the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later gave it the popular form "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". The social scientist Donald Campbell made the same point about social indicators: the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is corrupted. In engineering this happens quickly because engineers are good at optimising. Make test coverage a target and people write tests that execute code without checking anything. Count pull requests and they split work into smaller pull requests. Reward closed bugs and bugs get closed as "cannot reproduce".

The lesson is not "never measure". It is that metrics are best used for learning (what is happening, where are we slow, did the change help?) and dangerous when used for judging individuals or as targets attached to rewards.

9.2What is worth measuring

Three families of metrics have held up well. The DORA metrics, from the research behind Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble and Kim, 2018), measure delivery: how often you deploy, how long a change takes from commit to production, how often a change causes a failure, and how quickly service is restored; later reports added reliability and, in 2024, rework rate. They work because they measure the system, not individuals, and because speed and stability are measured together, so neither can be bought with the other. The SPACE framework (Forsgren and colleagues, 2021) reminds you that productivity has several dimensions (satisfaction and well-being, performance, activity, communication and collaboration, efficiency and flow) and should never be reduced to one number. And developer experience surveys ask engineers directly where they lose time, which often finds problems no system metric shows. The details are in Software quality 10.5 and 13.3.

Above all of these sit the outcomes the engineering exists for: users retained, revenue, cost per transcoded minute, uploads that succeed. A useful habit is to label every metric as an outcome, an output (features shipped, deploys) or an activity (commits, hours, meetings), and to be most sceptical of the activities.

9.4Using metrics well

Five rules cover most of it. Measure teams and systems, not individuals; individual metrics are the fastest route to gaming and destroyed collaboration. Look at trends, not absolute values, and never compare teams with different work on the same number. Pair every metric with a counter-metric that would expose gaming (speed with stability, coverage with escaped defects). Look at distributions, not averages: a median lead time of one day can hide a long tail of changes that wait two weeks. And before adding a metric, ask which decision it will change; a number that no one will act on is just a cost.

Metrics are also more useful as questions than as answers. "Lead time doubled this quarter" is not a conclusion; it is the start of an investigation, which might find a slow new test suite, a reviewer who left, or a change in the kind of work.

9.5When leadership asks for a productivity number

Sooner or later a director asks: "how productive is engineering, and how do we compare?" Refusing to answer is not an option, and handing over commit counts is harmful. A senior engineer turns the question into one that can be answered: what decision is the number for? If it is "are we investing enough in developer tooling?", developer-survey results and lead-time trends answer it. If it is "is this team in trouble?", talk to the team and look at its delivery and incident trends together. If it is "did the new AI coding tools help?", run a comparison on a few flow metrics before and after, with the survey, and report the uncertainty honestly.

Talk track

"I can give you numbers, but let's pick them for the decision you need to make. If it's whether we're getting faster, I'd show lead time and change failure rate together, plus what engineers say slows them down. I'd avoid anything per person; the moment we measure individuals on activity, we'll get more activity and less of what we actually want."

10Leading a production incident

An outage is the most compressed leadership test in engineering. The technical fix is often the easy part; what prolongs incidents is coordination: everyone debugging, nobody deciding, nobody telling customers. Senior engineers are expected to be able to take command, and the role has a clear structure.

10.1Why incidents need a commander

When something breaks in production, the natural reaction of a group of engineers is for everyone to start investigating. Within minutes there are six people in a call, three hypotheses being tested at once, two changes being made in parallel that interfere with each other, and nobody has told support, the status page or the executives who are now asking in chat. The incident management practice used across the industry, described in Google's Site Reliability Engineering book (2016) and derived from the incident command system that US firefighters developed in the 1970s, solves this by separating roles.

The incident commander (IC) coordinates and decides, and deliberately does not debug. The operations lead (often the on-call engineer) investigates and changes the system, and is the only one who does. The communications lead keeps stakeholders informed on a fixed rhythm. On larger incidents a scribe keeps a timeline, and other engineers join as needed, asked for by name by the IC. The separation is the point: the person deciding is not the person with their head in the logs, and the people changing production are known.

The process side (severity levels, on-call design, tooling) is covered in Software quality 11.4; this chapter looks at it from the commander's seat.

10.2Scenario: two hours in command

At 14:02 on a Tuesday, Lumen's upload error rate jumps to 18 %. The on-call engineer on the media platform team acknowledges the alert and, after a few minutes of looking, declares a severity-2 incident. You are the most senior engineer of the team who is available, so you take the commander role. Step through what happens.

The single most important decision in this incident was the rollback at about 38 minutes, and it was a commander's decision, not an investigator's. The operations lead had a promising hypothesis about connection pooling and wanted ten more minutes. That might have found the cause, but users were failing to upload the whole time, and a rollback was cheap and reversible. "Restore service first, understand later" is the default for good reason: the investigation can continue on a staging system without anyone waiting.

Talk track (opening an incident)

"I'm taking incident command. Current state: upload errors at 18 % since about 14:00, cause unknown. Operations lead is the on-call, and only they change production. Sam has comms; first update to the status page in ten minutes. Everyone else: tell me in the channel if you have information, and don't make changes. Next check-in in fifteen minutes."

10.3The commander's habits

A few habits separate calm incidents from chaotic ones. Say decisions out loud ("we roll back now"), with who does what, so that nobody acts on a guess. Time-box hypotheses: "ten more minutes on the pooling theory, then we roll back regardless". Ask what changed before asking why: most incidents follow a deploy, a configuration change, a traffic shift or an expiring certificate. Keep a log of what was observed and done, with times; it costs little during the incident and is the backbone of the postmortem. Escalate early when you need people or authority you don't have; waking someone up unnecessarily costs a night's sleep, while not waking them can cost hours of outage. And hand over deliberately in long incidents: tired commanders make poor decisions, and a structured hand-over ("here's the state, here's what's in flight, here's what I'd do next") is a strength, not a weakness.

Pitfall

The most common failure for a senior engineer acting as commander is to start debugging, because they are often the best debugger in the room. The moment the commander is reading logs, nobody is coordinating. If you are the person most likely to find the cause, hand the commander role to someone else.

10.4After the incident: the postmortem as a leadership tool

A blameless postmortem (practice and format in Software quality 12.4) asks how the system, including its people, processes and tools, allowed the incident, not who caused it. The reason is practical rather than kind: if people expect blame, they hide information, and the next incident takes longer. The senior engineer's specific contributions are three. Make sure the conversation stays on contributing factors ("the storage-client release passed canary because the canary had no uploads above 100 MB") rather than on the person who deployed. Keep the action items few, owned and dated, because a postmortem with twenty actions produces none. And look across postmortems for patterns: if three incidents in a quarter came from configuration changes that skipped review, that is no longer an operational issue; it is a strategic one, and it belongs in the technical strategy (Chapter 14).

11Dependencies, organisational complexity and escalation

As scope grows, most delays stop being caused by hard technical problems and start being caused by other teams: waiting for an interface, a decision, a release or a vendor. Understanding how organisational structure shapes systems, and how to manage and escalate dependencies, is the core of staff-level execution.

11.1Conway's law and team topologies

Melvin Conway observed in 1968 that organisations which design systems produce designs that copy their own communication structures. If three teams build a compiler, you get a three-pass compiler. The law works in both directions: teams that need to communicate constantly end up building tightly coupled systems, and a system split into components will, over time, need an organisation that mirrors the split. The inverse Conway manoeuvre uses this deliberately: shape teams the way you want the architecture to look, and the architecture follows. (The architectural side is in Software architecture 14.4.)

Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais built on this in Team Topologies (2019), which gives a vocabulary that many organisations now use. There are four team types: stream-aligned teams own a flow of work for users (a product area) end to end; platform teams provide internal services that stream-aligned teams consume; enabling teams help other teams adopt new skills and then step away; and complicated-subsystem teams own a part that needs rare expertise. Teams interact in three modes: collaboration (working closely together, expensive and time-boxed), X-as-a-Service (one team consumes another's service through a clear interface, the cheap steady state) and facilitating (one team teaches another). The central idea behind the types is limiting each team's cognitive load, so that a team can actually own what it is responsible for.

For a staff engineer, the useful skill is to read dependency problems as structure problems. If the Upload team waits for the platform team every sprint, the interaction mode is wrong: it is collaboration that should be X-as-a-Service, and the fix is a self-service interface, not better meetings. If every product team needs the codec team for every feature, the codec team is a bottleneck whose expertise should be packaged into the platform.

11.2Managing dependencies on a cross-team project

Dependencies are where cross-team projects lose their time, and the remedies are mostly about visibility. Draw a dependency map early: for each piece of work, what it needs from whom and by when. The longest chain of dependent work is the critical path; a delay anywhere on it delays the end, while delays elsewhere are absorbed. Put buffers on the critical path, as a block of time before the deadline, rather than padding every task, because padded tasks tend to expand to fill their padding.

Then reduce dependencies wherever you can. Agree interfaces early and build against stubs or contracts, so that teams can work in parallel instead of in sequence. Integrate early and often: the first end-to-end run of a cross-team system always finds surprises, and finding them in month two is far cheaper than in month eight. And make status two-way: ask the teams you depend on not only "are you on track?" but "what do you need from us?", because many dependency slips are caused by the waiting team's own late inputs.

Numbers

Communication paths grow as \(n(n-1)/2\): 3 for three teams, 10 for five, 45 for ten. That is why a project spanning ten teams needs explicit structure (a shared plan, a fixed sync rhythm, one owner of the integration) that a project spanning two does not.

11.3Escalation is a tool, not a failure

Many engineers think of escalation as tattling or as admitting failure. It is neither. Escalation means moving a problem to the level that has the authority to solve it, and it is the right move whenever the problem is beyond your authority: two teams' priorities conflict, a dependency will be late and nobody at your level can change that, or a decision threatens one of the non-negotiables. Not escalating such problems is the failure, because the people who could have acted find out too late.

Good escalations share a pattern. They come early, while there are still options. They contain facts, options and a recommendation, not only a complaint. They have a deadline ("we need a decision by Friday to keep the date"). They are transparent: you tell the other party that you are escalating and why, ideally escalating jointly (Section 7.6), so it is not experienced as an ambush. And they go one level at a time, to the lowest level that owns both sides of the conflict. The project-process side of escalation, including steering committees, is in Project management 10.4.

Talk track

"I want to flag a conflict that neither team can solve on its own. Feed's latency goal and our migration both need the same three weeks of their time this quarter. Option one: Feed migrates in Q1 and we run both pipelines until then, which costs about 40 thousand in duplicate infrastructure. Option two: we migrate them ourselves, and our storage work slips a quarter. I recommend option one. I've shared this with Feed's tech lead, and we need a decision by Friday."

11.4Scenario: a slipping SOP at an automotive supplier

Automotive projects have a deadline that tech companies rarely face: the start of production (SOP), when the vehicle plant starts building cars with your part in them. It is tied to the vehicle launch, the plant's schedule and dozens of other suppliers, so it almost never moves. Imagine you are the lead software engineer at a Tier 1 supplier delivering a front camera ECU, eighteen months before SOP. Step through what happens when a dependency slips.

The senior engineer's reasoning goes in a specific order. First, find the real critical path: the perception features are on track and get most of the management attention, but validation is what will now be late. Second, break the dependency where possible: most of the image-quality tuning does not depend on the new BSP, so it can start on the old one, with a short delta tuning later. Third, reorder for risk: validation runs the tests most likely to find serious problems first, so that if time runs out, what is left untested is the least risky part. Fourth, hold scope: the C-sample feature list agreed with the vehicle manufacturer is the scope, and new requests go into a later software release, which may be delivered by an over-the-air update if the vehicle supports it.

Fifth, escalate with options, early. The vehicle manufacturer's project team will hear about the risk at the next steering meeting (Project management 10.2) and should hear it from you first, with the recovery plan, the remaining risk and what you need from them: perhaps earlier access to test vehicles, or agreement to move one low-priority function to the first post-SOP update. And the chip vendor's late delivery belongs in a separate escalation through the supplier relationship, with facts on what the delay cost; it is the same pattern as Section 13.5.

Talk track (to the vehicle manufacturer)

"I want to raise a risk early. The SoC software drop arrived ten weeks late, which removed our validation buffer. Here's the recovery plan: tuning already runs on the old BSP, validation starts early on HIL, and tests are ordered by risk. With that, we hold SOP with about six weeks of buffer instead of eight. Two things would help: test-vehicle time in month 11, and agreement that the low-speed manoeuvring function moves to the first post-SOP software update."

Interesting detail

The difference from a tech-company launch is that almost nothing moves the date and few things can be cut after the customer has agreed the scope, while software can often still be updated after SOP. That makes "what must be in the SOP software, and what can follow in an update?" one of the most important scope conversations in an automotive programme, and one a senior engineer should start early rather than at the last gate.

12How start-ups, large tech companies and automotive organisations differ

The same senior engineer can thrive in one company and struggle in another, not because their skills changed but because the companies reward different things. The differences follow from what each kind of organisation is short of, and understanding that makes moving between them much easier.

12.1What drives the differences

Each kind of organisation is constrained by something different, and its culture grows around that constraint. A start-up is short of runway: it has a limited amount of money and must find a product that customers want before it runs out, so speed of learning beats everything, and processes that slow it down are removed. A scale-up has found the product and is short of focus and structure: the systems and teams built in a hurry start to break, and the work is turning heroics into something repeatable. A large tech company is short of alignment: thousands of engineers, hundreds of teams, and the hard part is getting many teams to move in the same direction, so writing, influence and process carry more weight. A vehicle manufacturer (OEM) or Tier 1 supplier is short of certainty: it ships hardware that is built in huge numbers, must be safe and legally homologated, and has to be supported for many years, so predictability, traceability and contractual clarity dominate.

Some skills transfer everywhere: clear writing, good judgement about trade-offs, debugging, ownership, the ability to work with people who disagree with you. Others have to be unlearned. An engineer moving from a start-up to a large company must learn that being right is not enough without bringing people along, and that a proposal nobody read did not happen. An engineer moving from a tech company into automotive must learn to respect the reasons behind process (safety cases, traceability, customer contracts) while still pushing to make it lighter where it adds no safety, and must get used to the customer-supplier relationship, where scope and changes are contractual.

Talk track (changing industry)

"In automotive I learned to think in terms of what can't be undone: safety, traceability, commitments to a customer with a fixed SOP. What I'd bring to a faster environment is that discipline for the few decisions that really are one-way doors, while moving fast on everything that isn't. What I'd expect to learn is the speed: shorter feedback loops, shipping behind flags, deciding with less data."

13Build versus buy, vendors, and platform versus product teams

Every engineering organisation repeatedly decides what to build itself, what to buy, and how to share what it builds between teams. These decisions are expensive to reverse, mix technical and commercial reasoning, and are often made badly in both directions: building commodity plumbing out of pride, or buying a product whose limits become the product's limits.

13.1Core and context: what deserves your engineers

Geoffrey Moore distinguished core work, which differentiates a company in the eyes of its customers, from context, everything else that must be done well but does not make customers choose you. Lumen's users choose it for the editing and sharing experience, not for its transcoding; for a car buyer, the driver-assistance experience may differentiate the vehicle, while the diagnostic protocol does not. The general rule follows: build the core, where your engineers' time creates an advantage, and buy or adopt the context, where it only creates cost.

The rule needs two corrections. First, context at sufficient scale can become core through cost: at a few thousand videos a day, transcoding is a line item; at hundreds of millions, its cost and quality can decide the company's margins. Second, the full cost of building is always underestimated, because it is mostly paid after the launch.

13.2A framework, and the cost curves

The decision flow asks three questions in order. Is this what customers choose you for? If so, build it and put strong people on it. If not, does a mature product meet about 80 % of the real need at your scale? If not, build only the part you need or adopt an open-source project. If a product fits, can you leave it within a reasonable time (data export, standard interfaces, contract terms)? If so, buy it and keep it behind an interface at the edge of your system; if not, buy only with an explicit exit plan.

The cost comparison must use total cost of ownership over several years, not the first year. On the build side, the hidden costs are operations and on-call, security patches, upgrades of dependencies, documentation, and the fact that the engineers who built it are not available for core work; maintenance commonly costs a substantial fraction of the original effort every year, for as long as the system lives. On the buy side, they are integration, vendor management, workarounds for missing features, price increases at renewal (especially with usage-based pricing), and the cost of leaving. In the illustrative numbers of Figure 13.1, the cumulative cost after one year is about 520 thousand for building against 150 thousand for buying; after three years 760 against 430; after five years 1000 against 880; the lines cross a little before year six. Change the growth rate of the licence and the crossing moves by years, which is why the price terms in the contract matter as much as the list price.

Pitfall

"Not invented here" (rejecting external solutions because they were not built in-house) and its mirror image, "buy everything", both avoid the analysis. A useful check on yourself: if you are arguing to build, could you explain to the CFO why your engineers' time is better spent on this than on the product? If you are arguing to buy, have you priced leaving?

13.3Scenario: build or buy transcoding

Lumen's transcoding pipeline is ageing, and the team proposes rewriting it on GPU instances using open-source encoders. A product manager points out that the major cloud providers sell managed transcoding services. You are asked for a recommendation.

You start with core versus context. Users do not choose Lumen for its transcoding; but at Lumen's volume, transcoding cost is a visible part of the cloud bill, and the new video-stories format needs a special effect rendering step that no managed service offers. So the answer is not a single build or buy. You compare total cost over three years for the standard renditions (the managed service is cheaper at current volume, and the team's maintenance load falls), check the exit path (the service reads and writes standard formats in your own storage, so leaving means re-implementing the pipeline, not migrating data), and identify the threshold at which building becomes cheaper (roughly three times the current volume, at the quoted prices).

The recommendation: buy the managed service for standard renditions, behind an internal transcoding interface that the product teams call, so that the vendor is replaceable; build only the stories effect step in-house, because it is product-specific; and set a revisit trigger at twice the current volume or at the first contract renewal, whichever comes first. The team that wanted to build is not overruled silently: the rewrite proposal's analysis of encoder settings becomes the quality benchmark against which the vendor is evaluated.

Talk track

"I'd buy the commodity part and build the part that's ours. Standard renditions go to the managed service behind our own interface, so we can leave if prices change; the stories effect stays in-house because no vendor does it and it's part of the product. We revisit at twice today's volume or at renewal, because that's roughly where building starts to pay."

13.4Managing vendors

Buying a product starts a relationship, and senior engineers are often the people who make it work or fail technically. A few things are worth knowing. A service-level agreement (SLA) usually promises service credits when it is missed, not compensation for your losses; your own availability still depends on designing for the vendor's failures. Evaluate with your own data: a proof of concept on your real workload and a conversation with a reference customer of similar size tell you more than any sales presentation. Keep the vendor at the edge of your system behind an interface you own (in software terms, the vendor SDK stays in an adapter; see Software architecture 6.3), so that a change of vendor is a project rather than a rewrite. Plan the exit before you sign: data export, notice periods, price caps at renewal, and for critical embedded software, source-code escrow (a copy of the source held by a third party and released to you if the vendor fails). And build the relationship: named technical contacts on both sides, regular reviews, and shared problem-solving when things go wrong, because a vendor that trusts you will fix your problem first.

13.5Scenario: a supplier dispute about a defect

Back at the Tier 1 camera supplier. In winter testing, the vehicle manufacturer reports that the front camera's images show a colour shift at low temperatures that degrades traffic-light classification. The camera module supplier says the module meets its specification and the problem is the ISP tuning; the tuning team says the tuning is correct and the module's colour response changes in the cold. Each side has sent long emails, and the programme manager wants a commercial claim against the module supplier. How does a senior engineer handle it?

Facts before positions. Reproduce the problem in a climate chamber with controlled lighting, then isolate it with swap tests: the same module with the previous tuning, and a different module batch with the current tuning. If the shift follows the module batch, the cause is in the module; if it follows the tuning, it is the tuning; if it needs both, it is the interface between them. In this case it follows the module batch, but only below minus 20 °C, a temperature that the component specification covers and the module supplier's validation did not test.

Check what was agreed. The component specification and the interface agreement between the companies decide responsibility, not the strength of the emails. Here the specification requires colour accuracy across the full temperature range, so the deviation is the module supplier's. Automotive companies usually run such investigations in a structured problem-solving format, often 8D (eight disciplines, from forming the team through containment, root cause and corrective action to prevention), which keeps the joint work factual.

Separate the technical and the commercial. The engineers' job is to find the root cause and a fix together; who pays is decided afterwards by purchasing and the contract. Mixing the two makes every technical conversation a negotiation and slows down the fix that the customer is waiting for. A short-term containment (a temperature-dependent colour correction in the tuning) can protect the programme while the module supplier fixes the root cause.

Protect the relationship. You will work with this supplier for the whole life of the vehicle programme and probably on the next one. Being firm on facts and polite in tone costs nothing and makes the next problem easier.

Talk track (to the module supplier)

"Our chamber tests show the colour shift follows your module batch below minus 20 °C, independent of the tuning; the data and the setup are attached so your team can reproduce it. The specification covers that range. Can we set up a joint 8D this week? In the meantime we'll add a temperature-dependent correction in the tuning to contain it for the vehicle tests. The commercial side will go through purchasing; I'd like our engineers to focus on the fix."

13.6Platform teams and product teams

Once several product teams need the same capability (transcoding, authentication, deployment, a perception middleware), a company builds a platform and a team to run it. The platform team's success is measured by how much it speeds up the product teams, and the most common failure is forgetting that. A platform built to an internal architecture vision, without asking its users, ends up mandatory and resented, and product teams build workarounds around it.

Platforms that work are run like products. They have users (the product teams) whose problems they study, a roadmap driven by those problems, documentation and self-service so that nobody needs to file a ticket, service-level objectives they are on call for, and a paved road: a supported, well-documented default path that is easier than the alternatives, rather than a mandate. Team Topologies recommends a "thinnest viable platform": start with the smallest set of services, sometimes just a well-maintained wiki page of conventions, and grow only where teams are actually slowed down. Good platform metrics measure the users' outcomes: adoption without mandates, time for a product team to do a common task, and the number of tickets the platform needed to handle by hand.

Why it matters

Platform versus product is one of the most common staff-level debates: when is a shared capability worth a dedicated team, who owns which part, and how much should product teams be allowed to diverge? The answer is rarely technical; it is about the interaction mode between teams (Section 11.1) and whether the platform's users would choose it if they were free not to.

14Long-term technical strategy and removing bottlenecks

Staff and principal engineers are expected to look further ahead than the current project: to see where the organisation's technology is heading, decide what to do about it, and remove what slows everyone down. Strategy sounds grand; done well it is specific, somewhat boring and grounded in what teams are already struggling with.

14.1What a technical strategy is, and what it is not

Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy Bad Strategy (2011) gives the most useful definition. A strategy has a kernel of three parts: a diagnosis that explains what is going on and what makes it hard, a guiding policy that says how you will deal with it (and, by implication, what you will not do), and coherent actions that carry the policy out and reinforce each other. Rumelt's "bad strategy" is just as recognisable: goals presented as strategy ("be the best video platform"), fluff, or a list of unrelated initiatives that avoids choosing.

Will Larson's practical advice for engineering strategy is to write it from the bottom up: "write five design docs, then synthesise". Recent design documents already contain the organisation's real problems and the decisions it keeps facing. Read five of them side by side, and the recurring problem is the diagnosis; the recurring way the good ones solved it is the guiding policy. A strategy written this way is concrete because it starts from real decisions, and it is accepted because teams recognise their own problems in it.

At Lumen, the five documents (upload retries, GPU transcoding, storage tiers, a tagging pipeline, client caching) all spend pages working around the same fact: each product team handles video differently, so every improvement has to be made four times and costs keep rising. The diagnosis follows directly. The guiding policy is a real choice with losers: product teams give up control of media processing in exchange for a platform that does it for them. The actions are specific and coherent: migrate the four pipelines in three quarters, stop new bespoke pipelines, and build the per-team cost dashboard first, because visible costs make the migrations easier to prioritise.

Pitfall

A strategy that nobody has to change their plans for is not a strategy. If every team can read it and continue exactly as before, it has avoided the choice. The test is to ask who will be unhappy with it, and whether the document says why that is acceptable.

14.2Find the constraint before you optimise

Eliyahu Goldratt's theory of constraints, told as a novel in The Goal (1984), makes a simple point that applies directly to engineering organisations: the throughput of a system is set by its single tightest constraint, so improving anything else does not make the system faster. A team whose changes wait three days for review gains nothing from faster builds. A company whose releases wait two weeks for a manual approval gains nothing from faster code review.

The practical question is "where does work wait?" Follow a few typical changes from idea to production and record where they sat idle: in a review queue, waiting for a test environment, waiting for one expert's answer, waiting for an approval, waiting for the next release train. Lean practitioners call this a value-stream map. The longest wait is the constraint, and very often it is a person or a queue, not a technology. Once you have found it, the order is: make the most of the constraint (no idle time on it, no unnecessary work for it), move work away from it, and only then add capacity.

14.3Scenario: the bottleneck is you

Six months into the media platform migration, you notice that the team's lead time has doubled. Following changes through the system shows where they wait: in your review queue. As the person who knows the transcoding code best, you are a required reviewer for every change to it, and you spend about half your time reviewing. The figure shows why this degrades so suddenly.

The numbers come from queueing theory (the same mathematics as in Backend system design 10.2). With six requests a day, thirty minutes each and half your time available, you are 75 % utilised, and a change waits on average three working hours before you even start reviewing it. At seven requests a day (88 %), the wait is seven hours, nearly a working day; at seven and a half (94 %), fifteen hours, two working days. For a single server the wait is \(\rho/(1-\rho)\) times the service time, where ρ is the utilisation: four times at 80 %, nine times at 90 %. A second reviewer sharing the same load, each spending half their time, cuts the wait from three hours to about ten minutes, because two servers rarely are busy at the same moment.

The remedies follow from the model. Add reviewers: teach two engineers the transcoding code, pair on reviews for a few weeks, and make review ownership a group rather than a person. Shorten the work: smaller pull requests and a written review checklist reduce the minutes per review. Remove unnecessary arrivals: decide which changes do not need you at all (tests, configuration with automated checks, documentation). And accept the lesson about utilisation: a person or system run near 100 % is not efficient; it is a queue.

Why it matters

Senior engineers become bottlenecks precisely because they are good, and nobody notices because each individual wait looks reasonable. Making yourself unnecessary for routine decisions is a sign of seniority, not a loss of it: it frees you for the larger scope that the next level expects, and the team becomes faster and more resilient. A useful related measure is the bus factor: how many people would have to be unavailable before a piece of knowledge is lost.

14.4Migrations, deprecations and the long game

Strategies are mostly executed through migrations: moving many teams from an old way to a new one. Larson argues in An Elegant Puzzle (2019) that migrations are the main mechanism by which growing companies pay down technical debt, and that the way to run them is to make the migration easy before asking anyone to migrate. In practice that means three phases. First, de-risk: prove the new path on one or two of the hardest real users, not the easiest. Second, enable: build tooling that does most of the migration automatically and documentation for the rest, so each team's cost is small. Third, finish: the last 10 to 20 % of users take the longest, and an unfinished migration is worse than none, because now there are two systems to run. Staff engineers are often the ones who insist on finishing, and on deleting the old system, which is the only moment the cost actually falls.

14.5Your own career strategy

The same kernel works for your career. The diagnosis: where are you on the ladder of Figure 1.1, what scope is your work today, and what is the gap to the level or role you want? The guiding policy: which direction (deeper IC, tech lead, architect, management, or a move to another kind of company) and what you will deliberately stop doing to make room. The actions: a project with next-level scope, a sponsor who knows about it, a habit of writing (design docs, internal posts) that makes your thinking visible beyond your team, and enough hands-on technical work to keep your judgement current. Revisit it once or twice a year, ideally just before the review cycle, when the evidence of the last months is fresh.

15Behavioural interviews for senior roles

For senior, staff and management roles, the behavioural interview often decides the level offered. It is not small talk: interviewers score your past behaviour against the same dimensions as the career ladder, and you can prepare for it as systematically as for a system-design interview.

15.1What the interviewer is scoring

Behavioural questions ("tell me about a time when…") rest on the idea that past behaviour predicts future behaviour. The interviewer usually has a list of competencies to cover (ownership, handling conflict, influencing without authority, dealing with ambiguity, learning from failure, developing others) and a description of what each looks like at each level. For a senior candidate they want to hear about a project of team scope handled independently; for staff, about cross-team problems you found and drove; for a manager, about people you developed and a team you shaped. Amazon makes this explicit with its sixteen Leadership Principles and a "bar raiser" interviewer from outside the hiring team; most large companies do something similar with less branding.

The standard answer structure is STAR: situation, task, action, result. The action part should be the longest and should be about you ("I proposed", "I set up", "I decided"), not "we". A short reflection at the end ("what I'd do differently") turns a good answer into a senior one, because it shows judgement about your own decisions.

15.4A STAR answer, sketched

Question: "Tell me about a disagreement between teams that you helped resolve." Sketch of a strong answer, based on Section 7.4:

Situation: "Two teams had argued for three weeks about the design of a new video pipeline: events versus a central orchestrator. It had started to get personal, and the project was blocked." Task: "I was the senior engineer on the platform side, so I was a party, but I was also the person best placed to unblock it." Action: "Instead of arguing the design, I met the other tech lead one-to-one to understand what they actually needed. It turned out to be independence: they had waited weeks for our changes before. My team's need was visibility of job state. I wrote both needs into a proposal as shared goals, laid out three options against them, including a hybrid, and named the director who would decide if we couldn't agree. I also wrote down the weakest point of my own recommendation." Result: "The review took one week instead of the three we had lost, the hybrid was chosen, and the other team's concern was recorded with a trigger to revisit. A year later the pipeline handles four teams' stages, and the two teams co-own the stage-registration API." Reflection: "What I'd do differently is have the one-to-one in week one, not week three."

Pitfall

The common mistakes are: answering with "we" throughout so the interviewer cannot tell what you did; spending three minutes on technical context and thirty seconds on the action; ending without a result; and stories in which the other side was simply wrong. Interviewers for senior roles are listening for how you treated people who disagreed with you.

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

What is the difference between a senior and a staff engineer?Basic

Scope and where the work comes from. A senior engineer independently delivers a project or owns a team's system, usually on goals the team already has. A staff engineer works across teams, finds problems nobody has been assigned, and drives decisions and alignment that several teams execute; the output is often a direction or an unblocked set of teams rather than code. Ambiguity and time horizon grow with the step: a quarter versus a year or more.

What are the staff engineer archetypes?Basic

Will Larson's four: the tech lead, who guides one team's approach and execution (the most common); the architect, who owns the direction of a critical area across teams; the solver, who goes deep on one hard problem at a time and moves on; and the right hand, who extends an executive's reach across the organisation. They differ by breadth of scope and whether the focus stays with one area.

How would you decide between the IC and the management track?Basic

By what I want to be accountable for. A manager is accountable for people and the team: hiring, growth, performance, health and delivery. A staff or principal engineer is accountable for technical outcomes across a scope. Both lead. The switch is reversible, especially early, and many good leaders move between the tracks; the mistake is moving to management only because it looks like the only way up.

How do performance reviews and calibration work?Basic

Usually once or twice a year: self-review, peer feedback, a manager's draft rating against the level guide, then calibration, where managers compare ratings across teams so that the same rating means the same thing, and finally the outcome. Because the people in calibration may not know the person, the decision rests on written evidence of scope and impact; that is why self-reviews should describe impact with numbers rather than list activities.

How would you prepare a promotion case?Intermediate

Map the last year against the next level's expectations rather than against effort, collect verifiable evidence for three to five impact stories (problem, my role, what changed, a number), find out how the committee decides and what failed cases lacked, have someone senior outside my team read it, and ask my manager early for a frank gap assessment. A "not yet" becomes a plan: a specific project that would supply the missing evidence.

How do you influence a team you have no authority over?Intermediate

Build trust before I need it, by helping them and being reliable; understand their goals and how they are measured; connect my request to those goals where possible; make the request explicit and written, with what, why and by when; pre-wire it one-to-one before any group meeting. If their priorities genuinely conflict with mine, I raise it jointly to the person who owns both priorities rather than pushing harder.

A launch is slipping. What do you do?Intermediate

Say so early with numbers, then work through the levers in order. Is the date really fixed? If not, move it once with a confidence level. If it is, cut or phase scope by user value, using flags and staged rollout. Add people only to separable work with enough runway, because of ramp-up and communication overhead (Brooks's law). Trade only quality that can be repaid without harming users, never security, privacy, data integrity or safety. Bring the business owner a clear choice and a recommendation.

How do you make decisions with incomplete information?Intermediate

Classify the decision first by reversibility and impact: small or reversible ones should be fast. For the important irreversible ones, write down the assumptions, run a pre-mortem, buy information when it is cheap relative to being wrong, name a decider, and set a revisit trigger. Where possible, design reversibility in (parallel run, flags). Judge the decision by its reasoning, not only its outcome.

How do you run an architecture disagreement to a decision?Intermediate

Move from positions to interests: talk one-to-one to find what each side actually needs, write those needs as shared goals, and get agreement on them before discussing solutions. Then a proposal with real options judged against the goals, async review with named reviewers, a short meeting only on open questions, a decider named in advance, the dissent recorded, and a revisit trigger. Disagree and commit afterwards.

What makes a good design review?Basic

It happens early, while options are open; it critiques the design, not the author; reviewers ask about risk (failure modes, rollback, operations, cost at scale, migration) and separate blocking issues from preferences; the most senior people speak late; a decider and the outcome are clear at the end, with actions owned.

How do you give difficult feedback?Basic

Soon, privately, about one thing, using situation, behaviour and impact rather than judgements about character. I state my intent, ask how they saw it before offering my view, and agree a next step. The aim is to care personally and challenge directly; the common failure among nice engineers is ruinous empathy, which delays information the person needs.

How do you help a struggling team member?Intermediate

Diagnose in order: are expectations clear and written; is it a skill gap; is it context such as on-call load, unclear projects or conflict; only then motivation or personal circumstances. The first three are common and fixable: write outcomes together, pair and slice work smaller, fix the environment. As an IC I share observations factually with the manager; support for personal issues and any formal performance process belong to the manager.

How do you delegate effectively?Intermediate

Hand over outcomes rather than tasks, at a level that matches the person's experience with that specific task (task-relevant maturity), and say the level out loud ("you decide, I'll advise"). Agree check-in points proportional to the risk, and allow recoverable mistakes. If I cannot accept a mistake, I delegate at a lower level instead of taking the work back halfway.

Which engineering metrics would you use, and which would you avoid?Intermediate

Team-level flow and stability metrics such as the DORA set, measured together, plus developer-experience surveys and the outcomes the work exists for. I would avoid individual activity metrics (lines of code, PR counts, story points per person) and single targets like coverage percentage, because of Goodhart's law. Every metric gets a counter-metric, is read as a trend and a distribution, and must change some decision.

You are incident commander. What do you do in the first fifteen minutes?Intermediate

Declare and take the role explicitly, assign an operations lead who is the only one changing production and a communications lead, open a dedicated channel, state what is known, and set a check-in rhythm. Then ask what changed recently and prefer a fast, reversible mitigation such as a rollback over understanding the cause. I do not debug myself, and I escalate early if I need people or authority.

What is Conway's law, and how does it affect your work?Basic

Systems end up mirroring the communication structure of the organisation that builds them. So dependency problems are often structure problems: if one team waits on another every sprint, the interaction mode is wrong, and the fix may be a self-service interface or a change of team boundaries (the inverse Conway manoeuvre) rather than more meetings.

When and how do you escalate?Intermediate

When a problem is beyond my authority: conflicting priorities, a dependency nobody at my level can fix, or a threat to safety, security, privacy or legal obligations. Early, with facts, options, a recommendation and a deadline; transparently, telling the other party or escalating jointly; one level at a time, to the lowest level that owns both sides.

How do you decide build versus buy?Intermediate

Core versus context first: build what customers choose us for, buy or adopt what they do not. Then whether a mature product fits most of the need at our scale, and whether we can leave it. Compare total cost over several years, including operations and maintenance for building and integration, renewal pricing and exit for buying. Keep a bought product behind our own interface and set a revisit trigger, for example a volume threshold or the first renewal.

How would you set up a platform team so that product teams want to use it?Advanced

Run it as a product: treat the product teams as users, study where they lose time, provide self-service and documentation, publish and meet SLOs, and offer a paved road that is easier than the alternatives rather than a mandate. Start with the thinnest viable platform. Measure adoption without mandates and the time product teams need for common tasks, not the number of features the platform has.

What is a technical strategy, and how would you write one?Advanced

A diagnosis of the real difficulty, a guiding policy that makes a choice, and coherent actions that carry it out (Rumelt). I would write it bottom-up from recent design documents, since the recurring problem across them is the diagnosis and the recurring good solution suggests the policy. A test is whether anyone has to change their plans because of it; if nobody does, it avoided the choice.

How do you know you have become a bottleneck, and what do you do?Advanced

Follow work through the system and see where it waits; if it waits for me (reviews, decisions, answers), I am the constraint. Queueing explains why it gets bad suddenly: near full utilisation, waiting grows as ρ/(1−ρ). Remedies: spread the knowledge and make ownership a group, shrink the work per request with smaller changes and checklists, remove requests that do not need me, and keep my own utilisation well below 100 %.

How do start-ups, big tech companies and automotive suppliers differ for a senior engineer?Intermediate

They are short of different things: a start-up of runway, so speed of learning dominates; a large tech company of alignment, so writing and influence dominate; an OEM or Tier 1 of certainty, because hardware, safety, homologation and long programmes make many mistakes irreversible, so predictability, traceability and contracts dominate. Good judgement and clear writing transfer everywhere; the sense of which decisions are one-way doors has to be recalibrated.

How do you handle a dispute with a supplier about who caused a defect?Advanced

Facts before positions: reproduce under controlled conditions and isolate with swap tests. Check what the specification and interface agreement say. Work the root cause jointly in a structured format such as 8D, contain the problem in the meantime, and keep the technical work separate from the commercial question of who pays, which goes through purchasing and the contract. Be firm on facts and polite in tone, because the relationship outlasts the defect.

Q.2Why and what if

Why is "years of experience" a poor measure of seniority?Why

Because ladders measure scope, ambiguity and independence, not time or depth alone. Someone with long, deep expertise who still needs others to decide what to work on operates below someone with fewer years who reliably turns vague goals into shipped, operated systems.

Why do companies calibrate ratings instead of trusting each manager?Why

Managers differ in strictness, so without calibration a rating would reflect the manager more than the work. Calibration aligns ratings across teams against the level guide; the side effect is that evidence must be written and understandable to people who do not know you.

Why does adding people to a late project often make it later?Why

New people need time to become productive and take time from the existing team to get there, and communication paths grow as n(n−1)/2. Only truly separable work with months of runway benefits; otherwise the overhead arrives before the help.

Why should the incident commander not debug?Why

Because coordination is a full-time job during an incident: deciding, keeping changes serialised, communicating and escalating. A commander reading logs leaves the incident without anyone doing that, which is the usual reason outages last longer than the technical fix.

Why do metrics used as targets stop working?Why

People optimise the number rather than what it was meant to indicate (Goodhart's and Campbell's laws): coverage without assertions, smaller PRs to raise counts, closed rather than fixed bugs. Metrics keep their meaning best when used for learning, at team level, paired with counter-metrics.

Why does review waiting time explode rather than grow gradually as load rises?Why

Requests arrive randomly, so they sometimes bunch up; with little spare capacity, a bunch takes long to clear. For a single server the mean wait is ρ/(1−ρ) times the service time, which is 3 at 75 % but 9 at 90 % and unbounded at 100 %. A second server absorbs bunches and cuts the wait sharply.

What if your manager decides against your recommendation on something you think is merely suboptimal?What if

State the disagreement once, clearly and with reasons, ask for it to be recorded, then commit fully to executing the decision. If an agreed revisit trigger fires, raise it again with data. Continuing to argue, or executing half-heartedly, damages both the project and my credibility.

What if the decision is not just suboptimal but unsafe or illegal?What if

Then disagree-and-commit does not apply. I escalate, in writing, with the specific risk and the obligation it violates, to the level that owns it, and I do not execute the unsafe part. In safety-related automotive work there are formal routes for this, such as the safety manager and the safety case.

What if a spike would take as long as reversing the decision would?What if

Then the spike rarely pays: its delay is certain, while reversal is paid only if the decision turns out wrong. Decide now, design for cheap reversal, and set a trigger to revisit. The value-of-information model shows the spike paying only when reversal cost is well above the spike's cost divided by the probability of being wrong.

What if the platform team mandates its platform and product teams build workarounds?What if

The workarounds are data: the platform does not solve their problems well enough, or costs more than it saves. Remove the mandate where possible, find out what the workarounds do, and make the paved road easier than them. Measure adoption that happens without force.

What if you are both the best debugger and the only available senior during an outage?What if

Hand the commander role to someone else, even someone less experienced, and give them the checklist (roles, rhythm, decisions out loud). Coordination by a less senior commander is better than no coordination with the best debugger. If nobody else is available, time-box my own debugging strictly and keep the communication rhythm.

What if your promotion case keeps being rejected for "not enough scope"?What if

Treat it as a scope problem, not a quality problem. Ask what next-level scope looks like in concrete terms at this company, and work with the manager to get a project of that shape: cross-team, ambiguous, with decisions that are mine. Sometimes the scope does not exist in the current team, and the answer is to change team or company.
