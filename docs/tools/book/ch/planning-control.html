Localization, planning and vehicle control

The part of a driving stack that turns a picture of the world into motion: knowing where the car is to a few centimetres, describing the road and the other road users, predicting what they will do, choosing a route, a manoeuvre and an exact trajectory, and finally commanding steering, drive and brakes. One running scenario goes through every layer: an automated lane change at 100 km/h past a slow car, with a faster car approaching in the target lane. Every number quoted comes from a small experiment in this chapter's tools folder.

1The big picture: from "where am I" to "turn the wheel by 3 degrees"

Perception tells the car what is around it right now. That is not yet a decision. Between "there is a slow car 40 m ahead" and "the steering wheel turns by 3 degrees" sit several layers, each answering a different question on a different time scale. This chapter walks through them in the order the data flows.

1.1The driving stack is a chain of questions with different clocks

A useful way to understand a driving stack is to write down the questions it has to answer, in order. Where exactly am I, on which lane, facing which way? What is around me, and what does the road look like? What will the other road users do in the next few seconds? Which roads and lanes lead to the destination? Which manoeuvre is right now: stay, overtake, merge, stop? Which exact trajectory realises that manoeuvre without hitting anything? And finally, which steering angle and which acceleration should be requested in the next ten milliseconds so that the car follows that trajectory? Each question becomes a layer of software (Figure 1.1), and the remarkable thing is how different these layers are in character.

They differ first in their clocks. The controller runs at 50 to 100 Hz because the car moves almost 30 cm per 10 ms at motorway speed and a steering correction that comes 100 ms late is a correction for a situation that no longer exists. The motion planner runs at 10 to 20 Hz and looks five to eight seconds ahead, because that is how long a lane change or a braking manoeuvre takes. The behaviour layer thinks in manoeuvres that last several seconds and may change its mind once per second. The route planner looks kilometres ahead and runs only when something changes. Localization is the exception: it is corrected by slow sensors (GNSS at 1 to 10 Hz, a camera at 20 to 30 Hz), but propagated with the IMU so that its output is available at the controller's rate.

They also differ in what can go wrong. A localization error of 0.5 m sideways puts the car on the lane marking even if everything downstream is perfect. A prediction error turns a correct plan into a dangerous one. A planner can be correct about the world and still produce a trajectory that the car cannot physically follow, which the controller then tracks badly. Thinking in layers is therefore also how engineers localise faults: when the car wobbled in a lane change, the first question is which layer's output was already wrong.

Why it matters

Most system-level questions in this area are interface questions: in which frame is the pose given, how old is the object list, does the trajectory carry time stamps or only geometry, is the steering request an angle or a torque. A team that can draw Figure 1.1 for its own product with the real rates and latencies has usually already found the integration problems that other teams discover in the vehicle.

1.2The running scenario: one lane change at 100 km/h

The scenario that runs through the chapter is deliberately ordinary. The ego car drives at 100 km/h (27.8 m/s) in the right lane of a motorway. Forty metres ahead, a slower car drives at 22 m/s. In the left lane, a faster car at 32 m/s approaches from behind. The system is allowed to change lanes on its own, as a Level 2 lane-change assist or a Level 3 motorway pilot would.

Figure 1.2 shows what each layer does during the ten seconds around the manoeuvre. Localization keeps the car's lateral position known to a few centimetres, which is only possible because the camera sees the lane markings and the map says where those markings are (Chapter 2). The world model lists the slow car as "lead vehicle in ego lane" and the fast car as "approaching in left lane", each with a predicted trajectory (Chapters 4 and 5). The behaviour layer notices that the lead is slower than the set speed, decides to overtake, switches on the indicator and waits until the gap in the left lane is acceptable (Chapter 6). From then on, the motion planner produces a new six-second trajectory every 100 ms, roughly a hundred trajectories for one manoeuvre, each one a slightly corrected continuation of the last (Chapter 7). The controller turns each trajectory into steering and acceleration requests at 100 Hz, about a thousand commands in total (Chapter 10). The car moves 3.5 m sideways in five seconds with a peak lateral acceleration of 0.81 m/s², which passengers perceive as smooth. The steering wheel turns by a few degrees; Section 9.1 computes exactly how many.

Two details of this timeline are worth noticing early. First, re-planning every 100 ms is not a sign of indecision; it is how a planner absorbs new measurements and its own tracking errors. A plan computed once and followed blindly would accumulate every small error. Second, the layers overlap in time: the planner is already planning the end of the lane change while the controller executes its beginning, and the behaviour layer keeps checking whether aborting would still be possible.

1.3Who is responsible: assistance, automation and the operational design domain

The same software layers exist in a lane-keeping assistant and in a robotaxi, but who answers for the result differs, and that changes the engineering. SAE J3016 describes this with levels. At Level 1 and 2 the system steers and/or controls speed, but the driver monitors the road at all times and is responsible; the system may hand back control instantly. At Level 3 the system drives and monitors within its operational design domain (ODD), for example "motorway, up to 130 km/h, daylight, no road works", and the driver must take over only when asked, with a lead time of several seconds. At Level 4 the system must reach a safe state by itself if anything goes wrong.

For the planning stack, the line between Level 2 and Level 3 is the important one. At Level 2 a missed object is the driver's to catch; at Level 3 the system itself must detect its limits, request a takeover, and if the driver does not respond, perform a minimal risk manoeuvre (Section 11.3). This is why Level 3 localization needs an integrity estimate (Section 2.5), why its planner needs a safety checker (Section 11.1), and why the ODD must be monitored at runtime rather than written only in a manual. The regulations that fix these duties for cars sold in the UN-ECE markets (UN R79 for steering assistance, R171 for Level 2 driver control assistance, R157 for Level 3 lane keeping) are summarised in Standards and regulations 7.4.

2Localization: where exactly am I?

A lane is 3.5 m wide and a car is 1.9 m wide, which leaves 0.8 m on each side. Satellite navigation alone is accurate to a few metres. Closing that gap, reliably and with an honest estimate of the remaining error, is the job of localization.

2.1Lane-level accuracy, and what "accurate" has to mean

A navigation system is happy to know which road the car is on; an error of 5 m along the road is invisible to the driver. An automated driving function needs to know which lane the car is in and where inside that lane, because the trajectory it plans is expressed relative to the lanes in the map. The required accuracy follows from geometry: with 0.8 m of free space on each side of the car, a lateral error of a few decimetres already eats most of the margin. Reid and colleagues (2019) derived localization requirements for US road geometry and arrived at lateral bounds of a few decimetres, tighter on local streets than on freeways, which must hold not on average but with a very high probability. Along the road the requirement is looser, about a metre, because the planner keeps distances of tens of metres to other cars and to stop lines.

That last point, "with very high probability", is the part that makes localization hard. An average error of 10 cm is useless if once per hour the error is 2 m and the system does not know it. Localization therefore delivers three things, not one: the pose itself (position, heading, and for many uses roll and pitch), a covariance that says how certain the pose is, and an integrity statement that says whether that covariance can be trusted. Section 2.5 returns to the third.

Numbers

Standalone single-frequency GNSS: a few metres. Dual-frequency with SBAS or PPP: decimetres after convergence. RTK with a nearby base station: 2–5 cm when it has a fix. MEMS IMU dead reckoning: metres after tens of seconds (see Automotive sensors 6.6). Lane-marking matching: about 10 cm laterally, nothing along the road. Lidar scan matching against a prior map: centimetres in both directions where the map is up to date.

2.2Fusing GNSS, IMU and odometry

No single sensor gives a pose that is accurate, smooth, fast and always available. GNSS is absolute but slow, noisy and disappears in tunnels and under bridges. The IMU is fast and smooth but integrates its own errors: a gyro bias of a fraction of a degree per second becomes a heading error of degrees within a minute, and a heading error becomes a growing position error. Wheel odometry measures distance travelled and, from the wheel speed difference or the yaw-rate sensor, turning, but it is fooled by tyre radius changes and wheel slip. The physics of each sensor is in Automotive sensors 6.2–6.5; here the question is how they are combined (Figure 2.1).

The standard structure is a filter that predicts with the fast relative sensors and corrects with the slow absolute ones. Every IMU sample (100 to 1000 Hz) moves the estimated pose forward: integrate the yaw rate into heading, integrate speed along that heading into position. Every GNSS fix, every lane-marking observation, every matched landmark pulls the estimate back towards the measurement, weighted by how much the filter trusts the measurement relative to its own prediction. In production systems this is almost always an error-state extended Kalman filter: the nominal pose is integrated with the full nonlinear equations, and the filter estimates only the small error of that pose together with the sensor errors (gyro and accelerometer biases, wheel scale factor, GNSS antenna lever arm). Estimating the biases is what lets the car drive through a tunnel with a gyro that is better calibrated than it was from the factory. How the EKF update itself works is shown with a worked radar example in Calibration and fusion 8.4.

There are two ways to feed GNSS into the filter. Loose coupling uses the receiver's own position solution as a measurement; it is simple, but when fewer than four satellites are visible the receiver produces nothing and the filter gets nothing. Tight coupling feeds the raw pseudoranges and Doppler measurements of each satellite into the filter, so that even two or three satellites in an urban canyon still constrain the pose, and a single satellite with a suspicious residual can be excluded. Tight coupling is more work and needs access to raw measurements, which not every receiver exposes, but it is what high-end systems use.

Pitfall

A Kalman filter assumes that measurement errors are independent from one sample to the next. GNSS errors are not: multipath and atmospheric errors drift slowly over tens of seconds. In this chapter's experiment, a filter that treats 10 Hz GNSS with 1 m error as independent believes its lateral error is 0.32 m (1σ); with realistically correlated GNSS errors the actual RMS error is 0.96 m and 44% of the samples lie outside the filter's own 2σ bound. The filter is not wrong about the sensor's noise level, it is wrong about how much ten samples in a row tell it. Common fixes are to model the correlated error as an extra state, to down-weight GNSS, or to decimate it to about 1 Hz.

2.3Map matching: lanes and landmarks fix what GNSS cannot

The decisive trick for lane-level localization is to measure the car's position relative to things whose position is known from the map. The cheapest such things are lane markings. The front camera already detects them for lane keeping, and its measurement, "the left marking is 1.62 m to my left and the lane runs 0.4° to my right", is very precise. If the map says where that marking is, the measurement becomes a correction of the car's lateral position and heading in the map.

The important limitation is what that measurement does not constrain. A straight lane marking looks identical everywhere along the road, so it says nothing about the longitudinal position. Figure 2.2 shows this with the numbers from the experiment. With GNSS and odometry alone, the error ellipse is roughly round, about 0.8 m along and 1 m across the road. Adding lane-marking observations shrinks the lateral error to 0.05 m and leaves the longitudinal error at 0.76 m: the ellipse becomes a thin line along the lane. To fix the longitudinal position, the car needs features that are distinct along the road: signs, poles, the start of a lane, a bridge, curvature. In the experiment a sign every 150 m only reduces the longitudinal error from 0.76 to 0.67 m, because between signs the filter keeps pulling towards the drifting GNSS it believes. Dense features (poles and kerbs every few metres, seen by lidar or radar) do much better, which is why lidar localization against a prior map reaches centimetres in both directions.

Map matching also exists at a coarser level. A navigation system snaps a noisy GNSS track onto the road network, and the standard way to do it is a hidden Markov model (Newson and Krumm, 2009): the hidden state is "which road segment am I on", the observation probability falls with the distance from the GNSS point to the segment, and the transition probability favours moves that are plausible along the road graph. The Viterbi algorithm then picks the most likely sequence of segments. The same idea, applied to lanes instead of roads, answers "which lane am I in" when the markings are worn or hidden by traffic: lane changes are transitions, and a lane that does not continue at a split simply has no successor.

In practice

The lane-marking measurement only helps if the map and the camera agree on what a marking is. Typical integration bugs: the map stores the centre of a 15 cm wide marking and the camera reports its inner edge (a constant 7.5 cm lateral bias); the map has been updated after a re-paint and the camera sees the old, partly removed lines; at a split the camera follows the marking into the exit while the map continues straight.

2.4SLAM and odometry as localization sources

Visual odometry, lidar odometry and SLAM are usually presented as their own field, and their algorithms are covered in Classic computer vision 12.5. From the localization point of view they are simply additional sensors with particular error characteristics. Visual and lidar odometry measure the car's motion between frames, very precisely over short distances, but their error grows with distance travelled (a 1% drift is a metre per 100 m). They are relative sensors, like the wheels, and enter the filter in the prediction or as relative-pose measurements.

A prior map turns them into absolute sensors. If the car carries a lidar point cloud or a visual feature map of the road recorded earlier, matching the current scan against that map (with ICP or NDT for lidar, feature matching for cameras) gives a pose in the map frame, and the drift disappears. This is what many robotaxi stacks rely on. Full SLAM, building the map while localizing in it, matters mostly where no map exists: parking garages, private sites, the first drive through a new area, or memory parking, where the car records the route to a home parking space once and then repeats it.

Interesting detail

Localization quality depends on the scenery. A long tunnel with smooth walls defeats lidar matching for the same reason that a straight marking defeats the camera: the scan looks the same everywhere along the tunnel. Systems handle this by monitoring the shape of the matching cost (the Hessian of the scan-matching cost is nearly singular along the tunnel axis) and inflating the covariance in that direction instead of reporting a confident but arbitrary position.

2.5Integrity: knowing when you are wrong

Aviation, where satellite navigation guides aircraft to the runway, has a precise vocabulary for this problem, and automotive localization has borrowed it. The alert limit is the largest error the function can tolerate, for example 0.5 m laterally. The protection level is a bound that the system computes in real time, such that the true error exceeds it only with a tiny integrity risk, for example 10−7 per hour. The time to alert is how fast the system must report that the protection level has exceeded the alert limit. As long as the protection level is below the alert limit, the function may use the pose; when it rises above, the function must degrade, for example from hands-off to hands-on, or start a takeover request.

Computing a protection level is only meaningful if the error model is honest, which brings back the pitfall from Section 2.2: a covariance that is too small yields a protection level that is too small. Integrity work is therefore largely about the tails: detecting and excluding faulty satellites (receiver autonomous integrity monitoring), checking that independent sources agree (GNSS, map matching and odometry should tell the same story), and validating the error model on many hours of recorded data against a reference system.

Talk track

"Localization fuses fast relative sensors, IMU and wheels, with slow absolute ones, GNSS and map matching, in an error-state Kalman filter. Lane markings fix the lateral position to centimetres but say nothing along the road; landmarks or lidar matching fix that. The output is a pose plus a covariance plus an integrity flag, and the covariance must be honest, because a filter that assumes independent GNSS errors is confidently wrong."

3Maps: the road as data

A map gives the car knowledge it cannot see: the lane beyond the crest, the speed limit that applies after the next junction, which lane leads to the exit. Maps range from the navigation map in every car to centimetre-accurate lane models, and the trend of the last few years is to build part of the map online from the car's own sensors.

3.1From navigation maps to HD maps

Three kinds of map are worth separating. A standard-definition (SD) map is the navigation map: roads as polylines with metre-level accuracy, attributes such as speed limits, number of lanes and turn restrictions, and the road network as a graph for routing. An ADAS map adds what simple assistance functions need, mainly curvature and slope along the road, so that adaptive cruise control can slow down before a bend that the camera cannot see yet. The map is delivered to the assistance ECUs as an electronic horizon: a description of the most probable path ahead and its attributes over the next few kilometres, sent over the vehicle network in a standard format (ADASIS v2 for SD content over CAN, ADASIS v3 for richer, lane-level content over Ethernet).

An HD map (also called a lane-level or high-definition map) describes every lane: its centre line and boundaries with an absolute accuracy of the order of 10–20 cm, the type of each marking, which lanes connect to which across a junction, stop lines, traffic lights and which lanes they control, and often localization features such as poles and signs. HD maps are made by survey vehicles with lidar and precise GNSS, then checked and annotated, and they are expensive to keep current. That cost, more than any technical limit, is why a large part of the industry now prefers systems that work with SD maps plus online perception, using HD maps only where they are available.

Why it matters

The map is a sensor with infinite range and no weather dependence, but with a time delay measured in weeks. Everything it knows was true when it was surveyed. A planner that trusts the map more than its own eyes will drive into the construction site that appeared yesterday; one that ignores the map loses the information about the lane drop 300 m ahead that is hidden behind a truck.

3.2What is inside a lane-level map

The common way to model lanes is as small pieces with explicit relations, shown in Figure 3.1. Lanelet2 (Poggenhans et al., 2018), widely used in research and in Autoware, calls them lanelets: each is a short stretch of one lane defined by its left and right boundary, and each boundary carries its type, which decides whether crossing it is allowed. ASAM OpenDRIVE, the format used by most simulators, describes the road instead by a reference line with lanes defined as offsets from it; NDS.Live is the automotive industry's format for delivering map data to vehicles in tiles. The formats differ, but they all encode the same three things: geometry (where the lane is), topology (which lane follows which, which lanes are neighbours) and regulation (speed limits, right of way, which signal applies to which lane).

The topology is what makes the map useful for planning. A lane-level route is a path in this graph, and it immediately tells the behaviour layer where the car must change lanes: in Figure 3.1, a car in lane A1 that wants to take the exit must reach B3 before the solid line begins, which means two lane changes within a few hundred metres. Regulatory elements attached to lanelets answer questions perception struggles with: which of three traffic lights governs the left-turn lane, which lane has right of way at a merge.

Interesting detail

Lane geometry in maps is usually stored as polylines or as clothoids (curves whose curvature changes linearly with length), because roads are built from clothoids: a driver turning the wheel at a constant rate traces one. Planners like clothoid-based maps because curvature, which maps directly to steering, is then continuous.

3.3Maps that age, and maps built online

Because maps age, every map-based system needs a way to notice that the map is wrong. The simplest check compares what the camera sees with what the map predicts: if the marking types, the number of lanes or the lane widths disagree over a stretch of road, the map is marked as unreliable there and the function falls back to perception-only behaviour or hands back control. Fleets can report such disagreements to the map provider, which is how many maps are kept current.

Online mapping goes further and builds the local lane model from the car's own sensors every frame. Networks such as HDMapNet and MapTR predict lane boundaries, centre lines and pedestrian crossings in the bird's-eye view as vectors, directly usable by the planner; how they work is in Bird's-eye view perception 8.3. Their weakness is range and topology: they see 50 to 100 m and can confuse which lane continues where at a complex junction. A common architecture therefore fuses both: an SD map provides the route and the coarse topology, online perception provides the precise local geometry, and an HD map, where available, adds the details neither can provide.

Talk track

"An SD map tells you the road, an HD map tells you the lanes, their connections and the rules, to about 10–20 cm. HD maps are precise but expensive to keep current, so modern stacks combine an SD map for routing with online vectorized maps from perception, and check any prior map against what the sensors see before trusting it."

4World model and scene understanding

Perception outputs lists: boxes, lane lines, a free-space grid, a traffic light state. A planner needs a scene: which car is the one I follow, which lane is that pedestrian heading for, which light applies to me. The world model is where lists become a scene.

4.1What the planner needs to know

The world model is the planner's single, consistent picture of the present. It contains the ego state from localization; the tracked objects with position, velocity, size, class and their uncertainties; the static environment (lanes from the map or from online mapping, road edges, free space and occupancy for things that are not objects); traffic control (light states, signs, speed limits); and the predicted futures of the other road users. Every item carries a time stamp and a frame. The word "consistent" carries much of the weight: objects measured 80 ms ago by the camera and 30 ms ago by the radar must be brought to the same time, and the lanes from the map must be expressed in the same frame as the objects from perception, which is only possible because localization ties the two together. The transform tree that makes this possible is described in Calibration and fusion 2.2.

The world model must also represent what is not known. A car that has just disappeared behind a truck has not ceased to exist; a good tracker keeps it, with growing uncertainty, for a second or two. An area hidden behind a parked van may contain a child; the planner should treat occluded areas at a pedestrian crossing as possibly occupied and reduce speed accordingly. Free, occupied and unknown are three different states, and planners that collapse "unknown" into "free" are the ones that look confident in the demo and dangerous in the city (see Bird's-eye view perception 9.2 on free space).

Pitfall

Latency is part of the world model. If the object list is 120 ms old when the planner reads it, a car that brakes hard has moved about 3 m relative to where the list says it is at motorway speeds. The planner must either predict objects forward to its own planning time or know their age and carry the error as uncertainty. Systems that silently mix ages across sensors produce objects that jitter by a metre between frames.

4.2The Frenet frame: describing motion relative to the lane

On a road, almost everything interesting is relative to the lane: how far along it a car is, how far from its centre. The Frenet frame (Figure 4.1) expresses positions in exactly those terms. Choose a reference line, usually the centre line of the ego lane. Any point is then described by s, the arc length along the reference line to the closest point, and d, the signed lateral distance from it. In this frame a curved road becomes straight, lane keeping becomes "keep d near zero", a lane change becomes "move d from 0 to 3.5 m", and following a car becomes a one-dimensional problem in s.

The convenience has a price. The transformation needs the closest point on the reference line, which is ambiguous where the line curves tightly (a point near the centre of a tight bend is close to many points on it), and a trajectory planned as a straight line in (s, d) has a curvature in the map that depends on the curvature of the reference line. On motorways, where curvature is small, the Frenet frame works very well. In parking lots or tight urban turns, planners work in Cartesian coordinates instead (Section 8.2).

4.3Scene understanding: relations, rules and context

The step from objects to scene is mostly about relations. Which lane is each object in, and is it leaving it? Which object is the lead vehicle, the one the longitudinal controller follows? Which vehicles bound the gap in the target lane? Does the pedestrian on the kerb face the crossing? Which traffic light belongs to the ego lane, and does the oncoming car have priority at the unprotected left turn? None of these is a perception output in itself, yet the planner cannot act without them.

Classical stacks answer such questions with explicit code: assign each object to the lanelet it overlaps most, select as lead the nearest object in the ego lane's corridor, associate lights with lanes through the map's regulatory elements. These rules are transparent and testable, but every new situation adds another rule, and the interactions between rules are where surprises come from. Learned approaches encode the scene as a graph of lanes and agents (VectorNet, Section 5.2) and let a network learn the relations implicitly. In practice both coexist: a learned model proposes, a rule-based layer interprets what it must not get wrong, such as the colour of the light that applies to the ego lane.

In practice

"Cut-in" detection is the classic scene-understanding problem on motorways. A car that drifts towards the ego lane may be about to cut in, or may be correcting within its own lane. ACC systems that react late brake hard; systems that react early brake for nothing. The useful signals are lateral velocity towards the lane boundary, the indicator (if a camera classifies it), the gap ahead of the other car, and its speed relative to its own lane. Section 5.2 shows why lateral velocity alone is not enough.

5Prediction: what will the others do?

A trajectory is safe or unsafe only with respect to where the others will be when the ego car gets there. Prediction turns the present scene into a set of possible futures for every relevant road user, each with a probability.

5.1Why the planner needs the future

A lane change takes five seconds. During that time the fast car in the left lane moves 160 m. Whether the manoeuvre is safe depends on where that car will be at second three, not where it is now, and on whether it will brake, accelerate or change lanes itself. The planner therefore needs predicted trajectories for the next 3 to 8 seconds, at the same time resolution as its own plan (typically 0.1 s), with an uncertainty that grows over time.

Because the future is genuinely uncertain, a single predicted trajectory per object is not enough. A car approaching a motorway exit may stay or take the exit; a pedestrian at a kerb may cross or wait. Good predictors output several modes, each a trajectory with a probability and a spread (Figure 5.1, left). The planner can then plan against all modes above a threshold, or weight them by probability in its cost, and stay ready to react when one mode becomes certain.

5.2Physics, manoeuvres and learning

The simplest predictors extrapolate physics. A constant-velocity (CV) model assumes the object keeps its speed and direction; a constant turn rate and velocity (CTRV) model also keeps its yaw rate. They are excellent for one second and poor beyond, because drivers do not drive with constant yaw rate, they drive along lanes. Figure 5.1 (right) shows the numbers from the experiment for a car that has started a 3.5 m lane change one second earlier. Assuming it continues straight along its lane underestimates its lateral position by 0.91 m after one second and 3.3 m after four. Extrapolating along its current heading is better early (0.37 m after 1 s) but still 1.5 m off at 3 s, because a lane change ends: the car straightens out in the new lane, which no physics model knows.

Manoeuvre-based predictors add that knowledge. They first classify the intention (keep lane, change left, change right, turn, stop) from cues such as lateral velocity, distance to the marking, indicator and the lane topology, then generate a trajectory for each manoeuvre that follows the map: a lane change ends in the centre of the target lane, a turn follows the turning lane. With the map as a prior, predictions stay on the road for many seconds.

Learned predictors are now the standard in research and in the larger production stacks. They take the past few seconds of every agent and the map around them, encode both (VectorNet, Gao et al., 2020, represents lanes and agent histories as polylines and connects them in a graph; MTR and Wayformer use transformers over the same kind of tokens), and output K trajectories with probabilities, typically K = 6. They learn interaction and context that hand-written models miss: cars slow down before a merge, pedestrians speed up when the light starts flashing. They also inherit the usual weaknesses of learned models: rare situations are poorly predicted, and a confident prediction can be confidently wrong.

Numbers

Public benchmarks fix the horizons: Argoverse 1 predicts 3 s from 2 s of history, Argoverse 2 predicts 6 s from 5 s, the Waymo Open Motion Dataset 8 s from 1 s. Metrics are computed over the best of K predictions: minADEK (average displacement error of the best mode), minFDEK (error at the final time) and miss rate (fraction of cases in which no mode ends within about 2 m of the truth).

5.3Interaction: prediction and planning depend on each other

Predicting each agent independently ignores that agents react to each other, including to the ego car. The fast car in the left lane will slow down a little if the ego car signals and starts moving, as most drivers do. A planner that predicts the others as if the ego car did not exist faces a wall of traffic in dense merges and never finds a gap; this is called the freezing robot problem. Human drivers merge by nudging: they start moving, watch the reaction and either continue or abort.

There are three ways to model this. Joint prediction outputs consistent scenes for all agents together rather than independent trajectories per agent, so that two cars never both occupy the same gap in one predicted future. Conditional prediction asks "what will the others do if I do this", and evaluates each candidate ego plan against predictions conditioned on it. Game-theoretic and learned interactive planners go furthest and treat the problem as a game between agents. Production systems mostly use conditional reasoning in simple form: the behaviour layer's "prepare" phase (Section 6.3) signals intent and checks whether the gap opens, which is a nudge implemented as a state machine.

Talk track

"Prediction gives the planner multiple possible futures per agent with probabilities, over three to eight seconds. Physics models are fine for a second, manoeuvre models that follow the map work for several, and learned models such as VectorNet or MTR capture context and interaction. The hard part is that others react to us: predicting without that leads to a frozen planner in dense traffic."

6Route, behaviour and motion planning

Planning is split into layers because the questions differ in scale. Which roads lead to Munich is answered over kilometres and minutes; whether to overtake the truck, over hundreds of metres and seconds; exactly how to steer around the cone, over metres and tenths of a second.

6.1Three layers, three time scales

The classical split has three layers (Figure 6.1). The route planner finds a sequence of roads and lanes from the current position to the destination on the map's graph. The behaviour planner chooses the next manoeuvre given the route, the traffic rules and the current scene: keep lane, follow the lead, change left into this gap, yield at this junction, stop at this line. The motion planner turns the chosen manoeuvre into a trajectory, a time-stamped sequence of positions, headings, speeds and accelerations, that is collision-free, comfortable and drivable.

The split is not a law of nature. It exists because each layer's problem is easier when the layer above has fixed some choices. The motion planner does not need to consider every possible lane change, only the one the behaviour layer chose; the behaviour layer does not need to know the route to Munich, only that the exit comes in 2 km. The cost is that a choice fixed above may be bad below: the behaviour layer may choose a gap that the motion planner cannot reach comfortably. Good stacks therefore let information flow back up (the motion planner reports "infeasible" and the behaviour layer picks another gap), and some planners merge behaviour and motion planning by generating trajectories for several manoeuvres and letting a single cost function choose.

6.2Route planning on a road graph

Route planning is the shortest path problem on a graph whose nodes are junctions (or lanelets) and whose edges are road segments weighted by travel time, distance, tolls or energy. Dijkstra's algorithm and A* solve it; both are explained, with an interactive comparison, in Data structures and algorithms 5.4. On a continental road network with tens of millions of nodes, plain Dijkstra is too slow for interactive use, so navigation systems pre-process the graph. Contraction hierarchies (Geisberger et al., 2008) rank nodes by importance and add shortcut edges so that a query only climbs to more important roads from both ends and meets in the middle; queries then take milliseconds instead of seconds.

For automated driving, the route is refined to lane level. The lane graph of Figure 3.1 is searched with lane-change edges carrying a penalty, and the result says not only "take the exit" but "be in the right lane 500 m before the exit". That requirement becomes an input to the behaviour planner, which then has to fit the necessary lane changes into the traffic.

6.3Behaviour planning: deciding the manoeuvre

The behaviour layer is where traffic rules, route needs and the current scene meet. Most production systems implement it as a finite state machine or a hierarchy of them, because states and transitions can be reviewed, tested and argued about in a safety case. Figure 6.2 shows the lane change of the running scenario as such a machine. In Keep lane the car follows its lane and its lead. A reason to change, here the slow lead, the route or a driver request, moves it to Prepare: the indicator goes on, the system looks for a gap that stays acceptable for a minimum time, and it may nudge slightly towards the line to signal intent. When the gap is accepted the machine moves to Execute and the motion planner generates the lane-change trajectory. After the centre of the car crosses the line, the machine goes to Complete and centres the car in the new lane.

The interesting design work is in the guards on the transitions. "Gap acceptable" must combine the gap to the car ahead in the target lane, the gap to the car behind, their predicted speeds, and how hard they would have to brake if the ego car moved in (Section 7.2 makes this concrete). The abort paths need equal care. If the rear car accelerates during Execute, the system must decide whether to abort; returning to the own lane is safe only while the car has not yet crossed the line, and the slow lead must still be far enough ahead for it.

State machines become hard to maintain as situations multiply. Alternatives include behaviour trees (borrowed from game AI, easier to compose), cost-based selection where every manoeuvre is planned and scored, and learned policies. The more the behaviour layer is learned, the more the safety argument shifts to the checker that validates its output (Section 11.1).

Pitfall

State machines with thresholds oscillate. If "lead too slow" switches on at a speed difference of 3 m/s and off at 3 m/s, the system starts and cancels lane changes as the difference fluctuates around the threshold. Every guard needs hysteresis (on at 3, off at 1.5) and every state a minimum dwell time. The same applies to gap acceptance: a gap that was accepted should be abandoned only if it becomes clearly unacceptable, not marginally.

6.4Motion planning: from manoeuvre to trajectory

The motion planner's output is a trajectory: for every time step over the horizon (typically 0.1 s steps over 5 to 8 s), the planned position, heading, curvature, speed and acceleration. The time stamps matter. A path without timing ("drive along this curve") cannot be checked against moving obstacles, because whether the curve is safe depends on when the car is where. The trajectory also carries the derivatives the controller needs for feedforward: curvature for the steering, acceleration for the drive and brake.

The planner must satisfy three kinds of constraint. Safety: no overlap with any predicted obstacle at any time, with a margin, and staying inside the drivable area. Feasibility: the vehicle must be able to follow it: curvature within the steering limit, lateral and longitudinal acceleration within the tyre limits, steering rate within the actuator's limit. Comfort and legality: lateral acceleration and jerk well below the physical limits, speed within the limit, no unnecessary lane-marking crossings. Among all trajectories that satisfy the constraints, it chooses the one with the lowest cost, where the cost weighs progress, comfort, distance to obstacles and deviation from the lane centre. Chapters 7 and 8 describe the main ways to do this.

Talk track

"Planning has three layers. Routing is shortest path on the road or lane graph, with contraction hierarchies for speed. Behaviour picks the manoeuvre, usually with a state machine with hysteresis and explicit abort paths. Motion planning produces a time-stamped trajectory that is collision-free against predictions, drivable for the vehicle and comfortable, chosen by minimising a cost, and it re-plans every 100 ms."

7Trajectory generation and collision avoidance

A trajectory generator has to find, ten times per second, a curve through space and time that avoids every predicted obstacle, respects what the car can physically do, and feels natural to the passengers. The two main families are sampling, which generates many candidates and keeps the best, and optimisation, which bends one candidate until it is optimal.

7.1Constraints and costs

Every trajectory generator is defined by what it must satisfy and what it prefers. The hard constraints were listed in Section 6.4: collision-free, inside the drivable area, within the vehicle's limits. The preferences become a cost function, typically a weighted sum: squared lateral jerk and longitudinal jerk integrated over the trajectory (comfort), the time or distance needed for the manoeuvre (progress), the deviation from the desired speed, the deviation from the lane centre at the end, and a term that grows as the trajectory gets closer to obstacles (a soft safety margin on top of the hard one).

The weights are where the driving style lives. A planner tuned for comfort produces long, gentle lane changes; one tuned for progress produces short ones. There is no single correct setting, and much of a planning team's tuning time goes into weights that behave well across thousands of recorded scenarios, not just the one under discussion. One structural rule helps: hard limits should be constraints, not large cost terms, because a cost can always be outweighed by another cost.

Numbers

Comfortable everyday driving stays below about 2 m/s² lateral and 2 m/s² longitudinal acceleration, with jerk of a few m/s³. UN R79 limits the lateral acceleration a lane-keeping system may command to 3 m/s² and its lateral jerk to 5 m/s³ (averaged over half a second). The physical tyre limit on dry asphalt is around 8–10 m/s², on snow 2–3 m/s².

7.2Sampling in the Frenet frame

The most widely taught highway planner, from Werling and colleagues (2010), samples in the Frenet frame of Section 4.2. Lateral motion is planned as a quintic polynomial d(t) from the current lateral state (offset, lateral velocity, lateral acceleration) to a target state, for a set of target offsets (stay, half a lane, next lane) and a set of durations T. Longitudinal motion is planned the same way in s(t), towards a target speed or a target gap to the lead. A quintic is used because it is the lowest-order polynomial that can match position, velocity and acceleration at both ends, and among all such curves it minimises the integrated squared jerk, which is a good model of comfort.

For a lane change from rest to rest in d, the quintic has closed-form peaks that make the trade-off visible: the peak lateral acceleration is \(\frac{10}{\sqrt 3}\,\frac{D}{T^2} \approx 5.77\,D/T^2\) and the peak jerk is \(60\,D/T^3\). For D = 3.5 m, a 3 s lane change needs 2.25 m/s², 5 s needs 0.81 m/s², 8 s only 0.32 m/s². Shorter is harsher, but longer is not automatically better: it keeps the car longer in the old lane behind the slow car and longer half-way between lanes, where it occupies both. Figure 7.1 shows the candidates for the running scenario, each checked against the predicted motion of the slow car ahead and the fast car in the target lane.

The interactive Figure 7.2 lets you vary the duration and the two gaps. It uses the same checks as a simple planner: lateral acceleration below 2 m/s², at least 10 m to the slow car while the ego car still occupies its lane (until its outer edge has left, at d = 2.7 m), and a cut-in criterion for the fast car: when the ego car starts to occupy the target lane (at d = 0.8 m), the fast car must be able to keep a 1-second gap by braking at no more than 3 m/s². With the default gaps of 40 m, durations of 4 to 6 s pass; 3 s is too harsh and 8 s brings the ego car within 9.7 m of the slow car and forces the fast car to brake at 17 m/s², which is impossible. Shrink the rear gap to 25 m and no duration works: the fast car is only about 20 m behind when the ego car enters its lane, less than one second at 100 km/h. Enlarge it to 60 m and the fast car barely notices.

Interesting detail

Sampling planners are popular in production because their behaviour is easy to explain: every rejected candidate has a reason (too harsh, collision with object 17 at t = 3.2 s), which can be logged and visualised. Optimisation-based planners produce better trajectories, but when they fail, "the solver did not converge" is much harder to debug.

7.3Speed planning in the s–t graph

Many planners split the problem into a path ("where", geometry in s and d) and a speed profile along that path ("when"). The speed problem then has a beautiful graphical form, the s–t graph of Figure 7.3. The horizontal axis is time, the vertical axis is distance along the path. Every object that will cross or occupy the path becomes a forbidden region: a car ahead in the lane blocks everything above its rear bumper line, a pedestrian crossing at a known time blocks a band. The ego car's speed profile is a curve from the origin whose slope is its speed, and planning means finding a smooth, monotonic curve that stays below or above each region with a margin, within the acceleration and jerk limits.

The s–t graph also makes the choice between yielding and going visible. For a car crossing the path at a junction, the blocked region is a band; the ego curve can pass below it (wait, let the car pass) or above it (go first). That is a discrete choice, and Baidu Apollo's EM planner (Fan et al., 2018) handles it the way many planners do: first a dynamic programming search over a coarse grid of the s–t graph decides on which side of each region to pass, then a quadratic program smooths the profile inside the chosen corridor. The same two-step pattern, coarse discrete search to choose the topology and continuous optimisation to refine, is used for the path in s and d.

7.4Optimisation and collision checking

Optimisation-based planners represent the trajectory with a small number of parameters, for example the coefficients of piecewise polynomial splines or the positions at each time step, and solve for the parameters that minimise the cost subject to the constraints. If the cost is quadratic and the constraints are linear in the parameters (which is the case after the topology has been fixed and the corridor is described by linear bounds), the problem is a quadratic program that solvers such as OSQP solve in a millisecond. Fully nonlinear formulations, with the vehicle model and obstacle shapes in the constraints, are solved with sequential quadratic programming or interior-point methods and give better trajectories at the cost of time and robustness. Model predictive control (Section 10.3) is the same machinery applied at the control layer.

Collision checking is the inner loop of every planner, run thousands of times per cycle, so it must be fast. The car's rectangle is commonly approximated by three or four overlapping circles along its length; checking two cars then means checking a dozen circle pairs, which is cheap and conservative. The check is done at each time step against each object's predicted position for that time step, with the margin enlarged by the predicted uncertainty. For static obstacles, a distance transform of the occupancy grid gives the distance to the nearest obstacle in one lookup.

Pitfall

Checking collisions only at the trajectory's sample points misses obstacles that pass between two samples. At 0.1 s steps and a closing speed of 30 m/s, two consecutive samples are 3 m apart in relative position, more than a car's width. Planners either check swept volumes between samples or inflate the margins by half the step distance.

Talk track

"A trajectory generator either samples, for example quintic polynomials in the Frenet frame with a cost on jerk, time and distance, or optimises a spline with a QP. Many planners split path and speed: the speed profile is planned in the s–t graph, where other cars are forbidden regions. A coarse search picks the topology, pass before or after each car, and an optimiser smooths inside it. Collision checks use circle approximations at every time step with margins for uncertainty."

8Search and sampling algorithms: A*, Hybrid A*, RRT

Motorway planning can live with a few dozen candidates in the Frenet frame. A car park, a narrow street with parked cars or a three-point turn cannot: the space of possible paths has many branches, and the planner needs an algorithm that explores it systematically. Two families dominate: graph search on a discretised space (A* and its relatives) and random sampling (RRT and its relatives).

8.1A* and Dijkstra on a grid

The simplest way to plan a path among obstacles is to lay a grid over the area, mark the cells occupied by obstacles, and search for the cheapest path from the start cell to the goal cell, moving to the 8 neighbouring cells with a cost of 1 for straight and √2 for diagonal steps. Dijkstra's algorithm expands cells in order of their cost from the start, so its frontier grows like a ripple in every direction. A* expands cells in order of the cost so far plus a heuristic estimate of the cost to the goal; with the octile distance (the exact cost on an empty 8-connected grid) as heuristic, it never overestimates, so the first path it finds is still optimal, but the ripple is pulled towards the goal. The general algorithms, with proofs and an interactive comparison, are in Data structures and algorithms 5.4.

Figure 8.1 runs both on a small car park with three rows of parked cars. Both find the same path, cost 38.0 cells, but Dijkstra expands 466 of the 504 free cells and A* only 278. The saving is smaller than on an open field because the rows of parked cars force the search to explore around them, and the heuristic, which ignores obstacles, keeps pulling it into dead ends towards the goal. That is the general lesson: A* is only as good as its heuristic, and in a maze the straight-line distance is a weak one.

8.2Hybrid A* and state lattices: search that respects the car

A grid path has a flaw for a car: it can turn 90 degrees from one cell to the next, which no car can do. Smoothing the path afterwards helps on open ground but fails in tight spaces, where the only feasible manoeuvre may involve reversing. Hybrid A*, developed by Dolgov, Thrun and colleagues for Stanford's entry in the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge, fixes this by searching over poses (x, y, heading) and expanding each node with short drivable arcs: full left, straight and full right, forwards and in reverse (Figure 8.2). Each arc respects the minimum turning radius, so every path the search returns can be driven. Reversing and changes of direction get extra cost, so that the planner prefers fewer manoeuvres.

Two details make Hybrid A* work in practice. The heuristic is the maximum of two estimates: the shortest path for a car with a turning radius but no obstacles (the Reeds–Shepp distance, which allows reversing) and the shortest grid path with obstacles but no turning radius. Each is admissible and each knows something the other does not. Second, every few expansions the search tries to connect the current node to the goal directly with a Reeds–Shepp curve; if that curve is collision-free, the search ends early. The result is then smoothed by optimisation. State-lattice planners follow the same idea with a precomputed set of motion primitives that connect regularly spaced states exactly, which makes the search graph regular and fast to search, and are used for structured roads as well as for parking.

8.3RRT and RRT*: growing a tree by random sampling

Grids become impractical as the dimension grows: a grid over position, heading and speed with fine resolution has billions of cells. Sampling-based planners avoid discretisation. The rapidly-exploring random tree, RRT (LaValle, 1998), starts with a tree containing only the start. In each iteration it draws a random point in the space, finds the nearest node of the tree, and extends the tree from that node a short step towards the point, if the step is collision-free. Because large empty regions are more likely to contain the random point, the tree grows preferentially into unexplored space, which is the "rapidly exploring" property. A small fraction of samples is drawn at the goal to pull the tree towards it.

RRT finds a path quickly, but not a good one: in the experiment, over eight random seeds, the first path appeared after 129 to 297 nodes, and after 1200 nodes the path was still 39 to 52 units long (mean 47.5), against 38.0 for the grid optimum. RRT never improves a path once found, because a new node is simply attached to its nearest neighbour. RRT* (Karaman and Frazzoli, 2011) adds two steps: a new node is attached to whichever nearby node gives it the lowest cost from the start, and nearby nodes are then rewired through the new node if that shortens their path. With these steps the path converges towards the optimum as the number of samples grows: in the experiment the RRT* path was 35.3 to 37.5 units long after 1200 nodes (mean 36.6), shorter even than the grid optimum, because it is not restricted to eight directions. Figure 8.3 steps through both trees.

Pitfall

Randomness is a problem in a car. The same scene can produce a different path every cycle, so the trajectory jitters, and run-time is not bounded. Production uses of sampling planners therefore seed the tree with the previous cycle's path, cap the number of iterations, smooth the result, and use them mostly for unstructured, low-speed situations where a grid or lattice would be too coarse or too large.

8.4Which algorithm where

Each family has a natural home. Graph search on the road or lane graph is the only reasonable choice for routing. On structured roads at speed, where the car follows lanes and the decisions are few, Frenet sampling or lattice planners with an optimisation step dominate, because their candidate sets match the structure of the problem and their run-time is predictable. In car parks and other unstructured spaces at low speed, Hybrid A* is the standard, with RRT variants as alternatives where the space is large or high-dimensional. Optimisation-based smoothing and MPC appear at the end of almost every pipeline, whatever produced the first trajectory.

9Vehicle kinematics and dynamics

Planner and controller both need a model of how the car responds to steering, drive and brakes. The model can be very simple at parking speed and must include the tyres at motorway speed; knowing which one is valid where is half of the craft.

9.1The kinematic bicycle model, with numbers

The bicycle model merges the two front wheels into one and the two rear wheels into one, connected by the wheelbase L. In its kinematic form it assumes that the wheels roll without slipping sideways, so each wheel moves in the direction it points. Then the car rotates about a turning centre on the extension of the rear axle, and simple geometry (Figure 9.1) gives the curvature of the path of the rear axle as \(\kappa = 1/R = \tan\delta / L\), where δ is the road-wheel steering angle. The yaw rate is \(\dot\psi = v\,\kappa\) and the lateral acceleration \(a_y = v^2 \kappa\). Together with \(\dot x = v\cos\psi\) and \(\dot y = v\sin\psi\), these four lines are the model used by most planners.

Now the title of the chapter can be made concrete. The driver, or the system, turns the steering wheel by 3 degrees. With a typical steering ratio of 15, the road wheels turn by 0.2 degrees. The kinematic model says the car now drives on a circle of radius \(L/\tan\delta = 2.8 / \tan(0.2°) \approx 802\) m, and at 27.8 m/s that circle needs a lateral acceleration of \(27.8^2/802 = 0.96\) m/s². The lane change of the running scenario peaks at 0.81 m/s², so by this model it needs about 2.5 degrees at the steering wheel. Steering inputs at motorway speed are tiny, which is why a steering angle sensor with a resolution of 0.1 degree and an EPS that can position the rack precisely both matter.

At parking speed the same model gives very different numbers: with the road wheels at 33 degrees (more than one full turn of the steering wheel), the rear axle drives on a circle of 4.3 m radius. Here the kinematic model is excellent, because at walking pace the tyres generate almost no lateral force and hardly slip. At motorway speed, as the next section shows, it is off by a factor of two.

9.2When tyres slip: the dynamic model and understeer

A tyre produces lateral force only by deforming, which means its contact patch moves in a slightly different direction from the one the wheel points in. The angle between the two is the slip angle, and for small slip angles the lateral force is proportional to it: \(F_y = C_\alpha\,\alpha\), with the cornering stiffness Cα (Figure 9.2, right). At larger slip angles the force saturates at the friction limit μFz and then falls as the tyre starts sliding. Everyday driving stays in the linear region; emergency manoeuvres do not.

The dynamic bicycle model adds the slip angles of the front and rear axle and the car's mass and yaw inertia. Its most useful result is the steady-state steering needed for a curve: \(\delta = L/R + K_{us}\,a_y\). The first term is the kinematic one; the second, proportional to lateral acceleration, appears because the front and rear tyres need different slip angles to carry their share of the centripetal force. Production cars are designed with positive \(K_{us}\), called understeer, because an understeering car responds to more speed in a bend by widening its path, which drivers can correct, rather than by turning in further and spinning. A typical value is a few degrees of road-wheel angle per g of lateral acceleration.

With \(K_{us}\) = 2°/g the numbers change markedly. The yaw response per degree of steering, which the kinematic model says grows linearly with speed (\(v/L\)), instead flattens and peaks at the characteristic speed \(\sqrt{L/K_{us}}\), here 101 km/h, where it is exactly half the kinematic value (Figure 9.2, left). For the running example that means the 3 degree steering-wheel input produces 0.49 m/s² instead of 0.96, a circle of 1589 m instead of 802, and the lane change needs about 5 degrees of steering wheel instead of 2.5. A controller that uses the kinematic model as feedforward at motorway speed will therefore consistently steer too little and rely on its feedback to make up the difference.

In practice

Teams usually use the kinematic model below about 20–30 km/h and in the planner's coarse checks, and a dynamic model (or a kinematic model with an understeer correction in the feedforward) in the controller at speed. The cornering stiffness and understeer gradient are identified from test drives (steady-state circular driving and steering-step tests) for each vehicle variant, because tyres, loading and even tyre pressure change them.

Interesting detail

The bicycle model ignores load transfer, roll, and the differences between left and right wheels. That is acceptable up to roughly 4 m/s² of lateral acceleration on dry roads. Emergency evasion and stability control need a full two-track model with a nonlinear tyre model; articulated vehicles such as trucks with trailers need additional bodies and hitch angles, which the commercial vehicles topic covers.

9.3Longitudinal dynamics and the actuators

Longitudinal motion is governed by the balance of drive or brake force against aerodynamic drag (growing with the square of speed), rolling resistance and the slope component of gravity. A controller requests an acceleration, and the powertrain and brake systems decide how to produce it: engine or motor torque, recuperation in an electric car, friction brakes when more deceleration is needed than recuperation can provide. On dry asphalt the friction limit allows about 8–9 m/s² of braking; on wet roads perhaps 5–6, on snow 2–3.

The actuators are where software meets hardware, and their behaviour shapes the controller. Electric power steering (EPS) accepts either an angle request, which its own position controller tracks, or a torque overlay added to the driver's torque. Angle interfaces are simpler for the controller; torque interfaces let the driver override naturally and are the usual choice for Level 2 assistance. Every interface has rate limits, delays and safety limits imposed by the EPS supplier, often 30–100 ms of effective delay. Brake systems need a few hundred milliseconds to build full pressure from rest; electro-mechanical brake boosters are faster than vacuum boosters. These delays, not the controller's algorithm, often decide how tight the tracking can be (Section 10.2).

"The kinematic bicycle model, κ = tan δ / L, is exact at parking speed and the default for planners. At motorway speed tyres slip, and with a typical understeer gradient the car turns about half as fast as the kinematic model says at around 100 km/h. 10Control: following the trajectory

The controller's job is narrow and unforgiving: make the car follow the planned trajectory, every 10 ms, despite model errors, wind, road slope, actuator delays and its own measurement noise. Most controllers combine a feedforward, which does what the plan says should be needed, with a feedback, which corrects what actually happens.

10.1Feedforward plus feedback, lateral and longitudinal

If the model were perfect, the controller would only need feedforward: the planned trajectory carries the curvature at each instant, the vehicle model converts curvature to steering angle (including the understeer term), and the planned acceleration plus estimated drag and slope gives the drive or brake request. Feedforward is fast because it acts before an error exists. Feedback corrects what the model gets wrong, by measuring the error between the planned and the actual state (lateral offset, heading error, speed error) and steering against it. A controller with only feedback would always lag behind the plan; one with only feedforward would drift away from it.

The lateral and longitudinal loops are usually separate (Figure 10.1, first tab), coupled only through the speed, which the lateral controller uses as a parameter. This works well at moderate accelerations; at the limit of grip, where steering and braking compete for the same tyre force, combined controllers or MPC with a coupled model are needed. Feedback in both loops uses the localization output, so localization noise and latency appear directly in the steering: a pose that jumps by 10 cm at each GNSS update produces a steering twitch at each update.

10.2Lateral control: pure pursuit, Stanley and LQR

Pure pursuit (Coulter, 1992) is the most intuitive path tracker. Pick a point on the path a look-ahead distance ℓ ahead of the rear axle; compute the circular arc from the rear axle, tangent to the car's heading, that passes through that point; steer to drive that arc (Figure 10.1, second tab). If α is the angle between the heading and the line to the look-ahead point, the arc's curvature is \(2\sin\alpha/\ell\) and, by the bicycle model, the steering angle is \(\delta = \arctan(2L\sin\alpha/\ell)\). The only tuning parameter is ℓ, and it has a clear meaning: a short look-ahead reacts strongly to small errors, a long one smooths and anticipates but cuts corners because it aims at a point that is already around the bend.

Figure 10.2 simulates pure pursuit on a kinematic bicycle with a 0.1 s steering lag, following a lane change spread over 80 m of road and starting 0.5 m off the path. At 100 km/h with no extra delay, every look-ahead works, but 5 m produces lateral acceleration spikes of 16 m/s² while correcting the initial offset. With 100 ms of delay, a look-ahead of 5 m becomes unstable, 10 m settles but still spikes, and 15–20 m tracks within a few centimetres. With 300 ms of delay, even 20 m keeps weaving and the look-ahead must be at least 30 m. At the other end, 50 m cuts the lane change by about 0.7 m. That is the central lesson of lateral control: delay forces you to look further ahead, and looking further ahead costs tracking accuracy in curves.

Two other lateral controllers are widely known. The Stanley controller, used by Stanford's car that won the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, steers by the heading error plus a term in the cross-track error at the front axle: \(\delta = \psi_e + \arctan(k\,e/v)\). It tracks tightly and converges exponentially for small errors, but it is sensitive to noise in the heading. The linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) linearises the lateral error dynamics (lateral offset, its rate, heading error and its rate, from the dynamic bicycle model) and computes the feedback gains that minimise a weighted sum of squared errors and squared steering. It handles the vehicle dynamics properly and is the standard in many production lane-keeping controllers, usually with gains scheduled by speed and a feedforward for the path curvature.

Pitfall

Simulations that ignore actuator delay produce controllers that oscillate in the car. The relevant delay is the sum of everything between measuring and acting: camera exposure and processing, localization, planning cycle, network transport, controller cycle and the EPS's own response, easily 150–300 ms end to end. The controller must be tuned and tested with that delay in the loop, or delay-compensated by predicting the state forward.

10.3Model predictive control: planning inside the controller

Model predictive control (MPC) turns control into a small, repeated optimisation. At each cycle it takes the current state, predicts the vehicle's motion over a horizon with a model, and chooses the sequence of future inputs that minimises a cost (tracking error, input effort, jerk) subject to constraints (steering limit, steering rate limit, lateral acceleration limit). It then applies only the first input of that sequence, and at the next cycle repeats the whole optimisation from the newly measured state. This receding horizon gives MPC its two strengths: it sees the reference coming and acts early, and it respects constraints explicitly instead of hoping that a tuned gain stays within them.

The experiment in Figure 10.3 is a linear MPC for the lateral motion at 100 km/h, with the state lateral offset, heading and curvature, the input curvature rate, a lateral acceleration limit of 2 m/s² and a lateral jerk limit of 5 m/s³. The requested lane change is deliberately too aggressive: 3.5 m in 3 s needs 2.25 m/s². With a 2 s horizon (40 steps of 50 ms), the MPC starts steering 0.75 s before the reference begins, never needs more than 1.42 m/s², and stays within 0.20 m of the reference (RMS 0.10 m). With a 0.4 s horizon it notices the lane change only when it has started, runs into the 2 m/s² limit and overshoots the new lane centre by 1.5 m. Each solve with OSQP took a median of 0.27 ms on the container's CPU, so the 2 s horizon is not expensive; on an automotive microcontroller with a hand-written or generated solver, similar problems run within a 10 ms cycle.

Why it matters

MPC blurs the line between planning and control. A planner is an optimiser with a long horizon and a coarse model; an MPC controller is an optimiser with a short horizon and a precise model. Many modern stacks use a long-horizon planner at 10 Hz and an MPC tracker at 50–100 Hz, and the question in a design review is which constraints each layer owns, so that they do not fight.

10.4Longitudinal control and adaptive cruise control

The longitudinal loop is typically a cascade. The outer loop chooses a target acceleration from the planned speed profile and from the gap to the lead vehicle; the inner loop turns the target acceleration into torque and brake requests, compensating for drag, slope and the powertrain's own dynamics with feedforward plus a PI controller on the acceleration error. Switching between drive and brake needs care: a hysteresis band avoids alternating between a little throttle and a little brake, which is both uncomfortable and wears the brakes.

Adaptive cruise control (ACC) is the classic longitudinal function. It keeps a set speed when the lane is free and a time gap to the lead vehicle otherwise. The time gap, rather than a fixed distance, scales the safety distance with speed: 1.8 s at 100 km/h is 50 m. ISO 15622 requires that the shortest selectable time gap is at least 0.8 s. A good ACC also manages the transitions: reacting to a cut-in without harsh braking, accelerating smoothly when the lead leaves, and limiting deceleration to a comfortable 2–3 m/s², leaving harder braking to emergency braking (AEB), which is a separate function with its own safety requirements.

Talk track

"Controllers combine feedforward from the planned curvature and acceleration with feedback on the tracking error. Pure pursuit has one parameter, the look-ahead, which must grow with speed and with delay. Stanley and LQR are the other classics; production lane keeping often uses LQR with speed-scheduled gains. MPC solves a small optimisation each cycle, sees the reference coming and respects limits explicitly. In practice, end-to-end delay of 150–300 ms is what limits tracking, not the algorithm."

11Safety envelopes, fallback and driver assistance

A planner is a large, complex piece of software, increasingly with learned parts, and it will sometimes be wrong. The safety architecture around it assumes exactly that: a simpler component checks every trajectory against rules that can be argued about, and a fallback is ready for the moment the check fails or the system itself degrades.

11.1Why a planner needs a safety envelope

It is not feasible to prove that a modern planner, with its cost functions, prediction models and possibly neural networks, produces a safe trajectory in every situation. It is feasible to define what "safe" means in a narrower, checkable form, and to verify each trajectory against that definition at run-time. This is the doer–checker pattern from functional safety (see Functional safety 4.2) applied to planning (Figure 11.1). The planner (doer) proposes; a safety checker validates the proposal against a safety envelope: minimum distances to other road users, staying inside the drivable area, respecting traffic rules that must never be broken, and staying within the vehicle's physical limits. If the check passes, the trajectory goes to the controller. If it fails, an arbiter switches to a fallback trajectory, which a separate, simple fallback planner keeps ready at all times.

The checker's value comes from being simpler and more independent than the planner, not from being smarter. It typically uses the same world model, which is a shared dependency the safety analysis must address (a perception fault fools both), but its logic is small enough to be specified completely, reviewed and tested exhaustively, and developed to a higher ASIL than the planner. The definition of "safe distance" it checks against should be explicit and agreed, which is where formal models such as RSS come in.

11.2RSS: a formula for "safe distance"

Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS), published by Shalev-Shwartz, Shammah and Shashua of Mobileye in 2017, formalises common-sense driving rules as mathematical conditions. Its best-known rule is the safe longitudinal distance to a car ahead. The worst case it assumes is: the car ahead brakes as hard as it can (bmax); during its response time ρ the ego car may still accelerate (at up to amax); after ρ it brakes at least moderately (bmin). The ego car is at a safe distance if, in that worst case, it still stops behind the car ahead:

\[ d_{\min} = \left[\, v_r\rho + \tfrac12 a_{\max}\rho^2 + \frac{(v_r + \rho\,a_{\max})^2}{2\,b_{\min}} - \frac{v_f^2}{2\,b_{\max}} \right]_+ \]

where vr is the rear (ego) speed and vf the front speed. With ρ = 0.5 s, amax = 2 m/s², bmin = 4 m/s² and bmax = 8 m/s², two cars at 100 km/h need 69 m, a time gap of 2.5 s; the 2-second rule gives 56 m. The interesting property, visible in Figure 11.2, is how strongly the result depends on the assumed braking. The distance grows with the square of speed because the formula assumes the ego car brakes only half as hard as the car ahead. If the ego car is assumed to brake at 6 m/s², the required distance at 100 km/h drops to 35 m; if both brake equally hard, to 18 m. The parameters are therefore not a detail: they are the safety policy, and they must be chosen per ODD and justified. IEEE 2846-2022 standardises a minimum set of such reasonably foreseeable assumptions for automated driving safety models.

RSS has similar rules for lateral distance, for right of way (being given the right of way is not the same as taking it), for limited visibility (drive so that you can stop for whatever may emerge from an occluded area) and for the proper response when a rule is violated by someone else. Its strength is that it turns vague intent ("keep a safe distance") into a checkable condition; its limits are that the assumptions must be chosen, that rules can conflict in dense traffic, and that it says nothing about whether perception saw the other car at all.

Pitfall

A safety checker that is too strict makes the system useless: with conservative RSS parameters, the planner cannot merge into normal motorway traffic, where real gaps of 1–1.5 s are common. A checker that is tuned until it lets the planner through has stopped being a checker. The parameters have to be argued from the ODD and from data on real braking behaviour, and the residual risk accepted explicitly in the safety case.

11.3Fallback, minimal risk manoeuvre and degradation

The fallback is what the system does when it can no longer continue nominally: a sensor fails, localization integrity is lost, the planner's output keeps being rejected, or the ODD ends and the driver does not take over. At Level 2 the fallback is the driver, and the system's duty is to warn early and hand back cleanly. At Level 3 and above, the system must perform a minimal risk manoeuvre (MRM) to reach a minimal risk condition (MRC) itself: typically braking to a stop in the current lane with hazard lights on, or, if the system can, moving to the hard shoulder first.

The degradation chain in Figure 11.1 (bottom) is the usual structure: nominal; degraded (reduced speed, no lane changes, larger gaps) when one redundant source fails; MRM when the system can no longer guarantee safe operation; MRC at standstill. Each transition is triggered by monitors: localization integrity (Section 2.5), sensor and perception health, timing watchdogs on the planning cycle, and driver monitoring. The fallback trajectory itself must be available even if the main planner has crashed, which is why it is often computed by a separate, simple planner on separate hardware or in a separate partition, and refreshed every cycle so that it always starts from the current state. How safety goals translate into such degradation modes, and how fast the reaction must be (the fault-tolerant time interval), is in Functional safety 4.7 and 2.5.

In practice

A "stop in lane" MRM on a motorway is itself risky: a stationary car in a live lane can be hit from behind. This is why regulations for Level 3 motorway systems require a long takeover lead time, so that most handovers end with the driver in control, and why later generations add the ability to reach the shoulder. The MRM is a last resort, and the design goal is that it is rarely needed.

11.4Driver assistance features and who is responsible

Most of what is sold today is driver assistance, and each feature uses a subset of the stack in this chapter. Adaptive cruise control is longitudinal control with a lead-vehicle selection from the world model. Lane keeping assistance is lateral control on camera lane markings, usually without a map. Lane centring combined with ACC is the typical Level 2 "highway assist". Automated lane change adds the behaviour layer and the rear-gap check of Section 7.2, which is why it needs rear-facing radars. Automatic emergency braking (AEB) is a separate, narrow function with its own perception path and a high integrity, because it acts when everything else has failed. Parking assistance uses ultrasonic sensors and cameras with Hybrid A*-style planning at low speed.

Regulation decides what each feature may do. UN R79 governs steering systems including lane keeping and system-initiated lane changes; R171 (Driver Control Assistance Systems, 2024) sets the rules for Level 2 systems that steer, brake and accelerate over longer periods, with driver monitoring; R157 governs Level 3 automated lane keeping, originally up to 60 km/h and since 2023 up to 130 km/h with lane changes; R152 governs AEB. The details are in Standards and regulations 7.4. The practical consequence for engineers is that the same planner code may run under very different responsibility models, and the safety case, not the code, changes most between them.

Talk track

"Around the planner sits a doer–checker structure: a simple checker validates each trajectory against an explicit envelope, for example RSS distances, and an arbiter switches to an always-ready fallback trajectory if it fails. RSS makes 'safe distance' a formula, but the result is dominated by the braking assumptions, so the parameters are a safety policy. Degradation goes from nominal through reduced function to a minimal risk manoeuvre, triggered by integrity, health and timing monitors."

12Learned and end-to-end planners

Hand-written planners are made of rules and cost functions, and every unusual situation needs another rule. Learned planners promise to absorb the long tail from data instead. Since 2023 the debate has moved from "whether" to "how much, and how to check it".

12.1Why learn the planner

The modular pipeline has two structural weaknesses. Errors compound across interfaces: perception commits to a list of boxes, prediction commits to a few trajectories, and information that was not passed on (the posture of a pedestrian, the hesitation of a driver) is lost to the planner. And the behaviour of the planner is defined by rules and weights written by engineers, which scale poorly to the variety of real traffic: unprotected turns, construction zones, police directing traffic. Learning the planner from human driving, imitation learning, addresses the second weakness; learning the whole chain end to end addresses the first as well.

The idea is old: ALVINN (Pomerleau, 1989) steered a van with a small neural network, and NVIDIA's PilotNet (2016) mapped camera images directly to steering. The classic difficulty is covariate shift: a policy trained only on expert driving never sees the states that follow its own small mistakes, so the mistakes compound (see Training neural networks 9.2 on distribution shift). Remedies include adding perturbed examples (ChauffeurNet, Waymo 2018, synthesised deviations from the expert trajectory and trained the network to recover), interactive data collection where an expert labels the states the policy actually visits (DAgger, Ross et al., 2011), and closed-loop training in simulation.

12.2Planning-oriented end-to-end networks

UniAD (Hu et al., "Planning-oriented Autonomous Driving", best paper at CVPR 2023) gave the current wave of end-to-end planners its template (Figure 12.1, middle). A shared bird's-eye-view backbone turns multi-camera images into a BEV feature map. A sequence of transformer modules, each with its own learned queries, then performs tracking, online mapping, motion forecasting and occupancy prediction, and a planner head outputs the ego trajectory. All modules are trained jointly, and the losses of the intermediate tasks are kept, so the network still produces boxes, maps and predictions that can be inspected; but the interfaces between modules are learned features, not hand-designed lists, and the whole network is optimised for the final planning quality. Successors such as VAD (2023) use vectorised scene representations to make this faster.

In industry, Tesla has described its FSD version 12 (2024) as replacing most of its hand-written planning code with a neural network trained on fleet video; other companies, including Wayve, build their driving systems around learned end-to-end models. Most production stacks from established suppliers remain modular with learned components (perception, prediction, sometimes trajectory scoring), with a learned planner proposing candidates that a classical layer checks and refines.

12.3Vision-language-action models

Vision-language-action (VLA) models take the idea further by building on large vision-language models pre-trained on internet-scale data (see Generative AI 2.1 and Deep learning for vision 4.4). In robotics, RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023) and OpenVLA (2024) showed that such a model can output robot actions as tokens and transfer world knowledge to new tasks. In driving, DriveVLM (2024) combined a VLM that reasons about the scene in language with a classical planner that produces the fast trajectory, and was deployed in that dual form; Waymo's research model EMMA (2024), built on Gemini, maps camera images directly to trajectories, detections and road graphs as text. The appeal is that the model brings general knowledge ("a ball rolling into the road may be followed by a child") and can explain its decisions in words.

The obstacles are equally clear. These models are large and slow, with latencies of hundreds of milliseconds on automotive hardware, far from a 10 Hz planning loop with margin. They can produce fluent but wrong reasoning. And their failure modes are hard to bound, which makes a safety argument difficult. The pattern that has emerged is a dual system: a slow model that reasons about the scene a few times per second and gives guidance or a coarse plan, and a fast conventional or compact learned planner that produces the trajectory, inside a rule-based safety envelope.

12.4Evaluation: open loop versus closed loop

Learned planners raised an uncomfortable question about evaluation. The early end-to-end papers reported open-loop metrics on the nuScenes dataset: the L2 distance between the planned and the human trajectory over 3 s, and a collision rate. Two 2023–2024 studies showed how weak this is. Zhai et al. ("AD-MLP") and Li et al. ("Is Ego Status All You Need for Open-Loop End-to-End Autonomous Driving?", CVPR 2024) found that a simple model using only the ego vehicle's own past speed and acceleration, with no camera input at all, matched or beat sophisticated networks on these metrics, because most nuScenes driving is straight and steady. Open-loop metrics reward predicting what the human did next, not driving well.

Closed-loop evaluation lets the planner drive in a simulator, where its own actions change what happens next and errors compound as they would on the road. On the nuPlan benchmark, Dauner et al. (2023) showed that a simple rule-based planner, PDM-Closed (follow the lane centre with an intelligent-driver speed model and score a few speed variants), matched or beat the learned planners of the time in closed loop, and a hybrid variant won the 2023 nuPlan challenge. The lesson is not that learning fails, but that a planner must be judged in closed loop, against strong classical baselines, on scenarios that matter for safety. Newer benchmarks (NAVSIM, Bench2Drive in CARLA) are attempts to make that cheaper and more representative.

Talk track

"End-to-end planners such as UniAD train perception, prediction and planning jointly for the planning objective, keeping intermediate outputs for inspection. VLA models add a large vision-language model for world knowledge and explanations, but are too slow for the control loop, so they act as a slow advisor to a fast planner. Two cautions: open-loop metrics on nuScenes can be matched by a model that sees only ego speed, so evaluate in closed loop against strong rule-based baselines; and in a product, any learned planner sits behind a rule-based safety checker."

13Augmented navigation as a system feature

Augmented navigation draws the route onto the live camera image or into a head-up display: an arrow that lies on the lane to turn into. It uses almost every layer of this chapter, and it fails visibly when any of them is slightly off, which makes it a good integration test for the whole chain.

13.1The chain behind an arrow on the road

The feature looks like graphics, but most of its difficulty is upstream (Figure 13.1). The route planner and the electronic horizon say what the next manoeuvre is and where: "exit right in 300 m". Lane-level localization says where the car is relative to that exit, and on which lane. The camera's pose relative to the road, its extrinsic calibration plus the current pitch and roll of the body (which change with braking, acceleration, load and road bumps), says how the road appears in the image. Only then can the renderer place a 3D arrow on the correct lane, 30 to 80 m ahead, project it into the image or the HUD's virtual image plane, and draw it. The rendering itself, including HUD optics and distortion, belongs to the graphics and AR topic; here the interest is the error budget.

13.2Registration error and latency

The overlay is convincing only if it stays registered with the road, and the geometry is unforgiving. Rays to points on the road far ahead are nearly parallel to the road surface: a camera at 1.3 m height looks down at 2.5° to a point 30 m ahead and at 1.2° to a point 60 m ahead. A pitch error of 0.5°, which a moderate braking manoeuvre easily causes if the body pitch is not compensated, moves the drawn position of the 30 m point to 25 m, and of the 60 m point to 42.8 m. Even 0.25° moves the 30 m point by 2.7 m. A heading error of 1° shifts an arrow 50 m ahead sideways by 0.87 m, a quarter of a lane, enough to point at the wrong lane at a split.

Latency adds to this. If the image is 100 ms old when the overlay is drawn on it (or if the overlay is drawn from a pose 100 ms older than the HUD's view of the real world), then at a yaw rate of 10°/s in a bend the overlay is rotated by 1°, about half a metre at 30 m. Systems therefore estimate pitch and roll continuously (from the IMU, from the vanishing point of the lane markings, or from the road plane estimated by perception), predict the pose forward to the display time, and keep far-field graphics deliberately vague, for example as a carpet that fades with distance instead of a sharp arrow tip at 80 m.

Why it matters

Augmented navigation is often treated as an infotainment feature, but its requirements on localization, calibration and latency are close to those of automated driving. A team that builds it well learns to measure end-to-end latency, pitch estimation and lane-level localization in the vehicle, which is exactly the infrastructure an automated driving function needs. A team that builds it badly learns that a "graphics bug" was really a time-stamp bug three layers upstream.

Talk track

"Augmented navigation chains routing, lane-level localization, camera pose and rendering. Because rays to the far road are nearly parallel to it, half a degree of pitch moves an overlay at 30 m by about 5 m, so body pitch must be estimated live and the pose predicted to display time. It is a good integration test for the whole positioning and timing chain."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

Walk me through the stack from localization to the steering command.Basic

Localization estimates the pose in the map frame with a covariance. Perception and the world model describe objects, lanes and free space at a common time and frame. Prediction gives each relevant agent several future trajectories with probabilities over 3–8 s. The route planner finds a lane-level path; the behaviour planner picks the manoeuvre; the motion planner produces a collision-free, drivable, comfortable time-stamped trajectory at about 10 Hz. The controller turns it into steering and acceleration requests at 50–100 Hz using feedforward from curvature and acceleration plus feedback on the tracking error, and the EPS, brakes and powertrain execute them.

Why is GNSS not enough for lane-level localization, and what do you add?Basic

Standalone GNSS is accurate to metres, disappears in tunnels and has slowly varying errors from multipath and the atmosphere; a lane leaves only about 0.8 m on each side. Add IMU and wheel odometry in an error-state EKF for smooth, fast propagation and bias estimation, corrections such as RTK or PPP where available, and map matching: lane markings seen by the camera fix lateral position and heading, landmarks or lidar scan matching fix the longitudinal position.

What is the difference between loose and tight GNSS/INS coupling?Intermediate

Loose coupling feeds the receiver's position and velocity solution into the filter; it is simple but gets nothing when fewer than four satellites are visible. Tight coupling feeds raw pseudoranges and Doppler per satellite, so two or three satellites still constrain the solution, and individual faulty satellites can be detected and excluded by their residuals. It needs raw measurements and more modelling (clock states, per-satellite errors).

What is an HD map, what does it contain, and why are some companies moving away from it?Basic

A lane-level map with centre lines and boundaries to about 10–20 cm, marking types, lane topology across junctions, traffic lights and which lanes they control, speed limits and localization landmarks, in formats such as OpenDRIVE, Lanelet2 or NDS.Live. It gives the car information beyond sensor range, but it is expensive to survey and keep current, and it is wrong wherever the road has changed. Hence the trend to SD maps plus online vectorized mapping from perception, using HD maps where available.

What is the Frenet frame and when does it break down?Intermediate

Coordinates (s, d): arc length along a reference line, usually the lane centre, and lateral offset from it. Lane keeping is d ≈ 0, a lane change is d from 0 to 3.5 m, and longitudinal and lateral planning can be separated. It breaks down where the reference line curves tightly (the closest-point projection becomes ambiguous and straight lines in s–d map to awkward curvatures), so parking and tight urban manoeuvres are planned in Cartesian coordinates.

How would you predict the trajectory of a car in the next lane?Intermediate

Constant velocity or CTRV for the first second; manoeuvre-based prediction that classifies intent (keep, change left/right) from lateral velocity, distance to the marking, indicator and lane topology, and generates a map-following trajectory per manoeuvre; or a learned model (VectorNet, MTR) over the agent histories and the map, outputting K trajectories with probabilities. Evaluate with minADE, minFDE and miss rate, and consider interaction: the car will react to the ego car's indicator.

Explain route, behaviour and motion planning and why they are separated.Basic

Route: shortest path on the road or lane graph over kilometres, recomputed on change. Behaviour: the discrete manoeuvre decision over seconds (follow, change lane into this gap, yield, stop). Motion: the continuous, time-stamped trajectory over 5–8 s at 10 Hz. Each layer fixes choices that make the next layer's problem tractable; the cost is that a choice above may be infeasible below, so feasibility must flow back up.

How do quintic polynomials help in trajectory generation?Intermediate

A quintic is the lowest-order polynomial that matches position, velocity and acceleration at both ends, and it minimises integrated squared jerk, so it is a natural comfortable manoeuvre. In a Frenet sampling planner, lateral quintics to several target offsets and durations and longitudinal quartics or quintics to target speeds are generated, checked for limits and collisions, and the cheapest is chosen. For a rest-to-rest lane change, peak lateral acceleration is 5.77 D/T², so 3.5 m in 5 s needs 0.81 m/s².

Compare A*, Hybrid A* and RRT*.Intermediate

A* searches a graph or grid optimally using an admissible heuristic; on a grid it ignores the car's heading. Hybrid A* searches over poses with drivable arcs, forwards and reverse, with a heuristic combining the Reeds–Shepp distance and an obstacle-aware grid distance, and tries analytic Reeds–Shepp connections to the goal; the standard for parking. RRT* grows a random tree, choosing the best parent and rewiring neighbours, so it is asymptotically optimal and works in high dimensions, but it is random and has no bounded run-time.

What is the kinematic bicycle model, and when is it not enough?Basic

Two wheels on a wheelbase L, no lateral slip: curvature κ = tan δ / L, yaw rate vκ, lateral acceleration v²κ. It is exact at low speed. At higher speed tyres need slip angles to produce lateral force, and the dynamic bicycle model with cornering stiffness and understeer is needed; at 100 km/h a typical car turns about half as fast as the kinematic model predicts. Near the grip limit even the linear tyre model fails.

How does pure pursuit work, and how do you choose the look-ahead?Basic

Choose a point on the path at distance ℓ ahead, compute the arc from the rear axle tangent to the heading through that point, curvature 2 sin α / ℓ, and steer δ = atan(2L sin α / ℓ). Short look-ahead tracks tightly but oscillates, especially with delay; long look-ahead is smooth but cuts corners. ℓ is scheduled with speed (about 1–2 s of travel) and must grow with the loop delay: in the experiment at 100 km/h, 5 m was unstable with 100 ms delay and 300 ms delay needed at least 30 m.

What is MPC and why is it popular for vehicle control?Intermediate

Model predictive control solves, every cycle, an optimisation of the future inputs over a horizon with a vehicle model, a cost on tracking error and effort, and explicit constraints (steering, steering rate, lateral acceleration), applies the first input and repeats. It anticipates the reference, handles constraints explicitly and coordinates multiple inputs. Its costs are computation, dependence on the model, and tuning of horizon and weights; linear MPC with a QP solver like OSQP runs in well under a millisecond on a PC.

Explain RSS and its safe longitudinal distance.Intermediate

Responsibility-Sensitive Safety formalises driving rules as conditions. The safe distance assumes the car ahead brakes at bmax, the ego car accelerates at up to amax during its response time ρ and then brakes at least bmin; the distance is vrρ + ½amaxρ² + (vr+ρamax)²/2bmin − vf²/2bmax. With ρ = 0.5 s, 2, 4 and 8 m/s² that is 69 m at 100 km/h. It is used as a checkable envelope in a safety checker; the parameters are a policy that must be justified for the ODD.

What happens when an automated driving function fails at Level 2 versus Level 3?Basic

At Level 2 the driver monitors and is the fallback; the system warns and hands back control. At Level 3 the system monitors its own limits within the ODD, requests a takeover with several seconds of lead time, and if the driver does not respond performs a minimal risk manoeuvre, typically stopping in lane with hazard lights or reaching the shoulder, to a minimal risk condition. This requires integrity monitoring, a safety checker and an always-ready fallback trajectory.

What is UniAD and how does it differ from a modular stack?Advanced

UniAD (CVPR 2023) is a planning-oriented end-to-end network: a BEV backbone feeds transformer modules for tracking, mapping, motion forecasting and occupancy, and a planner head, all trained jointly with the intermediate losses kept. Interfaces are learned queries instead of hand-designed object lists, so information is not lost between modules and the whole network is optimised for planning. It still exposes intermediate outputs for inspection, but it needs large data and closed-loop validation, and in a product it would sit behind a rule-based checker.

What is a vision-language-action model and where could it fit in a car?Advanced

A large vision-language model fine-tuned to output actions or trajectories, for example RT-2 and OpenVLA in robotics, DriveVLM and Waymo's EMMA in driving. It brings world knowledge and language explanations, but its latency (hundreds of ms), hallucination risk and hard-to-bound failure modes rule it out as the direct control loop today. The realistic fit is a slow advisor that reasons a few times per second and guides a fast planner, inside a rule-based safety envelope.

Why are open-loop metrics misleading for planners?Advanced

Open-loop metrics compare the planned trajectory with what the human did on a logged scene, so the planner's own actions never affect the future and compounding errors never appear. On nuScenes, a model using only the ego car's past speed and acceleration matched camera-based end-to-end planners on L2 error. Closed-loop simulation (nuPlan, CARLA, NAVSIM-style evaluation) lets errors compound and reveals that strong rule-based baselines like PDM-Closed are hard to beat.

How would you design the lane-change feature of a Level 2 highway assist?Advanced

Sensors: front camera and radar for the lead and lanes, rear-corner radars for the target lane. A behaviour state machine (keep, prepare, execute, complete, abort) with hysteresis and minimum dwell times, triggered by the driver or a slow lead. Gap acceptance with predicted positions and a cut-in criterion for the rear car (required braking to keep a time gap). A quintic or optimised trajectory with lateral acceleration and jerk limits (R79), MPC or LQR tracking, driver monitoring and torque-overlay EPS so the driver can override, and explicit abort paths before the line is crossed.

How do you test a planner?Intermediate

Unit tests on components (collision checker, cost terms, state machine transitions); scenario-based simulation with parameter sweeps over gaps, speeds and timing (OpenSCENARIO); log replay in open loop for regression and in closed loop with reactive agents; metrics for safety (collisions, minimum time-to-collision, RSS violations), comfort (jerk, acceleration), progress and rule compliance; hardware-in-the-loop for timing; and on-road testing focused on scenarios simulation cannot cover. Every disengagement and near miss becomes a new scenario.

What is ACC's time gap and why not a fixed distance?Basic

The time gap is the distance to the lead divided by own speed, typically 1–2 s selectable by the driver; ISO 15622 requires the shortest selectable gap to be at least 0.8 s. A time gap makes the distance grow with speed, matching the growth of reaction distance; a fixed distance would be far too long in city traffic and far too short on the motorway.

Q.2Why and what if

Why do lane markings improve lateral localization but not longitudinal?Why

A straight marking looks the same at every point along the road, so measuring the distance to it constrains only the direction perpendicular to it. In the experiment lateral error fell from 0.96 to 0.05 m while longitudinal stayed at 0.76 m. Longitudinal position needs features that differ along the road: signs, poles, lane starts, curvature, or dense lidar structure.

What if the localization filter treats GNSS errors as independent when they are correlated over 20 s?What if

The filter averages successive fixes as if each brought new information, so its covariance shrinks far below the true error. In the experiment it believed 0.32 m lateral while the true RMS was 0.96 m and 44% of samples were outside its 2σ bound. Downstream integrity checks then trust a wrong pose. Model the correlation as a state, inflate or decimate GNSS, and validate covariance against a reference.

Why does the planner re-plan every 100 ms instead of following one plan?Why

Every plan is computed from uncertain predictions and an imperfect state, and the car never follows it exactly. Re-planning from the current state absorbs new measurements, changed predictions and tracking errors before they accumulate. To avoid jumps, each new plan starts from the previously planned state at the current time (or from a blend with the measured state) so that consecutive trajectories are consistent.

What if prediction ignores that other drivers react to the ego car?What if

In dense traffic every gap looks closed, because the predicted trajectories of others occupy the target lane regardless of what the ego car does, and the planner never merges: the freezing robot problem. Conditional prediction (others' futures given the ego plan), joint prediction, or a behaviour layer that nudges and watches the reaction resolve it.

Why is a longer lane change not always safer?Why

It is gentler, but it keeps the ego car longer behind the slow car and longer straddling both lanes, while the fast car in the target lane keeps closing. In Figure 7.2 with 40 m gaps, 8 s came within 9.7 m of the slow car and would have forced the rear car to brake at 17 m/s². There is a window of acceptable durations, bounded by comfort on one side and by the other road users on the other.

What if the steering feedforward uses the kinematic model at 130 km/h?What if

Above the characteristic speed, an understeering car needs considerably more steering than the kinematic model says (about twice at 100 km/h with 2°/g). The feedforward steers too little in every curve, the feedback must supply the rest, so the car runs wide with a steady lateral error in long bends and responds late in transients. Add the understeer term Kus·ay or use a dynamic model.

Why does actuator delay force a longer look-ahead in pure pursuit?Why

A short look-ahead means a high feedback gain on lateral error. With delay, the correction arrives after the error has already changed, so a high gain overcorrects and the loop oscillates or diverges. A longer look-ahead lowers the effective gain and lets the controller act on where the path will be, which tolerates more delay at the price of cutting corners.

What if the MPC horizon is shorter than the manoeuvre?What if

The controller cannot see the reference change coming, so it starts late, needs more input than allowed and saturates at its constraints, then overshoots. In the experiment a 0.4 s horizon on a 3 s lane change hit the 2 m/s² limit and overshot by 1.5 m, while a 2 s horizon started 0.75 s early and stayed within 0.2 m. The horizon should cover the time needed to act within the constraints.

Why is the safety checker simpler than the planner, not smarter?Why

Its job is to be verifiable, not to find good trajectories. A small checker with explicit rules can be specified completely, reviewed, tested exhaustively and developed to a higher ASIL, and its independence from the planner's complexity is what makes the doer–checker argument work. A smart checker would inherit the same unverifiable complexity it is supposed to guard against.

What if RSS parameters are set very conservatively on a motorway?What if

Required gaps exceed those in real traffic (2.5 s or more at 100 km/h with bmin = 4 m/s²), so the checker rejects most lane changes and merges, the system becomes unusable or creates risk by hesitating in merges, and drivers switch it off. The parameters must reflect the ODD and real braking behaviour, with the residual risk argued in the safety case.

Why does the A* expansion advantage shrink in a car park?Why

The heuristic is the straight-line (octile) distance, which ignores obstacles. Rows of parked cars force detours, so the heuristic repeatedly pulls the search into regions that lead towards the goal but are blocked, and it must back off and explore around them. In the experiment A* expanded 278 cells instead of 466, a smaller saving than on open ground. A better, obstacle-aware heuristic recovers it, which is why Hybrid A* uses a precomputed grid distance.

What if the map says the lane continues but the camera sees a construction zone?What if

The map is stale. The system should detect the disagreement (marking types, lane count, temporary yellow markings, cones), reduce trust in the map locally, follow perception, and if the ODD excludes construction zones, request a takeover early. Reporting the disagreement to the map provider helps the fleet.

Why can an AR arrow 60 m ahead appear 17 m too close?Why

A camera 1.3 m high sees a road point at 60 m only 1.2° below the horizon, so rays to far road points are nearly parallel to the road. A 0.5° pitch error, easily caused by braking, changes that angle by 40% and moves the point to 42.8 m. Far-field overlays need live pitch estimation, latency compensation and deliberately soft graphics.

What if a learned planner beats the baseline on open-loop L2 error?What if

That shows it imitates the logged human trajectory well on average, which an ego-status-only model can also do on nuScenes. It says nothing about recovering from its own errors or reacting to others. Evaluate in closed loop, against strong rule-based baselines, on safety-relevant scenarios, before drawing conclusions.

Why does an MRM "stop in lane" exist if it is itself risky?Why

Because when the system can no longer drive safely and the driver does not respond, continuing is worse. Stopping in lane is the manoeuvre that requires the least capability and therefore remains possible after most failures. The design goal is to make it rare: long takeover lead times, degraded modes before it, and moving to the shoulder where the remaining capability allows.

ACC
Adaptive cruise control: keeps a set speed or a time gap to the lead vehicle.
ADASIS
Standard for transmitting the electronic horizon (map data along the probable path ahead) to driver assistance ECUs; v2 for SD content, v3 for lane-level content.
Alert limit
The largest position error a function can tolerate; compared with the protection level at run time.
A*
Graph search expanding nodes by cost so far plus an admissible heuristic estimate to the goal; optimal and usually faster than Dijkstra.
Behaviour planning
The layer that chooses the next manoeuvre (follow, change lane, yield, stop) from route, rules and scene.
Bicycle model
Vehicle model with one front and one rear wheel; kinematic (no slip, κ = tan δ / L) or dynamic (with tyre slip and cornering stiffness).
Characteristic speed
\(\sqrt{L/K_{us}}\) for an understeering car: the speed of maximum yaw response, where it is half the kinematic value.
Cornering stiffness
Slope of tyre lateral force versus slip angle in the linear region.
Covariate shift
In imitation learning, the policy reaching states the expert data never covered, so its errors compound.
Error-state EKF
Kalman filter that estimates the small error of an integrated nominal state (plus sensor biases) rather than the state itself.
Frenet frame
Coordinates along (s) and across (d) a reference line, usually the lane centre.
HD map
Lane-level map with geometry to about 10–20 cm, topology, regulatory elements and localization features.
Hybrid A*
A* over continuous poses expanded with drivable arcs, with Reeds–Shepp analytic expansions; standard for parking.
Imitation learning
Learning a driving policy from demonstrations of human driving.
Integrity
The trust that can be placed in the correctness of a position estimate, expressed through protection level, alert limit, integrity risk and time to alert.
Lanelet
A short piece of a lane defined by its left and right boundary, with successor and neighbour relations (Lanelet2).
LQR
Linear-quadratic regulator: optimal state-feedback gains for a linear model and a quadratic cost.
Map matching
Determining the car's position on the map by matching observations (a GNSS track, lane markings, landmarks, scans) to map content.
MPC
Model predictive control: repeated constrained optimisation over a receding horizon, applying only the first input.
MRM / MRC
Minimal risk manoeuvre, performed by the system to reach a minimal risk condition (usually standstill) when it cannot continue.
ODD
Operational design domain: the conditions (road type, speed, weather, time) under which a function is designed to operate.
Protection level
Run-time bound on the position error that is exceeded only with the specified integrity risk.
Pure pursuit
Path tracker steering along the arc through a look-ahead point on the path.
Quintic polynomial
Fifth-order polynomial matching position, velocity and acceleration at both ends; minimum-jerk manoeuvre profile.
RRT / RRT*
Rapidly-exploring random tree; RRT* adds best-parent selection and rewiring for asymptotic optimality.
RSS
Responsibility-Sensitive Safety: formal rules for safe distances and right of way, used as a checkable safety envelope.
Slip angle
Angle between the direction a tyre points and the direction its contact patch moves; needed to produce lateral force.
s–t graph
Distance along the path over time; obstacles become forbidden regions and the speed profile is a curve between them.
Stanley controller
Lateral controller steering by heading error plus atan of the cross-track error over speed.
Understeer gradient
Kus: extra steering angle needed per unit of lateral acceleration beyond the kinematic value.
VLA
Vision-language-action model: a vision-language model trained to output actions or trajectories.
