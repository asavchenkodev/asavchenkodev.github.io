Truck and commercial-vehicle perception

What changes when the camera and perception system sits on a heavy truck instead of a passenger car: the driver high up in a cab-over, large blind zones on the nearside and in front, a trailer that swings, cuts the corner and is swapped every day, a cab that moves on its own suspension. The chapter follows one running example, a European tractor with a 13.6 m semitrailer, through its blind zones, its camera and sensor suite, camera monitor systems, the blind-spot, moving-off and reversing detection that regulations now demand, trailer and articulation-angle perception, surround view across the coupling, forward emergency braking for a 40-tonne vehicle, the regulations and ratings that define all of this, and how it is validated. Buses appear as a side note.

1The big picture: why a truck needs different perception

A passenger-car perception stack mostly looks forward and asks "what is ahead of me and how fast is it approaching?". On a heavy truck the most dangerous questions are close and sideways: who is standing in front of the cab where the driver cannot see, who is riding alongside the trailer when the truck turns right, and where the trailer's rear wheels will go. The physics of a long, articulated, high vehicle changes which zones matter, where sensors can sit, and what the algorithms must estimate.

1.1The running example: a tractor-semitrailer in a European city

Throughout the chapter we use one vehicle: a two-axle tractor (category N3, the heavy goods vehicle category above 12 tonnes) with a flat-front cab-over, pulling a three-axle semitrailer. Its dimensions are the European norm: 2.55 m wide, about 16.5 m long in total, a tractor wheelbase of about 3.6 m, the fifth wheel (the coupling plate that holds the trailer's kingpin) about half a metre ahead of the tractor's rear axle, and a trailer whose axle group sits about 7.7 m behind the kingpin. Loaded it weighs up to 40 tonnes, roughly twenty times a car. The driver's eyes are about 2.5 m above the road, more than twice as high as in a car.

The vehicle spends half its life on motorways, where it behaves like a slow, heavy car: forward camera and radar, emergency braking, lane departure warning. The other half is the part that kills people: delivering in cities, turning right at junctions with cycle lanes, pulling away from traffic lights with pedestrians crossing close in front, reversing into loading bays. In the EU, heavy goods vehicles are involved in a disproportionate share of fatal crashes with cyclists and pedestrians relative to their number on the road, and the typical scenario is low speed, close range and on the nearside (the right side in right-hand traffic, the side away from the driver). That is why the truck-specific regulations in this chapter all concern close-range detection: blind-spot information for turning (UN R151), moving-off information (UN R159), reversing (UN R158) and direct vision (UN R167).

Why it matters

A passenger-car engineer moving to trucks usually expects "the same stack, bigger vehicle". The forward stack is indeed similar. But the features that are new, regulated and safety-critical on trucks are close-range VRU (vulnerable road user) detection around a vehicle whose shape changes while it drives. The hard problems are geometric: the trailer occludes and reflects, swings into the sensor's field of view, cuts inside the tractor's path, and carries no sensors you can rely on.

1.2Eight differences from a passenger car, and what each does to perception

The driver sits high. In a cab-over the driver's eyes are about 2.5 m above the road and the lower edge of the windscreen and side windows is close to 2 m. Figure 1.1 shows what that means with simple sight-line geometry: with an illustrative long-haul cab, the ground is hidden for about 2.5 m in front of the bumper and almost 6 m beside the nearside door, and a 1.1 m tall child standing closer than about 0.9 m in front of the cab, or within about 2.5 m of the nearside door, is completely invisible through the glass. In a car the eye is at the height of a child's head, so the child is visible at any distance even though the ground in front is hidden for several metres. The truck's blind zones are full of people; the car's blind zone is full of tarmac.

The vehicle is long and the nearside is far away. The nearside of the cab is 2.5 m from the driver, the trailer's nearside rear corner is more than 16 m away. Mirrors (or camera monitors) show it, but the driver has six of them to scan while also watching the junction. A cyclist alongside the trailer is in a mirror the driver may not be looking at.

The rear wheels cut inside on a turn. When the tractor turns, the trailer's axle group follows a tighter circle than the tractor's front wheels: in a typical urban right turn the trailer wheels run 2 to 4 m inside the path of the cab (Section 6.4). A cyclist whom the cab passed with a metre to spare is then run over by the trailer. A perception system for turning therefore has to predict the swept path, not just detect the cyclist.

The vehicle articulates. The tractor and trailer are two rigid bodies joined at the kingpin. Every sensor on the tractor that looks backwards or sideways sees the trailer swing through its field of view, and any system that reasons about the trailer (where its end is, what it hides, where its wheels go) needs the articulation angle. Figure 1.2 shows the parts on a real tractor-semitrailer: the trailer's front end rests on the tractor's fifth wheel, directly above the drive axles, and that hidden point is where the combination bends.

Trailers are swapped and carry no sensors. A tractor may pull a different trailer every day, owned by a different company, of a different length, often decades old. Anything mounted on the trailer cannot be assumed to exist, so the regulated systems are built with tractor-mounted sensors that must see along and past a trailer they know little about.

The cab moves on its own suspension. The cab rides on springs and dampers on top of the chassis to give the driver a tolerable ride. It pitches and rolls relative to the chassis by a few degrees under braking, acceleration and on bad roads. A camera on the cab and a radar on the chassis bumper therefore have a relative pose that changes all the time (Section 3.4).

The mounting is high, the environment is dirty. A camera behind the windscreen sits at 2.5 to 3 m, which changes the viewpoint every network was trained on (Section 10.1). Side and rear sensors sit in the spray thrown up by eight to twelve tyres; the front bumper meets the dirt of building sites. Cleaning, heating and blockage detection are not optional.

The vehicle is heavy and stops late. A loaded truck decelerates at roughly half the rate of a car and its air brakes take longer to build pressure. Forward emergency braking needs objects confirmed at longer range, and the regulation accepts speed reduction instead of full avoidance at higher speeds (Chapter 8).

Numbers

EU maximums for a tractor-semitrailer: 16.5 m length, 2.55 m width (2.60 m for refrigerated bodies), 4 m height in most member states, 40 t gross in international traffic. Kingpin to trailer rear at most 12.0 m, kingpin swing radius at most 2.04 m. A rigid city truck is typically 8 to 10 m long with a wheelbase of 4 to 5 m.

Talk track

"On a car, perception mostly answers 'what's ahead and how fast is it coming'. On a truck the deadly cases are slow and sideways: a cyclist on the nearside when we turn right, a pedestrian in front of the cab when we pull away, someone behind us when we reverse. The driver sits two and a half metres up, the trailer swings and cuts the corner, and we can't rely on sensors on the trailer because trailers get swapped. So the truck-specific work is close-range detection with tractor-mounted sensors that understand where the trailer is."

2Direct and indirect vision: the zones a truck driver cannot see

Every close-range perception feature on a truck exists because a zone around the vehicle is invisible or easy to miss. Regulations describe these zones in two ways: indirect vision (what mirrors or camera monitors must show, UN R46) and direct vision (what the driver sees through the glass, UN R167 and London's Direct Vision Standard). Detection systems then fill the gap that remains.

2.1Indirect vision: the R46 mirror classes on a heavy truck

UN R46 defines devices for indirect vision by class, and for each class a field of vision: an area on flat ground, measured from the driver's ocular points, that the mirror or camera monitor must show. The passenger-car view of R46 (classes I to III, the CMS rules and ISO 16505) is covered in Standards and regulations 6.3. A heavy truck uses a different set, and the differences explain the shape of a truck's blind zones.

A heavy truck (N3) must carry a class II main mirror on each side (the large, nearly flat mirror for looking far back), a class IV wide-angle mirror on each side, a class V close-proximity mirror on the nearside and a class VI front mirror. Class III (the small main mirror of cars) is not allowed, and the interior mirror is optional because the trailer fills it. Figure 2.1 draws the fields to scale. Class II must show a 5 m wide strip from 30 m behind the driver's eyes to the horizon, plus a 1 m strip along the vehicle from 4 m behind: it is the "what's coming in the next lane" view. Class IV must show a 15 m wide area from 10 m to 25 m behind, plus a 4.5 m strip from 1.5 m behind: it is the wide view that catches a cyclist alongside the trailer. Class V covers the ground beside the cab on the nearside, 2 m wide from 1.75 m behind to 1 m ahead of the driver's eyes; class VI covers 2 m in front of the cab and 2 m beyond each side. Class V and VI mirrors must be mounted at least 2 m above the road, so a pedestrian cannot walk into them.

Two things in Figure 2.1 are worth noticing. First, the fields are areas on the ground. The legal check is "can the ground area be seen in the mirror", not "will the driver notice a cyclist in it". The mirrors make the zone visible in principle; whether a person in it is seen depends on attention, glance timing and image size, which is exactly the gap a detection system closes. Second, the fields are defined relative to the tractor. When the trailer swings during a turn, it moves through the class II and IV fields and can cover what is behind it (Section 4.2).

Pitfall

"Fully covered by mirrors" is not "no blind spot". Six mirrors legally cover the ground around the cab, but the driver can look at one at a time, the class V and VI images are small and distorted, and a cyclist moves several metres per second. Accident studies of right-turning trucks repeatedly find vehicles with correctly adjusted mirrors. That is why the EU added detection (R151, R159) on top of indirect vision, and why London rates direct vision through the glass.

2.2The blind zones around a tractor-semitrailer

Combining direct and indirect vision gives the zones that perception engineers talk about. The front close zone is the area directly in front of the cab, from the bumper to about 2 to 4 m ahead, where a pedestrian crossing in front of a stationary truck is hidden below the windscreen. It is the moving-off zone of R159. The nearside zone runs along the passenger side from the front corner of the cab to behind the trailer. Near the cab it is hidden from direct view by the door; further back it is visible only in the class IV and II mirrors; during a right turn the trailer swings into it. It is the blind-spot information zone of R151. The rear zone behind the trailer is invisible except through a reversing camera, if the trailer has one; it is the zone of R158. On the driver's side the zones are smaller because the driver can look out of the side window directly, but the area beside the trailer is still mirror-only.

Buses (M3) have a different shape of problem. The driver sits low behind a large windscreen, so direct vision to the front is good, but a city bus is 12 to 18 m long, articulated buses have the same swept-path issues as tractor-trailers, and passengers walk close to the front and nearside at stops. That is why R159 (moving off) applies to buses as well as trucks, while R151 is optional for them.

2.3Direct vision: UN R167 and London's Direct Vision Standard

The most effective way to see a cyclist next to the cab is through a window, without mirrors, screens or sensors. A cab with a low driver position and deep glass (the "low-entry" city cabs, with the driver's eyes around 1.7 m instead of 2.5 m) shows a nearby person directly, in the driver's normal field of attention, with no latency and no failure modes. Figure 1.1's numbers show the effect: lowering the eye and the sill shrinks the hidden ground in front from about 2.5 m to about 1 m, and the standing child is no longer hidden at all.

London's Direct Vision Standard (DVS), run by Transport for London, was the first scheme to rate this. It gives each truck model a star rating from zero to five based on how much of a defined volume around the cab the driver can see directly. Since March 2021 heavy goods vehicles over 12 tonnes need a permit to drive in Greater London. Since 28 October 2024 the permit requires at least three stars, or else the fitting of the Progressive Safe System (PSS): a package of class V and VI mirrors, a camera monitoring system on the nearside, a nearside blind-spot information system that the driver cannot switch off and that shows a fault, a moving-off information system at the front, an audible warning to people outside when the truck turns left (the nearside in left-hand traffic) or reverses, and side under-run protection. The PSS rules on the blind-spot system are deliberately stricter than R151's minimum (no off switch, fault indication, a multimodal warning when a collision is imminent).

UN R167 turns the same idea into a type-approval regulation for M2, M3, N2 and N3 vehicles. It defines assessment volumes around the front and nearside of the cab and requires that a minimum part of them be directly visible from the driver's eye points. It has three levels with decreasing minimum visible volumes (level 1 requires 11.2 m³ in total, level 2 8.0 m³, level 3 7.0 m³); its 01 series has applied since 12 June 2025. Through the EU General Safety Regulation, direct-vision requirements apply to new heavy-vehicle types from 7 January 2026 and to all new heavy vehicles from 7 January 2029. For a perception engineer the consequence is that cab designs are changing (lower driver positions, extra glass in the passenger door), which changes where cameras can sit and what they need to cover, but does not remove the need for detection on long-haul cabs, which remain high.

Interesting detail

R159 measures the truck's front blind spot with a physical test object: a 50 mm diameter cylinder whose lowest edge stands 900 mm above the ground, moved in steps of no more than 150 mm until the driver can see it. The resulting "blind spot boundary" can define how far forward the moving-off zone must reach. A child of roughly that height is the design case.

Talk track

"There are three ways to see a cyclist next to the cab: through the glass, through a mirror or monitor, or with a sensor that tells you. Regulations now ask for all three: R167 and London's DVS rate direct vision, R46 defines the mirror fields, and R151 and R159 add detection. Detection doesn't replace the others; it catches the case where the person was visible in principle and the driver was looking at another mirror."

3Sensor suites and mounting on a truck

A modern heavy truck carries a forward camera and radar like a car, plus a set of sensors that cars do not need: rear-looking camera arms that replace the main mirrors, close-range cameras or radars for the front and nearside, and sometimes cameras on the trailer. Where each sensor can be mounted is constrained by a moving cab, a chassis that carries a trailer, and an environment of spray, dirt and vibration.

3.1The suite, sensor by sensor

Figure 3.1 shows a typical European tractor fitted for current regulations. The forward camera (1) sits high behind the windscreen, inside the wiped area, and does lane, sign and object detection for emergency braking and lane departure warning, exactly as in a car but from a higher viewpoint. The forward radar (2) sits low in the bumper on the chassis and provides range and relative speed for emergency braking and adaptive cruise control. Together they form the forward fusion pair of Chapter 8.

The front close-range sensor (3) watches the moving-off zone directly in front of the bumper. It can be a wide-angle camera, a short-range radar or both; a camera gives classification (is it a person?), a radar gives robust presence detection in rain and darkness. The camera monitor system arms (4) replace the class II and IV mirrors with rear-looking cameras on short arms at the top front corners of the cab and displays on the A-pillars inside (Chapter 4). On the nearside top corner, a class V/VI camera (5) or a wide mirror covers the ground beside and in front of the cab.

The nearside detection sensor (6) is the sensor behind the blind-spot information system: most often a short- or mid-range radar mounted on the side of the tractor behind the front axle, looking sideways and backwards along the trailer; some systems use a downward-looking or rear-looking camera, or both. A rear-of-cab camera (7) looking at the trailer's front face is used by some manufacturers to measure the articulation angle and detect the trailer (Chapter 6). At the trailer rear (8), a reversing camera and ultrasonic sensors are common in distribution fleets but not universal, because the trailer is a separate vehicle. Inside the cab, a driver-monitoring camera (9) watches for distraction and drowsiness, which the EU requires for heavy vehicles as for cars.

Lidar is not part of today's regulated driver-assistance suite on trucks. It appears in the outlook of hub-to-hub autonomous trucking, where driverless tractors run between logistics terminals on motorways; there, long-range lidar is valued because a heavy truck needs to see a stopped vehicle at several hundred metres to stop or change lanes comfortably. The sensor physics is in Automotive sensor technologies 1.2.

In practice

Truck programmes count sensors differently from car programmes. The forward camera and radar are often the same hardware family as the manufacturer's cars or a supplier's car product, re-tuned for height and braking. The close-range and side sensors are truck-specific products, frequently from suppliers who specialise in commercial vehicles, and they are also sold as retrofit kits for fleets that need London DVS permits. Expect several suppliers' sensors on one vehicle and integration work in the gateway between them.

3.2Why the nearside sensor is usually a radar

The blind-spot information system has to detect a cyclist anywhere from the cab's front corner to behind the trailer, at night, in rain and spray, at lateral distances up to about 4.25 m, and track them for several seconds (Chapter 5). A radar on the side of the tractor has three advantages for this. It measures range and radial velocity directly, so a moving cyclist separates cleanly from parked cars and street furniture that are stationary in the world. It works in darkness and through spray that would blind a camera. And its wide field of view (well over 100 degrees horizontally for typical corner radars) covers both the area beside the cab and the long strip along the trailer.

The radar's weakness is classification: a cyclist, a pedestrian and a road sign pole can have similar radar cross-sections, and a radar sees the trailer itself as a large, strong reflector that moves through its field of view whenever the vehicle articulates. Both problems are solved with geometry and context rather than with the radar alone: the trailer region is masked using the articulation angle and trailer width, static objects are suppressed using the vehicle's own speed and the target's measured Doppler, and turn intent comes from the vehicle bus. Camera-based nearside systems have the opposite profile: good classification, weaker in darkness and spray, and the same need to mask the trailer.

Radar measurement principles and their weaknesses: Automotive sensor technologies 3.9.

3.3Cab or chassis: where a sensor can live

A truck has two mounting bodies. The chassis is the ladder frame that carries the axles, the fifth wheel and the bumper; it is rigid relative to the road (apart from tyre and spring deflection) and relative to the trailer coupling. The cab sits on top of the chassis on its own suspension and is tilted forward for engine access during maintenance. Every sensor decision follows from this split.

Sensors that need a stable pose relative to the road and the other chassis sensors, such as the forward radar, belong on the chassis. Sensors that need height or a view through the windscreen, such as the forward camera, the camera monitor arms and the class V/VI camera, belong on the cab. Sensors on the cab must survive the cab being tilted forward in the workshop (cables with enough slack, connectors that are not strained), and their extrinsic calibration is relative to a body that moves. Sensors on the side of the chassis sit low, near the wheels, in the worst spray. And because the chassis of a tractor ends a little behind the rear axle, a tractor has no natural place for a rear-looking sensor that is not blocked by the trailer, which is why reversing coverage belongs to the trailer.

3.4A moving cab: what it does to calibration and range

The cab suspension lets the cab pitch forward under braking, backward under acceleration and roll in corners, relative to the chassis, by a few degrees. A car body also pitches, but the whole car pitches together, cameras and radar alike. On a truck, a camera on the cab and a radar on the chassis move relative to each other, so a fixed camera-to-radar extrinsic calibration is wrong exactly when it matters most, during hard braking (Figure 3.2).

How much does pitch matter? A monocular camera often estimates distance from where an object touches the ground: with mounting height h and the angle α below the horizon at which the contact point appears, the distance is d = h / tan α. A pitch error shifts α directly. The experiment truck_numbers.py shows that a 1° pitch error at a 2.5 m mounting height turns a true 30 m into 38 m and a true 50 m into 77 m. At a car's 1.3 m height the same error is worse (30 m becomes 50 m), because the angle below the horizon is smaller: the truck's height actually helps. But the truck's cab pitches by more than a car body, and it does so relative to the radar it is fused with. The practical answers are the same tools as in cars, used more aggressively: online pitch estimation from lane markings, the horizon or optical flow on the road surface (see Multi-sensor calibration and fusion 3.5), a cab-height or cab-pitch signal where the vehicle has one, and fusion designs that trust the radar for range and the camera for angle and class.

Pitfall

A camera calibrated at the end of the line with the truck unladen sits at a different height and pitch when the truck carries 25 t: air suspension levels the chassis, but tyres compress and the cab settles. A flat-ground distance model fitted on unladen test drives will be biased on laden ones. Validate with laden and unladen vehicles, and estimate pitch online rather than trusting the factory value.

3.5Dirt, spray, vibration and cleaning

A truck's side sensors live in the spray from its own tyres and the tyres of the vehicle it is overtaking. Front close-range sensors sit low on a bumper that drives through building sites. Camera arms at the top of the cab collect road film and, in winter, ice. Vibration is harsher than in a car: stiffer suspension, a diesel engine or a high-torque electric drive and twenty-year service lives.

The design response is a mix of placement, hardware and software. Placement puts lenses where the airflow keeps them cleaner (camera arms with the lens facing backwards are partly self-cleaning) and away from the wheel spray where possible. Hardware adds lens heating against ice and condensation and, increasingly, washer nozzles for the forward camera area and the camera arms. Software must detect a blocked or degraded sensor and say so: R151 and R159 both require the system to switch itself off and show a "temporarily unavailable" indication when sensors are contaminated by ice, snow, mud or dirt, and to recover automatically once they are clean (R151 and R159 test that the system comes back within 60 seconds of driving after the fault is removed). Blockage detection for cameras uses image statistics (low contrast, low edge density, frozen regions) and disagreement with other sensors; for radar it uses the loss of expected ground clutter and the attenuation of known reflections. Figure 3.3 shows what a camera image looks like in these conditions.

Why it matters

On a truck, "the sensor is dirty" is a daily operating condition, not a rare edge case. A system that silently loses performance when dirty is worse than none, because the driver relies on it. The regulations make degraded-mode detection and the driver indication part of the approval.

3.6Online extrinsics, trailer swap and the end-of-line problem

A car is calibrated once at the end of the production line and checked online afterwards. A truck adds three complications. Its cab tilts for service, so the cab sensors' relation to the chassis is restored only approximately each time. It is often finished by a body builder after it leaves the factory (a rigid truck gets its box, crane or tipper elsewhere), which can move or obstruct sensors. And the tractor is coupled to unknown trailers, so any calibration that involves the trailer, such as the position of trailer cameras or the trailer's width for masking radar returns, cannot be done at the factory at all.

The consequence is that truck perception relies more on online calibration and self-checks than a car does: continuous estimation of camera pitch and roll relative to the road, radar alignment from stationary roadside targets while driving (Multi-sensor calibration and fusion 3.4), and, after each coupling, a learning phase for the trailer (presence, width, length, effective wheelbase; Section 6.5). R159 even writes this into the approval: if the system has not completed its calibration after a cumulative 15 seconds of driving, the driver must be informed until it has.

4Camera monitor systems on trucks

A camera monitor system (CMS) replaces the large exterior mirrors with cameras and displays. On a car it is a styling and aerodynamics option; on a truck it changes what the driver can see, because a camera can do things a mirror cannot: follow the trailer end through a turn, brighten the night, and remove the mirror heads that hide pedestrians at the cab's front corners.

4.1Why trucks replace mirrors with cameras

A truck's class II and IV mirrors are large heads on arms at the front corners of the cab. They cost fuel (manufacturers have quoted savings of around one percent from removing them), and they create their own blind spot: the mirror head and its arm hide a pedestrian or a car at the front corners, exactly where the driver looks when turning. A CMS replaces each head with a small camera on a slim arm and shows the image on displays mounted on the inside of the A-pillars, where the driver's eyes go anyway. The first series truck to replace its main mirrors this way was the Mercedes-Benz Actros in 2018; several European manufacturers and retrofit suppliers have followed.

The legal framework is the same as for cars: UN R46 allows a CMS to replace a mirror class if it shows at least the class's field of vision at sufficient magnification and meets the image-quality requirements of ISO 16505, the CMS standard R46 refers to (ISO 16505:2019, with a revision in progress). The car-level details (magnification, resolution, luminance, glare, latency) are in Standards and regulations 6.3. What is different on a truck is the content of the image: a long trailer that moves through it.

A typical truck CMS shows two views per side on one tall display: the narrow, magnified class II view at the top for judging distance and speed of traffic far behind, and the wide class IV view below for the area alongside the trailer. Display guidelines (marker lines showing the trailer end or a distance behind it) help the driver judge gaps when changing lanes, something a mirror cannot do.

4.2Trailer-following panning: interactive

When a tractor-semitrailer turns, the trailer swings relative to the tractor. On the inside of the turn, the trailer's side moves into the mirror's field of view and fills it, and the trailer's rear corner, where the inside rear wheels and any cyclist next to them are, moves out of view to the outside. Drivers of mirror trucks compensate by leaning forward or by using the wide-angle mirror. A camera can compensate itself: the camera has a wider field of view than the displayed class II image, and the displayed crop can be shifted (panned) during the turn so that the trailer's rear end stays at the inner edge of the screen.

The numbers from the model are instructive. Driving straight, the trailer's rear corner appears about 1° inward of straight back, so the trailer side is a thin sliver at the inner edge of the view, as in a correctly adjusted mirror. At −15° the corner has moved 10° outward and the trailer covers about three quarters of the displayed view. At −30°, a normal value in an urban turn, the corner is 22° outward, outside a view that spans only about 16°, and the trailer covers the entire image: the driver sees a white wall. Panning by about 23° brings the corner back to the inner edge. On the outside of a turn the opposite happens: the trailer swings away and its corner disappears behind the cab, which is why trailer-following panning is mainly an inside-of-turn feature.

To pan, the system needs to know the articulation angle or the trailer end's position in the image. Systems use one or more of: a kinematic estimate from steering angle, yaw rate and speed; detection of the trailer's rear edge in the camera image; or a dedicated articulation sensor (Section 6.3). The panning must be smooth and predictable, must never pan away from an approaching vehicle, and must return to the legal default view: the regulated class II field must remain visible whenever R46 requires it, so panning is typically implemented as an extension of the view or as an added view during low-speed turns rather than as a replacement of the default field.

Pitfall

Panning driven purely by a kinematic model fails when the model is wrong: a trailer with a steered axle, an unusual wheelbase or a slippery yard where the trailer slides. The image then pans to the wrong place in exactly the manoeuvre where the driver relies on it. Robust systems confirm the model with the image (the trailer edge is a strong, high-contrast feature) and fall back to the unpanned default view when the two disagree.

4.3Latency, night, glare and weather

A mirror has zero latency, infinite dynamic range from the driver's point of view (the eye adapts), and works without power. A CMS has to approach those properties with a camera, an ISP, a link and a display. Latency: ISO 16505 limits the time from a change in the scene to its display (commonly quoted as at most 200 ms end to end), and good truck systems are well below it, because a driver changing lanes at 85 km/h covers almost 5 m in 200 ms. The latency budget and where the milliseconds go are discussed in Standards and regulations 6.5. Night: a CMS can show more than a mirror at night, because the camera gain and HDR processing brighten the scene; the risk is the opposite, headlights of following vehicles blooming into large white blobs. ISO 16505 and R46 include glare and smear tests for this. LED flicker: pulsed LED headlights and indicators must not appear to flicker or vanish, which requires flicker mitigation in the sensor and ISP. Weather: rain and road film on the lens hurt more than on a mirror, because a single drop on a small lens covers a large part of the image; lens heating, hydrophobic coatings and the backwards-facing lens position matter.

Displays inside the cab add their own constraints: they must be readable in direct sunlight and dim enough at night not to dazzle, positioned close to where the mirror was so that the driver's glance behaviour carries over, and the image must match the expected side (a nearside display on the nearside A-pillar, never mirrored the wrong way).

4.4Failure behaviour

A broken mirror is visible; a frozen camera image is not. A CMS therefore needs to detect its own failures and tell the driver: a black screen is safe in the sense that the driver knows the view is gone, while a frozen or delayed image that looks normal is dangerous. Practical systems monitor frame counters and timestamps end to end (camera to display), check that the image changes when the vehicle moves, watch the display backlight and panel status, and show a clear failure indication when any of these fails. Because the CMS replaces mandatory mirrors, a failed CMS is a failed legal requirement: manufacturers design the two sides to be independent (separate cameras, links, processing paths and power where possible) so that one fault does not blind both sides, and define what the driver should do when a side fails.

"A truck CMS is not just a mirror on a screen. It removes the mirror heads that hide pedestrians at the cab corners, it can pan with the trailer so the driver sees the trailer end during a turn, and it can show more at night. In return it has to meet ISO 16505 for latency, glare and image quality, and it has to detect its own failures, because a frozen image that looks fine is worse than a black screen."

5Detecting cyclists and pedestrians around the truck: BSIS, MOIS and reversing

Three detection functions are specific to heavy vehicles and now mandatory in the EU: the blind-spot information system for cyclists on the nearside when turning (UN R151), the moving-off information system for pedestrians and cyclists in front of a stationary or slow truck (UN R159), and reversing detection (UN R158). All three are information systems first: they tell the driver early, without braking, and escalate to a warning only when a collision becomes likely. The design problem is to inform early enough to help without informing so often that the driver stops listening.

5.1The turning-cyclist crash and what BSIS must do

The classic scenario is a truck approaching a junction slowly, intending to turn right, and a cyclist riding straight on in the cycle lane on the truck's nearside, faster than the truck. The cyclist comes up from behind the trailer, rides alongside it and reaches the cab just as the truck starts to turn. The driver checked the mirrors earlier, when the cyclist was still far behind or hidden behind the trailer, and does not check again. When the truck turns, the cab sweeps across the cycle lane and the trailer cuts even further inside.

UN R151, the Blind Spot Information System for the detection of bicycles, is built around this scenario. It is mandatory for N2 trucks over 8 tonnes and all N3 trucks, and optional for lighter N2 and for buses (M2, M3). Its requirements, in plain words: while the truck drives between standstill and 30 km/h, the system must give an information signal when a bicycle moving at 5 to 20 km/h on the nearside, between 0.9 m and 4.25 m from the truck's side, is in a position where it would collide with the truck if the truck turned. The signal must be given before the last point of information (LPI) and must not be given before the first point of information (FPI). The LPI lies at least 15 m before the theoretical collision point (more at higher truck speeds, growing to 18.61 m at 30 km/h, so that the driver can still stop); the FPI lies 4 seconds of truck travel before the LPI. A cyclist alongside the front wheel at only 0.25 to 0.9 m must also trigger the signal while driving straight. In addition to the information signal, the system must give a warning signal, distinct from the information signal, when a collision becomes likely; the warning may not be triggered by the turn indicator alone.

Numbers

UN R151 (EU OJ L 2020/1596): truck speed range standstill to 30 km/h; bicycle 5 to 20 km/h; lateral distance 0.9 to 4.25 m (0.25 to 0.9 m beside the front wheel); LPI at least 15 m before the collision point (18.61 m at 30 km/h); FPI 4 s of truck travel before the LPI; test truck speeds 10 and 20 km/h; information not required if the bicycle is more than 30 m behind or more than 7 m ahead of the cab's front corner; operation above 15 lux ambient light.

5.2Interactive: speeds decide where the sensor must look

The timing rules have a consequence that is easy to miss: where the cyclist is at the moment the system must speak depends on the speed difference. The interactive figure uses the same straight-line simplification as Figure 5.1. Both road users arrive at the collision point at the same time; we ask where the cyclist is, relative to the truck's front, when the truck passes the FPI and the LPI.

With the truck at 10 km/h and the cyclist at 20 km/h, the cyclist is 26 m behind the cab front at the FPI, which is about 10 m behind the end of a 16.5 m combination, and 15 m behind it at the LPI, alongside the rear of the trailer. With the speeds swapped (truck 20, cyclist 10) the truck is catching up: the cyclist is 19 m ahead of the cab at the FPI and 7.5 m ahead at the LPI. At equal speeds the cyclist rides exactly beside the cab front corner the whole time. One sensor position therefore has to cover from several metres ahead of the cab to beyond the trailer's end along the nearside, about 30 m of road, while the trailer swings through its view. This is the main reason the nearside sensor is a wide-angle radar or a rear-looking camera mounted far forward, and why it needs to know the trailer's position to tell "cyclist beside the trailer" from "trailer".

Interesting detail

The time between the LPI and the collision is not fixed: it is the 15 m divided by the truck speed, 5.4 s at 10 km/h but 2.7 s at 20 km/h and 2.2 s at 30 km/h (where the LPI grows to 18.6 m). The 15 m minimum is roughly the distance a driver needs to react and stop from 25 km/h; above that, R151 lengthens it with the stopping distance. Regulation numbers that look arbitrary usually encode a reaction-plus-braking budget.

5.3Inside a BSIS: from detections to a signal

A blind-spot information system is a small perception and decision pipeline with five stages. Detection produces candidate objects from the nearside sensor: radar detections with range, angle and radial velocity, or camera detections with class and image position. Masking and ego-filtering remove the truck itself: the trailer's side and wheels, the side under-run guard, the tractor's own fuel tank and steps. Radar returns that fall inside the trailer's current footprint, computed from the articulation angle and the learned trailer width and length, are discarded. Tracking turns detections into objects with position and velocity over the ground; a cyclist is a target that moves in the world at 5 to 20 km/h roughly parallel to the truck, while a parked car, a sign post or a hedge is static in the world even though it moves relative to the truck. Tracking over a second or more is what makes this distinction reliable. Conflict assessment asks the question R151 actually asks: would this object collide with the truck if the truck turned now? That requires a prediction of the truck's turning path, including the trailer's swept path (Section 6.4), and of the cyclist's straight path, and a test of whether they overlap in space and time. Signal logic finally maps the conflict to information or warning, with hysteresis so that the lamp does not flicker as a target crosses a zone boundary.

Turn intent is the input that decides between information and warning. The indicator alone is not trusted (drivers forget it, and R151 forbids a warning based solely on it); steering wheel angle, yaw rate, lateral acceleration and the truck's position relative to the junction (if a map or lane perception is available) give a more reliable estimate. Once the truck has started to turn, the time to collision drops quickly, so the warning has to be immediate and unambiguous.

Pitfall

The most common field complaint about blind-spot systems is false information signals from street furniture: bollards, sign posts, railings, parked cars and bins along the kerb as the truck drives slowly along a street. A naive "anything in the zone" logic produces a lamp that is on most of the time in a city, and drivers learn to ignore it. Suppressing static objects needs good ego-motion (wheel speed and yaw rate, including during slow turns), radar Doppler or camera classification, and a requirement that the object moves in the world before it can trigger the signal; but a cyclist waiting at a red light is also static, which is why some systems keep a recently moving track alive and why classification matters.

Talk track

"The blind-spot system has to say 'there's a cyclist who'd be hit if you turned' at least 15 metres before the collision point, but not more than four seconds before that, and stay quiet for parked cars and sign posts. The perception part is tracking a cyclist from behind the trailer to the front of the cab with a sensor that also sees the trailer swing through its view. The decision part is predicting the truck's swept path and the driver's turn intent, and keeping the lamp quiet enough that drivers still trust it."

5.4Moving-off information: the zone in front of the cab

The second classic crash happens at a traffic light or a pedestrian crossing. The truck is stopped; a pedestrian, often a child or an elderly person, or a cyclist who filtered to the front, is directly in front of the cab, below the windscreen line. The light turns green and the truck pulls away. UN R159, the Moving Off Information System, applies to M2, M3, N2 and N3 vehicles and requires an information signal when a pedestrian or cyclist is in the close front zone while the truck is stationary or moving off slowly in a straight line (below 10 km/h).

The zone is defined precisely (Figure 5.3). It starts 0.8 m in front of the vehicle and reaches 3.7 m (or, at the manufacturer's choice, the forwardmost point of the measured blind-spot boundary, but at least 1.0 m). For pedestrians it extends 0.5 m beyond each side of the vehicle; for cyclists it is the width of the vehicle. Test pedestrians are adult and child soft targets according to ISO 19206-2, crossing in front of the stationary truck at 3 to 5 km/h from either side; cyclists are adult soft targets (ISO 19206-4), stationary or moving at up to 10 km/h. The information must be on before the target reaches a defined last point of information and stay on while the person is in the zone, including while the truck is stationary. A separate collision warning, using at least two of optical, acoustic and haptic modes, is required when a collision is imminent.

Perception in front of the cab is different from the nearside. Distances are short (under 4 m), the person may be partly below the camera's field of view, and the scene includes the truck's own bumper and the vehicle in front at a traffic light. A wide-angle camera mounted high on the front looks down at steep angles, so a person appears as a head and shoulders seen from above, a viewpoint that pedestrian detectors trained on car data rarely see (Section 10.1). Radar helps with presence detection but must handle the strong reflections from the vehicle ahead. The decision logic has a special state: potential moving-off, when the truck is stationary, the master switch is on and a forward gear is engaged. That is the moment the information matters most and the moment the driver is looking at the traffic light, not the bumper.

Why it matters

The front close zone is the one area where even a perfect mirror set (class VI) fails in practice: the driver must look down into a small mirror above the windscreen exactly when the light changes. R159 exists because these crashes happen at walking speed, with the truck starting from rest, and are almost always fatal for the pedestrian.

5.5Reversing detection

UN R158 covers devices for reversing motion and applies to M and N categories, trucks included; its car-level description is in Standards and regulations 6.1. A vehicle must provide a means for the driver to detect an object behind it: a rear camera that shows a zone from 0.3 m to 3.5 m behind the vehicle, a mirror arrangement, or a detection system, tested with a cylinder-like object 0.8 m tall and 0.3 m wide. On a tractor-semitrailer the problem is that the zone is behind the trailer, the trailer is a separate vehicle and its approval is separate. In practice distribution fleets fit reversing cameras and ultrasonic or radar sensors to their trailers and connect them through the trailer cable or a wireless link, while long-haul tractors often reverse by mirror and CMS view alone. Ultrasonic sensors suit this job (short range, slow speed, cheap) but are weak at the ranges needed for a fast reverse and on soft or angled targets; see Automotive sensor technologies 5.5.

Reversing an articulated vehicle adds a perception twist: the trailer moves opposite to the steering input and is unstable (it jackknifes if not corrected), so the useful information is not only "something is behind" but "where will the trailer end go". Systems that show a predicted trailer path on the reversing display need the articulation angle and the trailer's wheelbase, the same quantities as the turning functions.

5.6Information, warning and the driver

All three functions share a two-level human-machine interface: an information signal that says "someone is there" early and calmly, and a warning signal that says "act now". The information signal is usually a steady amber lamp near the relevant mirror or monitor (the nearside A-pillar for BSIS, the windscreen area for MOIS) and in R151 it cannot be switched off by the driver; the warning is a different signal, typically flashing light and sound, and R151 and R159 allow the driver to switch it off, after which it must come back at the next start. London's Progressive Safe System removes that freedom for the blind-spot system. The systems also need a failure warning (yellow, distinct from the information signal) and a "temporarily unavailable" indication for blocked sensors or low light (both R151 and R159 allow automatic deactivation below 15 lux ambient light).

The trade-off between the two levels is the core of the product. An information signal that comes early and often gives the driver time but trains them to ignore it; a warning that comes late is trusted but may leave too little time at 40 tonnes. Regulations fix the window for the information (FPI to LPI) and leave the warning strategy to the manufacturer, who must document it. Automatic braking for these close-range cases is not required by R151 or R159; some manufacturers offer turn-assist braking at very low speed as an additional function.

6Trailer perception: detection, articulation angle, length and swept path

A passenger car's shape is a constant in the software. A tractor-semitrailer's shape is a variable: whether a trailer is coupled, how long it is, where its wheels are and at what angle it hangs behind the tractor. Almost every truck-specific perception function needs these values, and the trailer itself usually cannot report them.

6.1Who needs the trailer state, and why

The blind-spot system needs the trailer's footprint to mask the trailer out of its radar or camera data, and its swept path to decide whether a cyclist is in conflict (Chapter 5). The camera monitor system needs the trailer end's position to pan (Section 4.2). Surround view needs the articulation angle to place the trailer cameras' images (Chapter 7). Reversing assistance needs angle and wheelbase to predict where the trailer will go (Section 5.5). Even forward functions care: lane departure warning and lane keeping on a truck must consider whether the trailer, not just the tractor, stays in the lane on a curve.

The quantities are: trailer presence (coupled or not), the articulation angle (also called hitch angle: the angle between the tractor's and the trailer's longitudinal axes, measured at the kingpin), the trailer's effective wheelbase (kingpin to the effective centre of the axle group, which determines how it follows), its length (kingpin to rear end, which determines where its end is) and its width. Presence and angle change during a trip; length, width and wheelbase change only at coupling.

6.2Is a trailer coupled? Detection without trailer sensors

The simplest signal comes from the electrical connection. A modern European trailer with an electronic braking system talks to the tractor over the ISO 11992 link in the ISO 7638 connector, so the tractor knows that a trailer is present and gets some of its data (Section 11.2). Lighting circuits draw current when a trailer is plugged in. Some fifth wheels have a sensor that reports whether the kingpin is locked. None of these is fully reliable on its own (old trailers, adapters, a trailer coupled but not plugged in), so perception adds its own evidence: the rear-of-cab camera sees a large planar surface at a fixed distance behind the cab instead of the empty deck and the road (Figure 1.2, tab 2), the side radars see a long reflector moving with the tractor, the CMS cameras see the trailer side. The kinematic response confirms it: with a trailer coupled, the tractor's yaw response and the side sensors' view change in a characteristic way when the truck turns.

6.3Measuring the articulation angle

There are four families of methods, and production systems combine at least two.

A sensor at the coupling. A rotary or magnetic angle sensor at the fifth wheel measures the kingpin's rotation directly. It is accurate and independent of light and weather, but it needs a compatible fifth wheel or kingpin, is exposed to grease, dirt and impact, and is not standard on most tractors.

Geometry from a rear-of-cab camera. A camera on the back of the cab looks at the trailer's front face from less than a metre away (Figure 6.1). Because the camera is mounted on the tractor, the trailer face appears to rotate and shift in the image as the articulation changes. With the camera's calibration and the kingpin position known, the angle follows from where the face's vertical edges appear, from the face's apparent width (foreshortening), or from matching the current image of the face against a reference image taken while driving straight, then solving for the rotation about the kingpin's vertical axis that explains the difference. Trailer fronts are large, high-contrast and close, which makes this robust in daylight; but every trailer looks different (box, curtain, tanker, flatbed, container chassis without a front wall), and at night, in snow or with a flatbed the face may not be there to see.

Keypoints and learned regression. Instead of hand-designed edges, a network can find keypoints on the trailer (corners of the front face, the landing gear, the wheels seen from a CMS camera) and the angle is solved geometrically from them; or a network regresses the angle directly from the image. Learned methods cope better with the variety of trailers and with partial views, but need training data covering that variety, labelled with true angles (usually from a coupling sensor on the data-collection vehicles), and they need to report their confidence so that a fusion stage can ignore them on a trailer type they have not seen. Some systems use a printed marker on the trailer front, as trailer-assist systems for pickups do, which makes detection trivial but needs the driver or fleet to apply the marker.

A kinematic model. The trailer's motion follows from the tractor's. With the tractor's speed v and yaw rate ω, the fifth-wheel offset a (positive when the coupling is ahead of the tractor's rear axle) and the trailer's effective wheelbase L2, the articulation angle θ evolves as

\[ \dot\theta = \omega - \frac{v\sin\theta + a\,\omega\cos\theta}{L_2}. \]

The first term is the tractor turning away from the trailer; the second is the trailer turning to follow, pulled sideways at the kingpin. The equation needs no camera at all and is smooth and available at every instant. But it drifts with gyro bias, it is wrong when L2 is wrong, and it is wrong when the trailer slides (ice, low speed in a yard with a heavy load, a trailer with a steered axle).

The natural answer is fusion: predict with the model, correct with the camera. The experiment articulation_fusion.py simulates a two-minute urban drive (turns up to about 47° articulation, a roundabout, a lane change) with a camera that measures the angle at 15 Hz with 0.8° noise, 3 % gross outliers and a 6-second dropout from glare, and a model whose trailer wheelbase is 7.7 m when the real tri-axle trailer behaves like 8.3 m. Figure 6.2 shows the errors. The camera alone has a root-mean-square error of 2.1° and spikes of 12° from outliers; the model alone has 1.4° but slow errors of up to 4° during turns because of the wrong wheelbase and gyro bias; a scalar Kalman filter that predicts with the model and updates with the camera, rejecting measurements more than three standard deviations from the prediction, has 0.45° with a maximum of 1.7°, including through the dropout.

Interesting detail

The fused filter without the outlier gate has an RMSE of 0.78°, almost twice that of the gated one: a single 10° outlier pulls a well-tuned filter by several degrees for a second. Gating on the innovation (measurement minus prediction, compared with its expected spread) is cheap and is the difference between a filter that works in the lab and one that works on a trailer with reflective stripes.

In practice

Articulation-angle accuracy requirements depend on the consumer. CMS panning tolerates a few degrees, because the driver sees the image; masking a trailer out of a radar's data at 15 m behind the kingpin needs about 1° (1° is 0.26 m of lateral position at 15 m); surround-view stitching needs better than a degree near the coupling to keep seams aligned.

6.4Swept path and off-tracking: interactive

When a tractor-semitrailer turns, the trailer's wheels do not follow the tractor's wheels; they cut inside. The effect is called low-speed off-tracking, and it is the reason a cyclist beside the trailer is in danger even when the cab passes them safely. In a steady turn the geometry is simple. If the tractor's rear axle centre runs on a circle of radius R1, the coupling (offset a ahead of that axle) runs on a circle of radius \( R_h = \sqrt{R_1^2 + a^2} \), and the trailer axle, which is L2 behind the coupling and moves perpendicular to its radius, runs on a circle of radius

\[ R_2 = \sqrt{R_h^2 - L_2^2} = \sqrt{R_1^2 + a^2 - L_2^2}. \]

The tractor's front axle centre runs on \( R_f = \sqrt{R_1^2 + L_1^2} \). For our running example (L1 = 3.6 m, a = 0.5 m, L2 = 7.7 m), a tight urban turn with R1 = 10 m gives Rf = 10.6 m and R2 = 6.4 m: the trailer axle runs 4.2 m inside the front axle, with a steady articulation of 47°. At R1 = 20 m the off-tracking falls to 1.9 m and the articulation to 21° (truck_numbers.py). A longer trailer wheelbase cuts in more: 8.2 m instead of 7.7 m adds about 0.6 m at R1 = 10 m.

A real 90° junction turn is too short to reach that steady state, so the actual off-tracking is smaller and depends on the whole manoeuvre. The interactive figure simulates the kinematic model through a 90° right turn and traces the paths of the cab's outer front corner, the tractor's inner front wheel and the trailer's inner wheels.

With a 12 m front-axle radius the trailer's inner wheels run 2.4 m inside the tractor's inner front wheel and the articulation peaks at 34°; at 10 m the off-tracking grows to 2.8 m and the peak articulation to 40°; at 20 m it shrinks to 1.6 m. These are the numbers a turning-assist function must predict: a cyclist is in conflict if they are inside the area the combination will sweep in the next few seconds, not merely if they are close to the cab. A good prediction uses the current articulation angle as the starting state, the driver's steering as the input and the learned trailer wheelbase as the parameter, and is updated many times per second as the turn develops.

Pitfall

A turning-conflict check that uses only the tractor's path, or a fixed rectangle along the truck's side, gets both errors: it warns for cyclists outside the cab's path who are no risk once the truck has passed, and it misses cyclists inside the corner whom only the trailer will reach. The trailer path is what kills; it has to be in the prediction.

6.5Trailer length, wheelbase and the end of the trailer

The trailer's effective wheelbase can be learned from the same kinematic equation used for the angle. Rearranged, it says that \( \dot\theta - \omega = -(v\sin\theta + a\,\omega\cos\theta)/L_2 \): during any turn, the measured angle rate, yaw rate and speed give a linear least-squares estimate of \( 1/L_2 \). In the simulated drive this recovers 8.22 m for a trailer whose true effective wheelbase is 8.3 m, after a single trip through town. The effective wheelbase of a multi-axle group is not the distance to its middle axle: the tyres of a tri-axle group scrub sideways in a turn and the group behaves as if its axle were further back, which is why learning it beats reading it from a specification.

The trailer's length (where its rear end is) does not appear in the kinematics; it has to be seen. The CMS and class IV cameras see the trailer's rear edge as a strong vertical edge in the image at a bearing that, combined with the articulation angle, gives the distance from the kingpin. A side radar sees the strong return from the trailer's rear corner and the absence of returns behind it. Ultrasonic or radar sensors at the tractor's rear see the trailer front and give the distance to the coupling. A learning phase after coupling, typically the first few minutes of driving with some turns, fills in length, width and wheelbase and must be repeated after every trailer change, which is detected from the coupling signals of Section 6.2.

Talk track

"The trailer is the part of the vehicle we can't instrument, so we measure it from the tractor. The articulation angle comes from fusing a kinematic model, which knows physics but drifts, with a camera looking at the trailer, which sees the truth but is noisy. The trailer's effective wheelbase falls out of the same model during the first turns, its length from where the cameras see its rear edge. With those, we can mask the trailer out of the radar, pan the camera monitors, stitch the surround view and predict where the trailer wheels will go in a turn."

7Surround view on articulated vehicles

On a car, surround view stitches four fisheye cameras into one bird's-eye image of a rigid body (see Camera, ISP, GPU and display 5.3 and GPU surround view 10.6). On a tractor-semitrailer the vehicle is two bodies whose relative pose changes every frame, the trailer may have no cameras at all, and the useful view is 16.5 m long.

7.1Tractor-only surround view and its limits

Many tractors carry cameras at the front and both sides of the cab (often the CMS and class V/VI cameras doubling as surround cameras) and stitch a view of the tractor's surroundings. It covers the front close zone and the area beside the cab, which is where most low-speed crashes happen. It cannot cover the trailer: the tractor's own rear camera, if present, sees the trailer front, and the area beside and behind the trailer is out of reach of cameras mounted on the cab (Figure 7.1, tab 1). The bird's-eye image therefore ends at the coupling, and the trailer appears as a fixed graphic or a grey area.

7.2Stitching across the coupling

When the trailer has its own cameras (typically one on each side and one at the rear, common on dedicated city-distribution trailers that stay with their tractor), the bird's-eye view becomes two rigid camera rigs that must be joined. Each rig is calibrated to its own body; the joint is the articulation angle. Every frame, the trailer cameras' ground projections are rotated about the kingpin by the current angle before blending with the tractor's (Figure 7.1, tab 2). If the angle is off by 1°, the trailer's ground image is shifted by 0.2 m at 12 m behind the kingpin, and lane markings or kerbs show a visible break at the seam.

The seam geometry also changes with the angle. On the outside of a turn the tractor's side coverage and the trailer's side coverage spread apart and a wedge near the coupling is seen by neither; on the inside they overlap and the same ground is seen twice from different positions, which causes ghosting of raised objects unless the blending region is placed carefully. A practical system recomputes the seam positions from the angle each frame, prefers the camera that sees a region more directly, and shows the uncovered wedge honestly (grey) rather than smearing pixels into it.

Calibration is the harder operational problem. The trailer cameras' extrinsics relative to the trailer are fixed when they are installed, but their relation to the tractor is known only through the articulation angle and the kingpin position, and the trailer may be coupled to a different tractor tomorrow. Systems therefore refine the trailer-to-tractor transform online from the overlap regions (ground texture and lane markings seen by both rigs while driving straight), which doubles as a check on the articulation estimate.

In practice

Video from trailer cameras has to cross the coupling. Options are dedicated video couplings, spare lines in the 15-pole ISO 12098 trailer connector carrying analogue or low-rate digital video, or a wireless link with its latency and interference. Bandwidth and connector reliability usually limit trailer cameras to lower resolution than the tractor's, and the processing stays on the tractor.

8Forward perception for heavy-vehicle AEBS and LDW

Forward emergency braking and lane departure warning on a truck use the same kind of camera and radar as on a car, and have been mandatory on new heavy vehicles in the EU for more than a decade. What changes is the physics: a 40-tonne vehicle needs much more distance to stop, sees the road from higher up, and its regulation accepts mitigation where a car's demands avoidance.

8.1Why a truck needs to see further

Stopping distance has two parts: the distance travelled before the brakes act and the braking distance. Both are worse for a loaded truck. Air brakes take a few tenths of a second to build pressure along the pipes, compared with less than 0.2 s for a car's hydraulic brakes with a pre-filled system, and a laden truck's full braking deceleration is about 5 to 6 m/s² compared with 9 to 10 m/s² for a car on dry asphalt. On top comes the warning phase: an emergency braking system on a truck warns the driver before it brakes, because a 40-tonne automatic brake application is violent and the driver often has a better option.

With the assumptions in Figure 8.1 (0.45 s brake build-up and 5 m/s² for the loaded truck), the truck needs 59 m to stop from 80 km/h once braking starts, against 32 m for the car, and 93 m including a 1.5 s warning phase. The perception system must have detected, classified and tracked the obstacle with enough confidence to warn before that: in practice a stationary vehicle has to be confirmed at well over 100 m on a motorway. This is where radar matters most, and where the hardest radar problem, deciding whether a strong stationary return is a stopped vehicle in the lane or a bridge, a gantry or a can on the road, becomes the dominant false-braking risk. The truck's higher camera helps the camera side of that decision: from 2.6 m height an object at 100 m appears at a steeper angle below the horizon, so road surface and object separate better, and the camera can see over the car ahead to the traffic beyond it.

8.2UN R131 and what the 02 series asks of perception

Advanced emergency braking for heavy vehicles is regulated by UN R131 (cars and vans have their own UN R152, see Standards and regulations 7.3). R131 has applied to M2, M3, N2 and N3 vehicles in the EU since the early 2010s. Its 02 series, in force since 24 September 2023 (published in the EU Official Journal as 2025/1329), raises the bar in two ways that matter for perception. First, it adds pedestrians: the system must react to a child-sized pedestrian target (ISO 19206-2) crossing the vehicle's path at 5 km/h, with the function active from 20 to 60 km/h. Second, it tightens the vehicle-target scenarios, tested with standardised soft vehicle targets (ISO 19206-3), against stationary and moving vehicles.

The expected performance reflects heavy-vehicle physics: for the heaviest N3 trucks at 80 km/h approaching a stationary target, R131 requires a substantial speed reduction rather than avoidance of the collision; the required reductions are set per vehicle category and test case. Like R152, it includes a warning phase before emergency braking, false-reaction tests (the system must not brake for objects that are not in the path) and driver-override provisions. The test methods themselves (soft targets on low platforms, defined overlaps and speeds) are described for cars in Standards and regulations 8.2 and carry over.

Pitfall

A child pedestrian at 30 m seen from a camera at 2.6 m height is a small target seen from above, partly hidden by the truck's own bonnet line at close range, and much further away in pixels than the same child seen from a car. A pedestrian detector carried over from a car programme without retraining on truck viewpoints will lose recall precisely in the new R131 02 scenarios.

8.3Lane departure warning and lane keeping on a truck

Lane departure warning is regulated for heavy vehicles by UN R130. The system must be active above 60 km/h and must warn at the latest when the outside of the front tyre nearest the marking has crossed a line 0.3 m beyond the outer edge of the marking, in tests at 65 km/h with lateral drift speeds between 0.1 and 0.8 m/s; it may suppress the warning when the driver signals an intended lane change. Perception-wise it is the same lane detection as on a car, from a higher viewpoint that sees the markings further ahead at steeper angles (helpful) and with a vehicle that fills most of a 3.5 m lane (2.55 m wide, leaving less than half a metre on each side, so small lateral errors matter).

Two truck-specific details matter. On curves, the trailer off-tracks inward even at motorway speed (slightly, because radii are large), so the question "is the vehicle in the lane" concerns the trailer's inner wheels as well as the tractor's. And drowsiness and distraction detection on long-haul trucks often uses lane-keeping behaviour together with the driver-monitoring camera, because long, monotonous night drives are the typical risk.

Talk track

"Forward AEBS on a truck is the same camera-plus-radar idea as on a car, with harder physics: half the deceleration, slower air brakes and a warning phase before braking, so we need confirmed tracks much further out, well over 100 metres on the motorway. The R131 02 series adds child pedestrians crossing at 5 km/h between 20 and 60 km/h, which is a detection problem from a 2.6-metre viewpoint that car-trained networks don't handle well. And at 80 km/h the regulation asks a heavy truck to reduce speed substantially, not necessarily to avoid the crash, because that's what the physics allows."

9Regulations and ratings for truck detection

For passenger cars, regulations set the floor and Euro NCAP pulls above it. For heavy trucks, regulations do most of the work: the EU General Safety Regulation made blind-spot, moving-off and reversing detection, emergency braking, lane departure warning and now direct vision mandatory, London added a city-level permit scheme, and Euro NCAP started rating trucks only in 2024. Knowing which rule covers which zone is half of understanding the system requirements.

9.1How the pieces fit

The structure is the same as for cars (Standards and regulations 1.1 and 7.1). UN regulations under the 1958 Agreement define the technical requirements and tests for one system each. The EU General Safety Regulation (EU) 2019/2144 makes a list of them mandatory for EU type approval, with dates per vehicle category: first for new vehicle types, about two years later for all newly registered vehicles. Standards such as ISO 16505 (camera monitors) and ISO 19206 (test targets) are referenced by the regulations for test methods. On top of the EU floor sit London's Direct Vision Standard (a permit to drive in the city) and Euro NCAP Truck Safe (a voluntary consumer rating that fleets use in purchasing). Pitfall

UN R166 is sometimes cited as "the front and side VRU rule for trucks". It is not: it covers M1 and N1 vehicles (cars and vans). For trucks and buses the corresponding rules are R151 (nearside) and R159 (front). Quoting the wrong rule to a truck customer is a quick way to lose credibility.

9.3London's Direct Vision Standard and the Progressive Safe System

The DVS (Section 2.3) works differently from type approval: it applies to every heavy goods vehicle over 12 tonnes entering Greater London, old or new, through a permit. Since 28 October 2024 a permit requires a direct-vision rating of at least three stars or the Progressive Safe System. For perception suppliers this created a large retrofit market: a fleet with older, one-star trucks must fit a camera monitoring system on the nearside, a blind-spot information system that the driver cannot switch off and that indicates faults, a moving-off information system at the front, audible warnings for manoeuvres, mirrors and side guards. The DVS's tighter blind-spot requirements (no off switch, fault indication, a multimodal warning) show where regulators are heading when systems are accused of being switched off by annoyed drivers.

9.4Euro NCAP Truck Safe

Euro NCAP published its first truck ratings on 22 November 2024, for nine long-haul tractors. Truck Safe is organised in three stages: Safe Driving (including direct and indirect vision, driver monitoring and the human-machine interface), Crash Avoidance (emergency braking against vehicles and VRUs, blind-spot and moving-off performance, lane support) and Post Crash (rescue information and emergency call). It tests the systems against scenarios beyond the regulatory minimum, for example more turning-cyclist geometries and pedestrians, and it also rates how well the driver can see directly and through mirrors or CMS. A separate CitySafe label recognises trucks suited to urban use. For a perception team, Truck Safe plays the role NCAP has played for cars since the 2010s: the regulation decides whether you can sell the truck, the rating decides whether a large fleet buyer will choose it.

9.5The United States

The US picture is different in three ways. Mirrors are required: FMVSS 111 and the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations (49 CFR 393.80) require rear-vision mirrors on trucks, so a camera monitor system can only replace them through an exemption. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration has granted such exemptions to specific products, starting with Stoneridge's MirrorEye in 2019; Safe Fleet's MirrorLESS system received one for 30 July 2024 to 25 July 2029. Second, there is no US equivalent of R151 or R159; blind-spot and close-range detection on US trucks is a voluntary or fleet-driven feature. Third, heavy-vehicle emergency braking is still in rulemaking: NHTSA and FMCSA proposed an AEB requirement for heavy vehicles in July 2023, no final rule has been issued, and a supplemental proposal is on the 2026 regulatory agenda. US trucks also differ physically: conventional (long-bonnet) tractors are common, which moves the driver back and changes the front blind zone, and 53-foot trailers are longer than European ones.

10Data and validation for truck perception

Truck perception is validated in the same framework as car perception (scenarios, test targets, SOTIF analysis, large-scale data), but the viewpoint, the vehicle's size and the regulated close-range scenarios each need their own data and their own test setups.

10.1The viewpoint domain gap

Almost every public driving dataset and most car-programme data are recorded from cameras about 1.2 to 1.6 m above the road. A truck's forward camera sits at 2.5 to 3 m, its front close-range camera looks steeply down, and its side cameras look along a trailer. The same scene looks different. A pedestrian's feet at 10 m appear 7.4° below the horizon from a car camera at 1.3 m and 14.6° below from a truck camera at 2.6 m; at 5 m the angles are 14.6° and 27.5° (truck_numbers.py). People and cars are seen more from above, their tops (heads, roofs) take up more of their image area, occlusion patterns change (the truck sees over the car ahead), and the road surface occupies more of the image. Networks trained on car data still detect cars and people from a truck, but with lower recall at the extremes (close, steep, partly occluded) and with biased distance estimates if any part of the model learned the car's camera height.

The remedies are the usual ones for domain shift (Training neural networks 9.2), applied deliberately: collect and label data from truck viewpoints, especially for the close-range cameras whose views have no car equivalent; fine-tune car-pretrained networks on it rather than training from scratch; augment with geometric re-projection from car data to a higher virtual camera where depth is available; and evaluate per viewpoint and per distance band, not only in aggregate. Classes matter too: trucks see many cyclists and pedestrians at very close range, many trailers, loading equipment and other trucks, and fewer of some classes that dominate car datasets.

10.2Test targets and track scenarios

Regulatory and NCAP tests use standardised soft targets that are safe to hit and that look like the real thing to cameras and radars: ISO 19206-2 for pedestrians (adult and child), ISO 19206-3 for passenger-car targets and ISO 19206-4 for bicycles with riders. They are moved on low, flat platforms that a vehicle can drive over, or on guided rigs, and their radar cross-section and appearance are specified so that a system cannot pass the test by recognising the dummy.

Truck tests stress this infrastructure. An R151 test needs the truck and a bicycle target moving at different speeds on parallel paths, so that both arrive at a virtual collision point at the same time, with the truck about 16 m long and the target passing along its whole side; the timing of the information signal is checked against the vehicle's position at the FPI and LPI lines with tight tolerances. R159's static crossing tests need a target crossing 0.8 m to 3.7 m in front of a stationary cab, where a guided platform must not touch the vehicle. The false-positive side is tested too: R151 drives the truck past a traffic sign with a stationary bicycle dummy, and the manufacturer must show by documentation or simulation that parked cars and cones do not trigger the system. Because the test catalogue is small compared with the variety of real junctions, manufacturers complement it with simulation of turning geometries, trailer types and speeds.

10.3Beyond the regulatory tests

Passing R151 on a test track says little about how often the lamp comes on for nothing in a real city, which is what decides whether drivers keep trusting it. The useful field metrics are rates: false information signals per hour of urban driving, missed conflicts in replayed real-world near-miss scenarios, availability (the share of time the system is not "temporarily unavailable" because of dirt or low light), and the distribution of warning timing relative to the regulatory window. Triggering-condition analysis in the SOTIF sense (Functional safety 8.3) gets truck-specific entries: spray from the truck's own wheels, low sun along the trailer side, unusual trailers (flatbeds, tankers, car transporters, trailers with reflective stripes), large articulation angles, cyclists waiting at a red light (static VRU), pedestrians leaning against the cab, and loading docks full of static clutter. Ground truth for articulation and trailer geometry on test vehicles comes from coupling angle sensors and measured trailers, which is one more reason truck test fleets carry instrumentation that production trucks do not.

In practice

A truck validation plan typically has three layers: the regulatory and NCAP track tests (pass or fail, with soft targets), a scenario library replayed in simulation with many trailer types and junction geometries, and a field programme with a fleet of instrumented trucks in several cities measuring false-signal rates and availability over months. The third layer is where most product changes come from.

11Vehicle context: kinematics, signals and the 24 V net

Perception on a truck consumes vehicle data that a car program would take for granted from its own bus: speed, yaw rate, steering, gear, indicator, and on a truck also trailer data. This short chapter names the sources so that the rest of the chapter is grounded; the networks and power systems themselves belong to other chapters.

11.1Articulated kinematics in one paragraph

The kinematic bicycle model of a car (Planning and control 9.1) extends to a tractor-semitrailer by adding one state, the articulation angle, with the equation of Section 6.3. Three consequences matter for perception. Low-speed off-tracking (Section 6.4): the trailer cuts inside, more for longer trailer wheelbases and tighter turns. High-speed behaviour is the opposite: at speed, tyre slip makes the trailer track slightly outward, and sudden steering can make it swing or, under braking on a slippery road, jackknife; perception does not control this, but a large, fast-changing articulation angle at speed is a useful plausibility signal. Reversing is unstable: the trailer's angle grows unless the driver keeps correcting, which is why reversing aids need the angle and a trailer-path prediction.

11.2Where the signals come from: J1939 and ISO 11992

Heavy vehicles use SAE J1939, a higher-layer protocol on classic CAN (see Automotive networks 4), with standardised parameter groups. A perception ECU typically reads the wheel-based vehicle speed (in the parameter group CCVS1, PGN 65265), steering wheel angle, yaw rate and lateral acceleration (VDC2, PGN 61449), the current gear including reverse (ETC2, PGN 61445), the direction indicators and the brake state, and it publishes its own information and warning states for the instrument cluster and the brake system. Between tractor and trailer, ISO 11992 defines a separate point-to-point CAN link through the ISO 7638 connector, primarily for the trailer's electronic braking system: it tells the tractor that a trailer is connected and reports trailer braking and running-gear data such as axle loads, which perception can use for trailer presence and laden state (Figure 11.1).

11.3The 24 V supply

Heavy vehicles run on a 24 V electrical system (two 12 V batteries in series). Cameras, radars and perception ECUs designed for 12 V cars cannot simply be carried over: their power input must accept the 24 V system's normal range and its transients (load dump when a battery connection is lost while the alternator charges, jump-starts with higher voltages, long cable runs to the rear of a 16 m vehicle), and power-over-coax camera supplies must be generated from it. This is usually solved once in the ECU's power front end; it matters to perception engineers mainly because it can rule out a car-qualified component. Details: Automotive networks 12.1.

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

What is different about perception on a heavy truck compared with a passenger car?Basic

The forward stack is similar, but the critical cases are close-range and sideways: a high driver position creates blind zones full of people (front of the cab, nearside), the vehicle articulates and the trailer cuts inside on turns, trailers are swapped and carry no reliable sensors, the cab moves on its own suspension relative to the chassis, the mounting is high (viewpoint shift) and dirty, and the vehicle stops much later. The regulated truck-specific functions (R151, R159, R158) are close-range VRU detection with tractor-mounted sensors that must understand the trailer.

Explain UN R151 in one minute.Basic

R151 is the blind-spot information system for bicycles, mandatory for N2 trucks over 8 t and all N3. While the truck drives at up to 30 km/h, it must inform the driver when a bicycle at 5 to 20 km/h, 0.9 to 4.25 m to the nearside, would collide with the truck if it turned. The information must come before the last point of information, at least 15 m before the collision point, and not before the first point of information, 4 s of travel earlier. It also needs a separate warning when a collision is likely, must not react to a traffic sign, must suppress parked cars and cones, and deactivates with a fault indication when sensors are dirty or light is below 15 lux.

What does UN R159 require and why does it exist?Basic

R159, the moving-off information system, applies to buses and trucks (M2, M3, N2, N3). When the vehicle is stationary or moving off below 10 km/h, it must inform the driver of a pedestrian or cyclist in the zone 0.8 to 3.7 m in front of the vehicle (0.5 m beyond the sides for pedestrians), tested with child and adult targets crossing at 3 to 5 km/h. It exists because a person directly in front of a high cab is invisible through the windscreen and easy to miss in the front mirror when the light turns green.

What are the mirror classes on a heavy truck and what does each show?Basic

Class II main mirrors on both sides show a 5 m wide strip from 30 m behind the driver's eyes to the horizon plus a 1 m strip from 4 m behind; class IV wide-angle mirrors show 15 m wide from 10 to 25 m behind plus a 4.5 m strip from 1.5 m; the class V close-proximity mirror on the nearside shows 2 m beside the cab; the class VI front mirror shows 2 m in front and 2 m beyond each side. V and VI must be at least 2 m above the road. A CMS may replace them if it meets R46 and ISO 16505.

Why is the nearside blind-spot sensor usually a radar?Intermediate

It must cover from ahead of the cab to behind the trailer, at night, in spray, and separate a moving cyclist from static street furniture. A wide-angle radar measures range and radial velocity directly, works in darkness and spray, and covers a long strip with one sensor. Its weaknesses, poor classification and seeing the trailer itself, are handled with masking from the articulation angle, ego-motion compensation, Doppler and tracking, or by fusing with a camera.

How would you estimate the articulation angle?Intermediate

Fuse two kinds of information. A kinematic model predicts the angle from speed, yaw rate, the fifth-wheel offset and the trailer's effective wheelbase; it is smooth but drifts with gyro bias and is wrong if the wheelbase is wrong or the trailer slides. A measurement from a rear-of-cab camera (edges of the trailer face, keypoints or a learned regression), a CMS camera or a coupling sensor is direct but noisy, has outliers and drops out. A Kalman filter predicting with the model and updating with gated camera measurements gave 0.45° RMSE in our simulation against 2.1° for the camera and 1.4° for the model alone.

What is off-tracking and why does it matter for perception?Intermediate

In a low-speed turn the trailer's axle group follows a tighter circle than the tractor's front wheels. In steady state \( R_2 = \sqrt{R_1^2 + a^2 - L_2^2} \): for our example the trailer runs 4.2 m inside the front axle at a 10 m rear-axle radius. A turning-conflict check must therefore predict the swept path of the trailer, not only the cab; a cyclist the cab passes safely can be inside the trailer's path.

What is trailer-following panning in a CMS?Intermediate

On the inside of a turn the trailer swings into the class II view and fills it, while its rear corner leaves the view. The CMS camera has a wider field than the displayed crop, so the crop can be shifted during the turn to keep the trailer end at the inner edge. In our model the corner is 22° outward at −30° articulation while the view spans only about 16°; panning by about 23° restores it. It needs an articulation estimate or trailer-edge detection, must be smooth and must not remove the legally required default field.

Why does a truck AEBS need a longer detection range than a car's?Intermediate

Half the deceleration (about 5 m/s² loaded versus 9 for a car), slower air-brake build-up and a warning phase before braking. From 80 km/h the truck needs about 59 m to stop once braking starts and over 90 m including a 1.5 s warning, versus about 32 m for the car's braking. The obstacle must be confirmed before the warning starts, so well over 100 m on a motorway, which makes stationary-target classification (stopped vehicle versus bridge or gantry) the key radar and fusion problem.

What changed in R131 with the 02 series?Intermediate

The 02 series (in force 24 September 2023) adds pedestrian AEB for heavy vehicles: a child target according to ISO 19206-2 crossing at 5 km/h, with the function active from 20 to 60 km/h, and uses ISO 19206-3 vehicle targets. For the heaviest trucks at 80 km/h against a stationary target it requires a speed reduction rather than full avoidance.

How does a moving cab affect sensor fusion?Advanced

The cab pitches and rolls on its own suspension relative to the chassis, so a camera on the cab and a radar on the chassis have a time-varying extrinsic. A fixed camera-to-radar calibration is wrong during hard braking, exactly when AEB works. Flat-ground camera range is also sensitive to pitch (1° turns 50 m into 77 m at 2.5 m height). Remedies: online pitch estimation, a cab-pitch or height signal where available, and fusion that takes range from the radar and angle and class from the camera, with association gates that tolerate the cab motion.

How do you keep a blind-spot system from crying wolf?Advanced

Mask the truck's own trailer using articulation and learned trailer geometry; compensate ego-motion so that static objects are recognised as static in the world; require world motion or a cyclist classification before informing, with care for cyclists waiting at a red light; assess conflict with the predicted swept path rather than a fixed rectangle; add hysteresis to the signal; and measure false information rates per hour in real city driving, because the regulation only tests one traffic sign and asks for documentation on parked cars and cones.

How would you validate a truck BSIS beyond the R151 track tests?Advanced

Three layers: the R151 and NCAP track tests with ISO 19206 bicycle targets; a simulation scenario library covering junction geometries, speeds, trailer types and articulation; and a fleet field programme measuring false-signal rate per urban hour, availability under dirt and darkness, and warning timing on replayed near-miss scenarios. Triggering-condition analysis adds spray, low sun along the trailer, unusual trailers, static cyclists and dense kerbside clutter.

What is the viewpoint domain gap and how do you close it?Advanced

Datasets are recorded from about 1.3 m; truck cameras sit at 2.5 to 3 m or look steeply down. Objects are seen more from above (feet at 10 m are 14.6° below the horizon instead of 7.4°), occlusion and scale distributions change, and close-range views have no car equivalent. Close it with truck-viewpoint data, fine-tuning car-pretrained models, geometric re-projection augmentation where depth is known, and evaluation by viewpoint and distance band.

Which regulations apply to which zone around a truck?Basic

Front close zone: R159 (moving off), R46 class VI, R167 direct vision. Nearside: R151 (turning cyclist), R46 class V, IV, II. Rear: R158 (reversing). Rear-view along both sides: R46 class II and IV, mirror or CMS under ISO 16505. Forward: R131 (AEBS) and R130 (LDWS). The EU GSR makes them mandatory with dates; London's DVS and Euro NCAP Truck Safe add requirements and ratings on top.

How does the US differ from the EU for truck camera systems?Basic

Mirrors are required by FMVSS 111 and 49 CFR 393.80, so a CMS needs an FMCSA exemption (granted to products such as MirrorEye in 2019 and MirrorLESS for 2024 to 2029). There is no equivalent of R151 or R159. Heavy-vehicle AEB was proposed in July 2023 and has no final rule yet.

How does surround view work on a tractor-semitrailer?Advanced

Two rigid camera rigs, tractor and trailer, each calibrated to its own body, joined by the articulation angle at the kingpin. Each frame the trailer cameras' ground projections are rotated about the kingpin by the current angle; seams are recomputed because a gap opens on the outside of the turn and an overlap on the inside. The trailer-to-tractor transform is refined online from overlapping ground features. Without trailer cameras the view ends at the coupling.

What is London's Progressive Safe System?Basic

The package a heavy goods vehicle over 12 t needs for a London permit if its direct-vision rating is below three stars (since 28 October 2024): class V and VI mirrors, a nearside camera monitoring system, a blind-spot information system without a driver off switch and with fault indication and a multimodal warning, a moving-off information system at the front, audible manoeuvring warnings and side guards.

Q.2Why and what if

Why can a car's driver see a child in front of the bonnet but a truck driver cannot, even though the car hides more ground?Why

Because what matters is the sight line at the height of the person, not at the ground. The car driver's eye is at about the child's head height, so the head is visible at any distance even though the ground is hidden for 9 m. The truck driver's eye is 2.5 m up and the window sill near 2 m, so the sight line over the sill reaches 1.1 m height only about 0.9 m in front of the cab: everything closer, including the child, is hidden.

Why does R151 forbid the information signal before the first point of information?Why

An early signal is a frequent signal: most cyclists far behind will never be in conflict, and a lamp that is on most of the time is ignored. Limiting it to 4 s before the last point keeps the information tied to a real, imminent decision (turning), which is what keeps drivers trusting it.

What if the trailer is longer than the system assumed?What if

The trailer mask is too short, so radar returns from the trailer's rear part look like objects alongside and can trigger false information; the predicted swept path is too short, so conflicts near the trailer end are underestimated; CMS guide lines and surround view place the trailer end wrongly. That is why trailer length and wheelbase are learned after each coupling, and why the learning phase is detected and the driver informed until it is complete.

What if the camera that measures the articulation angle is blinded for several seconds?What if

The fused estimate coasts on the kinematic model, which needs only speed, yaw rate and the learned wheelbase. In the simulation the error during a 6 s dropout stayed below 1.7°. The filter's covariance grows during the dropout, so consumers that need high accuracy (radar masking, stitching) should widen their margins or degrade, and the first camera measurements after the dropout must pass the innovation gate before being trusted.

Why is low-speed off-tracking larger for a longer trailer wheelbase?Why

The trailer axle moves perpendicular to its radius, at distance L2 behind the coupling, so its radius is \( \sqrt{R_h^2 - L_2^2} \): the longer L2, the smaller the radius. Physically, a longer trailer is pulled further across the inside of the corner before its axle starts to follow the coupling's circle.

What if a fleet switches off the blind-spot warning because drivers find it annoying?What if

R151 allows the warning signal to be switched off (it returns at the next start) but not the information signal. London's PSS forbids switching off the blind-spot system altogether. The real fix is the false-signal rate: if drivers switch it off, the system's suppression of static objects and its conflict prediction are not good enough for urban use.

Why does a higher camera reduce, not increase, the range error from a given pitch error?Why

Flat-ground range is \( d = h/\tan\alpha \), and its sensitivity to the angle is \( |{\rm d}d/{\rm d}\alpha| = (h^2 + d^2)/h \), which for distances much larger than the height is about \( d^2/h \): doubling the height halves the error. The truck's problem is not the height but that its cab pitches more, and relative to the chassis sensors.

What if the truck reverses with a trailer that has no reversing camera?What if

The rear zone behind the trailer is covered only by the CMS and mirrors along the sides, which do not see directly behind. Tractor-mounted sensors cannot see past the trailer. Options are trailer-mounted retrofit cameras or sensors with a link across the coupling, banksman procedures in depots, or reversing-path guidance from the articulation angle that at least shows where the trailer will go. R158 approval concerns each vehicle separately, which is exactly why this gap exists in mixed fleets.

Why do the truck VRU regulations require information, not automatic braking?Why

At turning and moving-off speeds the driver can usually avoid the crash by simply not turning or not pulling away, if they know. Information is cheap, has a low cost of being wrong and keeps the driver responsible; automatic braking of a 40 t vehicle on a false detection in a junction is itself a hazard. Regulators started with information and warning; braking assistants are offered as additional features.

What if the cyclist is waiting at a red light beside the cab?What if

The cyclist is static in the world, like a bollard, so a system that requires world motion to trigger will stay silent, and the cyclist will start riding exactly when the truck starts turning. Systems handle this by keeping recently moving tracks alive, by classifying cyclists with a camera, and by re-evaluating quickly as soon as the target or the truck starts to move; R151's close-range requirement (0.25 to 0.9 m beside the front wheel) targets this area.

Why does the R151 last point of information move further out above 25 km/h?Why

The 15 m minimum is roughly what a driver needs to react and stop at low speed. Above 25 km/h the stopping distance exceeds 15 m, so the regulation uses the stopping distance (18.61 m at 30 km/h) to keep the information useful.

What if a camera-only articulation estimator sees a flatbed trailer for the first time?What if

There is no front face to see; edge or face-based methods fail and a learned regressor may return a confident wrong answer. A robust system reports low confidence for unknown trailer types, falls back to the kinematic model and other cues (CMS view of the trailer side, landing gear, wheels), and the fusion gate rejects implausible measurements. This is a typical triggering condition to list in the SOTIF analysis.

Why can a CMS show more at night than a mirror, and what is the risk?Why

The camera's gain and HDR processing raise dark regions beyond what the dark-adapted eye sees in a mirror. The risk is glare: following vehicles' headlights bloom into large white areas that hide what is next to them, and pulsed LED lights can flicker or vanish without flicker mitigation. ISO 16505 and R46 test glare and smear for this reason.

What if your AEBS camera network was trained only on car data?What if

It will mostly work for vehicles at mid range, but lose recall for close and steeply viewed pedestrians, for children seen from above and for objects partly hidden by the bonnet line, exactly the R131 02 pedestrian scenarios, and any distance cue it learned from the car's camera height will be biased. Retrain or fine-tune with truck-viewpoint data and evaluate per distance band.
