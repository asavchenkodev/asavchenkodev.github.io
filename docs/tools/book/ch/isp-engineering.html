ISP Engineering: Algorithms, Tuning, Lab and Testing

A tutorial for a new image signal processor engineer. It explains what the job is and what you deliver; how to characterise a sensor; every ISP block in enough depth to tune it, with measured before/after results; the tuning workflow step by step; how tuning is done on Qualcomm (Spectra, Chromatix) and TI (VPAC/VISS, DCC) platforms and with the open libcamera tool; the lab, charts and instruments you need; the IQ test procedures and standards; and how tuning is validated and debugged. The running example is an automotive front camera with a 12-bit HDR-capable sensor that feeds both a driver display and a perception network. Every number comes from a small physically based camera simulator and real tools run on its output.

1The job: what an ISP engineer does

An image sensor delivers noisy, colour-blind, non-uniform measurements of light. The ISP turns them into images, but only if several thousand parameters are set to match this sensor, this lens, this platform and this customer. Finding those parameters, proving that they are good and keeping them good through the project is the ISP engineer's job.

1.1Tuning is turning a generic pipeline into a specific camera

The camera pipeline chapter walks once along the chain from photon to perception and shows the ISP as a stage table: decompanding, black level, defect correction, HDR merge, lens shading, white balance, demosaic, colour matrix, tone mapping, noise reduction, sharpening (Camera pipeline 10.2). That table describes what the silicon can do. It does not say how strongly any block should act, and the right answer is different for every camera. A sensor with 2 µm pixels at gain 16 needs far more noise reduction than the same sensor in daylight; a wide-angle lens loses half its light in the corners and a narrow one only a fifth; a fluorescent tube in a car park is greener than the daylight the colour matrix was fitted for. The ISP hardware exposes all of these decisions as registers, lookup tables and per-block parameters, and the vendor's software organises them into a tuning file that the driver loads at start-up and switches between at run time.

An ISP engineer therefore works on two kinds of numbers. Calibration numbers are measured: the black level is whatever the sensor outputs in the dark, the lens shading table is whatever flattens a uniform light source, and the white point of daylight is wherever a grey card lands under D65. There is a right answer and the job is to measure it carefully. Tuning numbers are chosen: how much noise to remove at gain 8, how much to sharpen, how strongly to brighten shadows. There is no single right answer, only a trade-off between noise, detail, artifacts and stability that the customer's requirements and the downstream consumers decide. Most of the craft is in knowing which kind of number you are looking at, measuring the first kind properly before touching the second, and being able to explain every trade-off you made.

Why it matters

The same sensor and SoC can produce a camera that a perception team trusts or one that loses pedestrians at dusk. Hardware sets the ceiling; tuning decides how close you get to it. In automotive programmes the tuning file is a released, versioned software artifact that goes through the same change control as code, because a change in it changes what every downstream algorithm sees.

1.2Inputs, work and deliverables

Figure 1.1 shows the job as a flow. The inputs are the sensor and lens module (datasheet, register map, the sensor's one-time-programmable memory with per-unit data), the SoC's ISP and its SDK (which blocks exist, what the 3A library does), the customer's requirements (viewing KPIs for a display camera, detection KPIs for a perception camera, regulations such as camera monitor systems), and a lab with charts, light sources and scenes. The work has four stages that loop: characterise the sensor, calibrate everything that depends on the optical design, tune the pipeline per operating point, and validate. The deliverables are the tuning files (one per sensor mode and platform), the calibration data specification (what is measured per design and what per unit at end of line), an IQ report that shows each KPI against its target, and release notes that say what changed and why.

Who consumes the output shapes everything. A viewing camera (rear view, surround view, a camera monitor system replacing a mirror) is judged by people: natural colour, no visible noise, no flicker, pleasant contrast. A perception camera is judged by networks and classical algorithms: stable colour and brightness from frame to frame, preserved small contrasts, no invented edges, predictable behaviour across temperature and units. Many automotive cameras serve both through two ISP outputs from the same raw frame, each with its own tuning (Camera pipeline 10.4). Section 8.6 returns to what this means for the workflow.

1.3The phases of a tuning project

ISP work follows the hardware samples of the camera project. With the first B-samples you bring up the sensor and get a first image, then characterise the sensor: black level against temperature and gain, the noise model, linearity, the defect population. Calibration follows as soon as the lens design is final, because shading, white points and colour matrices depend on the lens and the IR-cut filter. Objective tuning happens in the lab against charts and has measurable exit criteria. Subjective tuning adds real scenes and human review panels. In parallel, a raw library of lab and road captures is reprocessed after every tuning change, and field drives cover what the lab cannot: tunnels, low sun, rain, headlights, LED signs. For a perception camera the tuning must be frozen early enough for the training data to be recorded with it; after that freeze every change needs a justification and a regression run, because it shifts the input distribution of a trained network.

In practice

A new ISP engineer's first weeks are usually spent on the unglamorous end of the chain: capturing clean raw frames, checking that black level, bit depth, Bayer order and embedded metadata are what the documentation claims, and writing small scripts that read raw files and compute means and variances. Every later step depends on that plumbing being right, and mistakes there (a wrong Bayer order, a 10-bit capture read as 12-bit) produce symptoms that look like tuning problems.

2Characterising the sensor

Before any ISP block can be tuned you need to know what the sensor really delivers: its black level, how much noise it adds at every brightness and gain, where it saturates, how linear it is and which pixels are broken. These measurements feed directly into the ISP (black level registers, noise model tables, defect maps), and they set the limits that no tuning can beat.

2.1The raw signal model you will use every day

Each pixel converts photons into electrons with some quantum efficiency, collects them during the exposure, and an amplifier and ADC turn the charge into a digital number. The camera pipeline chapter describes the physics (Camera pipeline 3.3); for tuning, four quantities are enough. The conversion gain K says how many digital numbers one electron is worth (DN/e−) and scales with analog gain. Photon shot noise is Poisson: a pixel that collects N electrons on average fluctuates by √N electrons. Read noise is a constant floor added by the readout, a few electrons on modern sensors. The black level is a deliberate offset added before the ADC so that negative noise excursions are not clipped at zero. Put together, the temporal noise variance of a pixel in DN is approximately \(\sigma^2 = K\mu + \sigma_r^2\), where μ is the mean signal above black level. That one line is the noise model the ISP uses to set its noise filter thresholds, the AE uses to judge low light, and you use to predict SNR.

Numbers

For the simulated sensor (9000 e− full well, 2.5 e− read noise) the SNR of a single pixel is 7.9 dB at 10 e−, 19.7 dB at 100 e−, 30.0 dB at 1000 e− and 39.5 dB at 9000 e−. Shot noise sets the ceiling: even a perfect sensor at full well cannot beat 20·log10(√9000) ≈ 39.5 dB per pixel. Everything above that comes from averaging pixels, which is what noise reduction and downscaling do.

2.2Black level: measure it, and measure how it moves

The black level is measured by capturing frames with no light reaching the sensor: lens capped and the module in a dark box, or an exposure of zero lines if the sensor allows it. Averaging several dark frames per CFA channel gives the offset; the standard deviation of the difference of two frames divided by √2 gives the temporal dark noise without the influence of fixed patterns. In the simulation, eight 400×400 dark frames give exactly 256.00 DN and 1.11 DN of temporal noise, which is 2.6 e− at the simulated conversion gain. On a real sensor the work is in the variations: the black level drifts with temperature (dark current roughly doubles every 6–8 °C), may differ between gains and between the four Bayer channels, and many sensors clamp it automatically using optically black rows at the edge of the array. Your job is to find out which of these the sensor does, so you know whether the ISP needs a fixed value, a table per gain and temperature, or a per-frame value read from the black rows in the embedded data.

Pitfall

A black level that is a few DN too low leaves a lifted, milky black and a colour cast in the shadows, because the white-balance gains multiply the leftover offset differently in R, G and B. A few DN too high clips the noise in dark areas, which biases dark means upward and destroys shadow detail. Both errors are invisible in bright test scenes and obvious in a tunnel, which is why black level is checked at high gain and high temperature, not only at room temperature.

2.3The photon transfer curve: the sensor's ID card

The photon transfer curve (PTC) measures the noise model without trusting the datasheet. You illuminate the sensor with a uniform, flicker-free source, take pairs of frames at a series of exposure times from near-dark to saturation, and for each pair compute the mean above black and the variance of the difference image divided by two (the difference removes fixed-pattern variations such as residual shading and pixel response non-uniformity). Plotted on log-log axes, noise against signal shows three regions: a flat read-noise floor, a slope of one half where shot noise dominates, and a drop at saturation where pixels clip and stop fluctuating. The slope of variance against mean in the shot-noise region is the conversion gain K; dividing the saturation signal by K gives the full well in electrons; the ratio of full well to read noise is the dynamic range.

The simulation shows what the measurement recovers. At gain 1 the fit gives K = 0.420 DN/e− (the model's true value is 0.427), read noise 1.12 DN = 2.7 e−, saturation at the variance peak 3263 DN, which corresponds to 7761 e−, and a dynamic range of 69 dB. At gain 8 the fit gives K = 3.42 DN/e− and read noise 2.5 e−, but only 954 e− fit before the ADC clips, so the dynamic range falls to 52 dB and the best possible SNR to about 30 dB. Two details are worth noticing. The measured full well (7761 e−) is lower than the model's 9000 e− because the variance peaks where the brightest pixels in the noisy distribution start to clip, before the mean reaches full well; EMVA 1288 defines saturation exactly at that peak for this reason. And the read noise in electrons barely changes with gain on this model; on real sensors it usually falls somewhat at high gain, which is why sensors use analog gain rather than digital gain in low light.

Interesting detail

The PTC needs no knowledge of the sensor at all: no datasheet, no quantum efficiency, no lux values. Poisson statistics tie variance to mean, so the electrons are counted by their own noise. This is why the method is the backbone of EMVA 1288 and why an ISP engineer can verify a supplier's claims with a uniform light source and a spreadsheet.

Real cameras carry the result of this measurement with every frame. Android phones write it into the DNG NoiseProfile tag as two numbers, a slope S and an offset O, with variance σ² = S·x + O for a signal x normalised to full scale: the shot-noise line and the read-noise floor of the PTC. Figure 2.2 checks the tag on a night frame from a Pixel XL. In the dark sky only read noise remains, and it measures σ = 0.0036 of full scale against √O = 0.0034 from the tag. The same figure previews temporal noise reduction (Section 5.3): merging the seven frames of the burst lowers the sky noise 3.0×, slightly more than the √7 ≈ 2.6 of a plain average, because the HDR+ merge also filters noise in the frequency domain.

2.4Linearity, companding and HDR exposures

The ISP assumes that the raw value is proportional to light once the black level is removed; lens shading, white balance and the colour matrix are all multiplications that only work in linear data. Linearity is measured from the same exposure series as the PTC: mean against exposure time should be a straight line, and the deviation is reported as a percentage. Most sensors are linear to within a percent over most of their range and bend near saturation. HDR sensors complicate this. They capture several exposures or use split pixels and combine them on the sensor into 20–24-bit values, then compress them with a piecewise-linear (PWL) companding curve so they fit into 12–16 bits over the link (Camera pipeline 5.4). The first ISP block, decompanding, inverts that curve with a lookup table. Getting the table exactly right matters: a knee point that is off by one segment produces a visible band where the gradient of a smooth sky changes, and every block downstream inherits the non-linearity. You verify it by capturing a smooth ramp (a transmissive grey-scale chart or an exposure sweep) and checking that the decompanded values are a straight line across all knee points.

Pitfall

Noise behaves differently in an HDR merge than in a single exposure. Where the merge switches from the long to the short exposure, the signal is the same but the noise jumps, because the short exposure collected far fewer electrons. This SNR dip (Camera pipeline 5.3) means the noise model is no longer one curve but one curve per exposure segment, and the noise filter thresholds must follow it or the transition band looks noisier than its surroundings.

2.5Defects, fixed patterns and what EMVA 1288 adds

Every sensor has some pixels that are too bright in the dark (hot pixels, from local dark current), too dark under light (dead or weak pixels) or stuck. Their number grows with temperature and age. Characterisation captures dark frames at several temperatures and gains to find hot pixels, and flat fields at mid level to find weak ones, and produces a static defect map that the sensor's OTP or the ISP's defect correction can use. Fixed-pattern noise is the related, softer problem: column offsets from the readout amplifiers, or a per-pixel gain spread (photo-response non-uniformity, PRNU, typically below 1 %). Column noise is the one to watch: the eye and edge detectors are very sensitive to vertical stripes, so a column offset of a fraction of the temporal noise is already visible after averaging.

EMVA 1288 is the standard that turns all of this into a comparable datasheet. Release 4.0 (2021) defines how to measure quantum efficiency, conversion gain, read noise, dark current, saturation capacity, dynamic range, spatial non-uniformities (DSNU and PRNU) and linearity, with the photon transfer method at its core, and a "General" module for sensors that are not linear. It is being published as an ISO standard (ISO 24942). In practice you meet it in two ways: sensor suppliers quote EMVA numbers, and the lab equipment vendors sell setups that perform the measurement. The value for an ISP engineer is less in the formal report than in the method, because the same measurements produce the noise model and defect data the ISP needs (Standards 9 gives the standards context).

Talk track

"Before tuning anything we characterise the sensor with pairs of flat fields: the photon transfer curve gives us conversion gain, read noise, full well and dynamic range at every gain, and dark frames give black level, dark current and the defect map across temperature. That data goes straight into the ISP's black level, noise-model and defect tables, and it tells us what SNR is physically possible at a given light level, so we don't try to tune our way past physics."

3Raw-domain blocks and how to tune them

The first half of the ISP works on the Bayer mosaic while values are still proportional to light. These blocks repair the sensor and the lens: they remove offsets, broken pixels and shading, combine HDR exposures and filter noise where its statistics are still simple. Most of their parameters are calibrated rather than chosen, and errors here propagate into every later block.

3.1Why the order is fixed

Figure 3.1 lists the raw-domain blocks in the order most ISPs run them, with the measurement each one needs. The order follows from the mathematics. Black level must come first because every later operation is a multiplication, and multiplying an offset turns it into a colour cast. Defect correction comes before anything that spreads a pixel into its neighbours (shading has no spatial spread, but noise reduction and demosaic do, and they would smear a hot pixel into a coloured blob). Lens shading and white balance are per-pixel gains and can be combined, but they are calibrated separately because shading depends on the lens and position while white balance depends on the scene's light. Raw noise reduction works best before demosaic, when each pixel's noise is independent and follows the PTC; after demosaic the interpolation correlates neighbouring pixels and colours, and the noise is harder to separate from texture.

3.2Defect pixel correction: hot pixels versus distant lights

Defect correction has two halves. Static correction replaces the pixels listed in a defect map (from characterisation or from the sensor's OTP). Dynamic correction finds defects on the fly by comparing each pixel with its same-colour neighbours: if it is brighter than all of them by more than a threshold, it is replaced by their median. Dynamic correction is needed because new hot pixels appear with temperature and age, and the map cannot know them. Its threshold is the tuning knob, and the trade-off is sharper than it looks, because a small, bright, real object that falls on a single pixel is indistinguishable from a hot pixel by a same-colour test. At night, distant tail lights, street lamps and traffic lights are exactly such objects.

The experiment in Figure 3.2 makes this measurable. A dark night scene (30 e− background) contains 120 distant point lights blurred by the optics and 0.1 % hot pixels. With the plain same-colour test, every threshold from 20 to 320 DN removes 92–98 % of the hot pixels and also destroys 97 % of the lights; only at 640 DN do the lights survive, and then a third of the hot pixels stay. There is no good threshold. The fix is a better test: a real light, blurred by the lens, also brightens the directly adjacent pixels of other colours, while a hot pixel is isolated. Adding a check that the immediate neighbours stay below one eighth of the candidate's excess keeps all 120 lights intact while still removing 92 % of the hot pixels at a 20–160 DN threshold.

Why it matters

For a perception camera a deleted tail light at 200 m is a missed early cue; for a camera monitor system it is a vehicle that disappears in the mirror at night. Vendors' defect correction blocks have several modes and thresholds for this reason (separate thresholds for bright and dark defects, directional checks, cluster handling), and the tuning is verified on night scenes with known point sources, not only on dark frames.

3.3HDR merge: ratios, weights and motion

When the sensor delivers separate exposures (or the ISP receives them line-interleaved), the ISP merges them. The basic algorithm scales each exposure by its exposure ratio so they share one linear scale, then blends: in each pixel, use the long exposure where it is well below saturation (it has the best SNR), switch to the short one where the long one clips, and blend smoothly in between to avoid a visible seam. The tuning parameters are the blend thresholds and widths, the noise-dependent weights, and the motion handling. Motion is the hard part: an object that moves between the two exposures appears in different places, so a naive merge produces ghosts and coloured edges. Merge blocks detect motion as disagreement between the scaled exposures beyond what the noise model predicts, and then prefer one exposure locally. Too sensitive, and noise triggers motion everywhere so the merge falls back to the noisy short exposure; too insensitive, and moving objects ghost. Most automotive HDR sensors now merge on the sensor and send companded data, which moves the merge tuning to the sensor registers and leaves the ISP with decompanding, but the same trade-offs and the SNR dip remain (Camera pipeline 5.1).

In practice

HDR merge is validated on a scene with a large static contrast (a backlit transmissive chart covering the full range) for the transition bands, and on a moving target such as a rotating disc or a vehicle passing a bright background for ghosts. LED flicker interacts with HDR: a pulsed LED can be on during one exposure and off during the other, so the merged pixel flickers. Flicker mitigation is covered in Section 7.4 and in Camera pipeline 5.2.

3.4Lens shading: calibrate fully, correct partially

A lens delivers less light to the corners of the sensor than to the centre: geometry (the cos4 law), vignetting by the lens barrel and the microlenses' angular response all contribute. The fall-off also depends on colour, because the IR-cut filter and the microlenses behave differently at steep angles; red usually falls faster, which gives the corners a colour tint (colour shading). The lens shading block multiplies every pixel by a gain from a coarse mesh, interpolated between mesh points, with a separate mesh per Bayer channel. Calibration captures a uniform flat field: an integrating sphere or a diffuser in front of the lens, several frames averaged to remove noise, at several colour temperatures because colour shading changes with the light's spectrum. The gain in each cell is the centre value divided by the cell's value.

Measured on the simulated camera (Figure 3.3), the corners receive 49 % (red), 53 % (green) and 58 % (blue) of the centre signal, and a 16×12 mesh needs a maximum luminance gain of 1.88. The R/G ratio in the corner is 0.532 against 0.581 in the centre, so without colour correction the corners would turn cyan. The tuning question is how much of the luminance fall-off to correct. Full correction makes the corners as bright as the centre but multiplies their noise by the same gain: on a dark flat at 5 % of full scale, strength 1.0 gives 102 % brightness with corner noise 1.88× the centre noise, while strength 0.8 gives 92 % with 1.69×. The SNR in the corner does not change (a gain multiplies signal and noise alike, so both stay at about 21 dB), but the noise becomes visible and the noise filter, tuned for the centre, now sees more noise in the corners than it expects.

A real phone module can be much stronger than the simulated one. The HDR+ dataset stores the shading map the phone applied to each frame, 17×13 cells per Bayer channel. On a Nexus 6 (Figure 3.3b) the largest gains are 3.35× for red and about 2.8× for green and blue: the corners receive roughly a third of the centre's light, and red falls off faster, as in the simulation. A clear sky is a convenient real-world check because it is nearly uniform, so the uncorrected frame shows both the brightness fall-off and the corners' colour shift.

In practice

A common recipe is to correct colour shading fully (colour tints are objectionable and confuse colour-based algorithms) and luminance shading partially, around 70–85 % in good light, and to reduce the luminance strength further at high gain, where corner noise is the bigger problem. The Raspberry Pi tuning tool in Chapter 11 encodes exactly this: its output has a luminance strength of 0.8 and full colour tables. Some ISPs also feed the shading gain to the noise filter so the threshold rises in the corners.

Pitfall

Flat-field calibration needs a truly uniform source. A light box with a visible hot spot, a diffuser that is not perpendicular to the optical axis, or a module that is slightly tilted produces an asymmetric table that bakes the lab's error into every camera. Check the measured mesh for symmetry and compare it with the lens supplier's relative illumination curve before releasing it.

3.5Raw noise filtering driven by the noise model

A noise filter must decide, at every pixel, whether a difference to its neighbours is noise (smooth it away) or structure (keep it). The physically meaningful threshold is a multiple of the expected noise at that brightness, and Chapter 2 gave that expectation: \(\sigma(I) = \sqrt{\sigma_r^2 + K I}\). A fixed threshold fails in both directions (Figure 3.4): in bright areas the noise is larger than the threshold and survives, in dark areas the threshold is larger than real detail and erases it. Raw-domain filters therefore store a noise curve, usually a small lookup table of sigma against intensity per gain, and multiply it by tuning strengths. TI's AM6xA tuning guide describes exactly this step for its NSF4 filter: you load a raw chart image, the tool samples noise on the grey patches, and you adjust the threshold curve until the sample points sit on the fitted curve, keeping the overall strength at or below 1.0.

Two further raw-domain effects are tuned here. Green imbalance is a small difference between the Gr and Gb pixels (green pixels in red rows and in blue rows), caused by optical and electrical crosstalk from their different neighbours; uncorrected, it produces a fine maze pattern after demosaic. A green-equalisation step averages the two greens when their difference is within the expected noise. The Raspberry Pi tool calibrates this as "GEQ" with an offset and slope (Chapter 11). Crosstalk itself also desaturates colour, which the colour matrix later has to undo, at the cost of noise.

Talk track

"The raw domain is where we repair the sensor and lens: black level, defects, HDR merge, shading and a first noise filter. Most of it is calibrated, not tuned, and the noise filter is driven by the measured noise model so the same strength setting means the same thing at every brightness. The real trade-offs here are small: how much corner noise we accept for flat shading, and how aggressively we remove hot pixels without deleting distant lights at night."

4White balance and colour

A camera's raw colour depends on the light as much as on the object: a white wall is orange under a halogen lamp and blue in shade. White balance removes the light's colour so that neutral objects come out neutral, and the colour correction matrix maps the sensor's own colour response to a standard colour space. Calibrating both is a lab job with a colour chart; making AWB choose the right light in a real scene is the hard tuning problem.

4.1Two problems: which light, and which colours

White balance is three gains (in practice two, because green is the reference) applied in the raw domain. If you knew the light, the gains would be trivial: the inverse of the raw R/G and B/G ratios that a grey surface produces under it. The difficulty is that the camera does not know the light. It sees only the product of light and surface, and a white wall under warm light can produce the same raw values as a beige wall under daylight. Auto white balance (AWB) is the algorithm that estimates the light from the statistics of the scene, and its errors are the most visible colour failures of any camera.

The colour correction matrix (CCM) solves a different problem. Even after perfect white balance, a camera's three filters do not have the spectral shapes of the human eye's cone responses or of the sRGB primaries, so saturated colours come out with the wrong hue and saturation. A 3×3 matrix, fitted per light source, maps white-balanced camera RGB to linear sRGB (or another target space). Colour-science theory, colour spaces and transfer functions belong to topic 8; here we need only what the calibration and tuning use.

4.2Calibrating the white points

The calibration captures a grey chart or the grey row of a ColorChecker under each light source in the light booth, typically CIE A (incandescent, 2856 K), a cool white or TL84 fluorescent (about 4000 K), D50, D65 and often a horizon light (about 2300 K). For each, the mean raw R/G and B/G of the grey patches gives one white point. Figure 4.1 plots them for the simulated camera together with a series of blackbody and daylight sources between 2300 and 9000 K. They form a smooth curve, the camera's white-point locus: R/G falls and B/G rises as the colour temperature increases. Under D65 the camera needs gains of R ×1.73 and B ×1.18; under A it needs R ×0.95 and B ×2.22. TL84, a fluorescent with spiky emission lines, lands visibly off the curve on the green side. AWB therefore needs fluorescent lights as separate reference points; a locus fitted only to blackbody sources would push them toward magenta.

Interesting detail

These ratios are properties of one sensor and filter design, not of the light alone. A different sensor under the same D65 lamp produces different R/G and B/G values, which is why white points are recalibrated for every new sensor or IR-cut filter, and why per-unit spread matters: if the filter thickness varies between modules, each camera's locus shifts slightly. Per-unit white-balance calibration at end of line (topic 8) exists to remove that spread.

4.3AWB algorithms and why they fail

The ISP's statistics engine reports, for a grid of zones (for example 32×24), the mean R, G and B of pixels that are neither too dark nor clipped. AWB algorithms work on those zone values. Grey world assumes the average of the scene is grey and takes the gains from the mean of all zones. Max-RGB (white patch) assumes the brightest values are a white surface. Grey search, the family used by most production AWB algorithms in some form, keeps only the zones whose chromaticity falls inside the calibrated grey zone, then searches along the locus for the light that makes most of them neutral, weighted by their area and brightness. Production algorithms add priors on top: outdoors at high lux the light is almost certainly daylight, so the search is weighted toward 5000–7000 K; at low lux indoor and street lighting become likely. The Raspberry Pi tuning output in Chapter 11 contains exactly such lux-dependent priors.

Figure 4.2 measures the three algorithms on four scenes built from the ColorChecker's real reflectance spectra under D65. On the full chart, grey search is within 0.1° of the true light, max-RGB within 0.6° and grey world 4.7° off, because the chart's average is not quite grey. On a scene that is mostly foliage, grey world is 14.7° off and max-RGB 16.2°, while grey search finds the few grey road pixels and stays at 0.2°. A red bus filling the view pulls grey world 30.5° off. But on a scene with nothing neutral in it, only foliage and sky, grey search also fails (4.9° under D65 and 11.3° under A): it locks onto whichever coloured surface happens to fall in the grey zone. That is the fundamental limit of AWB, and it is why production algorithms combine grey search with priors, temporal smoothing and, increasingly, learned models.

Pitfall

Large single-colour scenes are where AWB tuning is tested: a green field, a red truck, a yellow tunnel lit by sodium lamps, a blue sky filling a fisheye's upper half. Widening the grey zone makes AWB more robust in dim mixed light but lets more coloured surfaces vote; narrowing it makes the estimate precise when greys exist and unstable when they don't. Tune it on a scene library, not on the chart, because the chart always contains greys.

4.4Deriving a colour correction matrix

The CCM is fitted on the 24 patches of a ColorChecker photographed under one light. After black level, shading and white balance, each patch gives a camera RGB triple; the reference gives the patch's true colour in linear sRGB (computed here from the measured spectral reflectances under D65). The fit finds the 3×3 matrix that maps the first to the second. Two constraints are standard. Each row of the matrix sums to one, so white stays white and the white balance is not undone. And the error is measured in a perceptual colour difference (ΔE00, CIEDE2000) rather than in RGB, because equal RGB errors are not equally visible: least-squares in RGB over-weights bright patches and under-weights the dark ones. The fit is a small non-linear optimisation over six free numbers.

On the simulated camera under D65, white balance alone leaves a mean ΔE00 of 6.7 and a maximum of 11.4 across the 24 patches. The fitted matrix brings that to a mean of 1.01 and a maximum of 1.87, which is better than the 2–3 a trained observer notices side by side. The matrix is

\[ M_{D65} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.718 & -0.662 & -0.055\\ -0.173 & 1.674 & -0.500\\ 0.032 & -0.482 & 1.451 \end{pmatrix} \]

The large diagonal and negative off-diagonal terms are typical: the camera's filters overlap more than the sRGB primaries do, so the matrix has to subtract neighbouring channels to restore saturation. Figure 4.3 shows the effect patch by patch: the middle third of each swatch is white-balanced camera colour, muted and slightly shifted; the right third, after the CCM, is close to the reference on the left.

4.5The colour-noise trade-off

Those negative coefficients have a price. If each input channel carries independent noise of the same size, the noise in output channel i grows by the length of row i of the matrix: for the D65 matrix 1.84, 1.76 and 1.53. Colour noise in dark areas grows by roughly 1.7×, which is often more visible than a slightly desaturated colour. Figure 4.4 makes the trade-off explicit by blending the fitted matrix with the identity: \(M(\alpha) = I + \alpha (M - I)\). At α = 0 there is no extra noise and mean ΔE00 is 6.7; at α = 0.6 noise gain is 1.41 and ΔE00 2.7; at α = 1 the colours are best (1.01) and the noise gain 1.71. Beyond α = 1 both get worse. In low light many ISPs therefore use a less saturated matrix, interpolated by gain or lux, accepting worse colour where noise would dominate the impression anyway.

4.6Matrices per light source, and colour for machines

A matrix fitted under D65 is not right under incandescent light, because the camera's spectral mismatch shows up differently when the light's spectrum changes. On the simulation, using the D65 matrix under illuminant A gives a mean ΔE00 of 4.38 (maximum 10.1); a matrix fitted under A gives 2.05 (maximum 6.0). The A matrix also differs in shape and amplifies blue noise most (row length 1.79), because the blue signal is weak under warm light and the white-balance gain on blue is already 2.2. ISPs therefore store a set of matrices at the calibrated colour temperatures and interpolate between them using the CCT that AWB reports. The Raspberry Pi tool, run on the same simulated sensor in Chapter 11, produces four such matrices and reports mean ΔE improving from 23–25 to 1.3–2.0 (its error metric starts from uncorrected camera RGB and uses its own reference values, so the numbers are not directly comparable to the ones above).

For perception, colour accuracy matters less than colour stability and the separability of the colours that carry meaning: red, amber and green signal lights, yellow lane markings in work zones, orange construction elements. A network trained on one CCM will see a shifted input distribution if the matrix changes, so a CCM change after the data freeze is treated like any other domain shift (Neural network training 9.2). Some perception pipelines skip the CCM altogether and train on white-balanced camera RGB, which avoids the noise amplification; this is a legitimate choice as long as it is made once and documented.

Talk track

"We calibrate white points and colour matrices under four or five standard lights in a booth with a ColorChecker. AWB then has to guess the light from scene statistics. Grey search with lux priors works well when something grey is in view and fails on scenes with nothing neutral, which is why we test it on a library of single-colour scenes. The CCM is fitted in a perceptual error metric with white preserved, and in low light we desaturate it because colour accuracy costs noise: our D65 matrix amplifies colour noise by about 1.7×."

5Demosaic, noise reduction and sharpness

After the colour gains, the ISP reconstructs full colour at every pixel and then shapes how the image trades noise against detail. This is where most of the subjective "look" of a camera is decided, and where tuning for people and tuning for networks diverge most.

5.1Demosaicing: guessing two colours out of three

Each pixel measured one colour; demosaicing estimates the other two from the neighbours. Bilinear interpolation averages the nearest pixels of the missing colour. It is simple and wrong at edges: when the red and green samples on either side of an edge are averaged, the colour channels are misaligned by up to a pixel, which shows as zipper patterns and false colour on fine detail. Better algorithms exploit two facts about natural images: colour changes more slowly than brightness, so colour differences (R−G, B−G) are smoother than the channels themselves; and edges have a direction, so interpolating along an edge is safer than across it. Malvar-He-Cutler adds a gradient correction from the green channel to bilinear interpolation using fixed 5×5 kernels; Menon's directional method interpolates horizontally and vertically and chooses per pixel. Hardware demosaic blocks are proprietary, but most are edge-directed variants with tunable thresholds for direction detection and false-colour suppression.

On a grey zone plate (Figure 5.1), where any colour at all is an artifact, bilinear reaches 16.1 dB PSNR with RMS false colour of 28 (8-bit units), Malvar-He-Cutler 27.5 dB and 9, and Menon 37.9 dB and 3. On the flag crop, where detail is coarser, the three are close (40.7, 41.6, 41.9 dB). The lesson is that demosaic quality only shows on fine, high-contrast detail near the sampling limit: thin lane markings far away, text on signs, fences. Those are also exactly the structures that cause moiré, a low-frequency pattern created when detail above the sensor's Nyquist frequency folds back. No demosaic can remove true aliasing; it can only avoid adding colour to it, which is why false-colour suppression (desaturating chroma where high-frequency luma is strong) is a standard tuning parameter.

On a real mosaic the differences are smaller but of the same kind (Figure 5.1b, a building facade in daylight at ISO 40). Bilinear leaves coloured fringes along the window frames and a zipper pattern on the rooftop railing, Malvar-He-Cutler removes most of them, and Menon's directional interpolation gives the cleanest edges. The lens has already blurred the finest detail before it reaches the sensor, which is why the gap is smaller than on the zone plate.

5.2Spatial noise reduction: luma and chroma separately

After demosaic and the colour matrix, the image is usually converted to a luma-chroma representation and the two are filtered differently. Chroma noise (coloured blotches) is ugly and carries little useful information at fine scales, so chroma is filtered strongly, often at several scales of an image pyramid, to remove low-frequency blotches as well as pixel noise. Luma carries the detail, so its filter must preserve edges and texture: bilateral filters, non-local means and their hardware approximations average only neighbours that are similar in value, with a similarity threshold that follows the noise model so the strength is consistent across brightness. Multi-scale designs filter each level of a pyramid, which removes coarse "blotchy" noise that a 5×5 filter cannot reach, and let the tuning set different strengths per scale and per gain.

What a noise filter cannot avoid is the confusion between noise and fine, low-contrast texture: asphalt, grass, fabric, distant foliage. Both are small random variations around a mean; a filter strong enough to remove noise also removes texture of the same amplitude, and the result looks "plastic" or "painted". This is why modern IQ testing measures texture separately from edge sharpness (Section 13.5): an ISP that smooths flat areas and sharpens edges can score well on SNR and edge MTF while destroying the textures that the eye, and many networks, use to judge surfaces.

5.3Temporal noise reduction: free SNR with a motion problem

Video gives a second dimension: a static pixel is measured again in every frame. A recursive temporal filter blends the current frame with the previous output, \(y_t = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha) y_{t-1}\); for a static scene and independent noise its output variance is \(\alpha/(2-\alpha)\) of the input, so α = 0.25 reduces noise power to one seventh (8.5 dB) without touching spatial detail. Figure 2.2 shows the effect on a real phone burst, where merging seven frames lowers the noise of a dark sky 3.0×. The problem is motion: anything that moves leaves a trail. Temporal filters therefore detect motion per pixel (the difference between the current frame and the history, compared with the noise model) and fall back to α = 1 where something moves, sometimes with motion-compensated history. The tuning knobs are the blend strength, the motion threshold and how fast a pixel returns to full filtering after motion stops. For perception, temporal NR is treated with care: a trail behind a crossing pedestrian or a delayed appearance of a brake light changes what the detector sees, so perception outputs often use weak or no temporal filtering, and the viewing output carries the stronger setting.

5.4Sharpening, and the noise-detail trade-off you can measure

Sharpening (edge enhancement) boosts high spatial frequencies: the classic unsharp mask adds a multiple of the difference between the image and a blurred copy. It compensates the blur of the lens, the demosaic and the noise filter, and it is what makes an image look crisp. It also amplifies noise and creates overshoot, a bright halo on the bright side of an edge and a dark one on the other. Hardware edge enhancers add controls to manage this: coring (ignore differences smaller than a threshold, usually tied to the noise level), separate gains for positive and negative overshoot, a limit on the halo amplitude, and a gain curve over brightness so dark, noisy areas are sharpened less. TI's EE block, for example, is tuned with a threshold, a slope and a gain that generate a lookup table.

Figure 5.2 lets you set noise reduction and sharpening on a low-light test target (gain 8, mid-grey at roughly 140 e−) and shows the processed image with four measurements: SNR on the flat patch, the slanted-edge SFR at a quarter of the sampling frequency, a texture fidelity score (correlation of the processed texture with the noise-free texture) and the edge overshoot. Without either, SNR is 21.8 dB, SFR 0.61, texture 0.74. Noise reduction at k = 2 lifts SNR to 29.9 dB and texture fidelity to 0.87, because removing noise also makes the texture easier to see; going to k = 4 adds only 0.6 dB more but lowers the SFR to 0.51, as the filter starts to soften the edge. Sharpening at 1.5 without noise reduction pushes SFR to 1.25 but drops SNR to 15.4 dB and texture fidelity to 0.58 with 17 % overshoot. The good combinations sit in the middle: k = 3 with sharpening 1.0 gives 28.4 dB SNR, SFR 1.05 and 2 % overshoot, while k = 2 with the same sharpening is crisper (SFR 1.18) but already shows 8 % overshoot.

Why it matters

Every metric in the figure can be pushed up by sacrificing another. A tuning judged only on SNR ends up plastic; one judged only on MTF ends up noisy with halos. This is why IQ targets are agreed as a set of KPIs with limits on each, and why the noise model is used everywhere: it lets one strength setting mean the same thing at every brightness and gain.

Pitfall

Sharpening halos are particularly harmful for perception. A network or an edge detector sees the halo as a real edge parallel to the true one, which can double lane markings at a distance or shift a box edge. Perception outputs are usually tuned with little or no sharpening; if the downstream algorithm needs crisper input, it is cheaper to say so in the CV KPIs than to discover it after the dataset was recorded.

5.5Chromatic aberration and purple fringing

Lateral chromatic aberration makes the lens image red, green and blue at slightly different magnifications, so high-contrast edges near the image corners show coloured fringes that grow with distance from the centre. Because it is a geometric effect, it is corrected by resampling the red and blue channels with a small radial scale (or a mesh) relative to green; the calibration measures the shift on a dot or checker chart across the field. The Raspberry Pi tool calls this CAC and uses a dot chart. Purple fringing around bright highlights has different causes (longitudinal aberration, sensor blooming) and cannot be fixed geometrically; ISPs reduce it by desaturating purple near saturated edges, which is a tuning trade-off against real purple objects.

6Tone mapping and contrast

An automotive scene can span 120 dB of luminance and an HDR sensor can capture it, but a display shows perhaps 40 dB and a perception network consumes 8 or 12 bits. Tone mapping decides which parts of the range get the output codes. It is the most scene-dependent block, the one most likely to produce visible artifacts, and the one whose temporal stability matters most for perception.

6.1Global curves: simple, stable and flat

A global tone curve maps every input level to an output level with one function, applied the same way everywhere in the image. The sRGB gamma is one example; a logarithmic or filmic curve that compresses highlights is another. Global curves are cheap, monotonic and stable from frame to frame, which makes them attractive for perception. Their weakness shows in high-contrast scenes. To test it, the experiment builds an HDR scene of 103 dB (99.9th percentile against 0.5th): a dim room with a window about 250× brighter. Exposing for the window leaves the room at a mean level of 2/255; exposing for the room clips the window. A log curve over the whole range brings the room to 49/255 and keeps the window, but the local contrast in the room falls from 17.4 to 7.5 (standard deviation of fine detail in 8-bit units). The curve spends its codes on the jump between room and window, a range in which no detail exists, and every region gets only a thin slice.

6.2Local tone mapping: compress the illumination, keep the detail

Local tone mapping exploits a property of scenes: large brightness differences usually come from illumination (sun and shade, inside and outside a tunnel), while the detail we care about is small local variation on top. The classic method splits the log luminance into a smooth base layer and a detail layer, compresses the base and adds the detail back unchanged. The choice of smoothing filter is decisive. A Gaussian blur mixes the bright window into the wall next to it, so the base there is too high, the wall gets compressed too much and a dark halo appears around the window: in the experiment the wall next to the window ends up 8 levels darker than the wall further away. An edge-aware filter (bilateral, guided filter or the multi-scale methods hardware LTMs use) stops at the window edge. At the same room brightness as the log curve (49/255), the edge-aware version keeps room contrast at 8.8 instead of 7.5 and window contrast at 25.0 instead of 21.2, without the dark band.

Hardware local tone mappers expose this as a handful of controls: overall strength, how much shadows may be lifted and highlights pulled down, the spatial scale of the base layer, and limits on local gain. TI's GLBCE (global and local brightness and contrast enhancement) and the local tone mapping blocks in Qualcomm's pipeline are examples. Tuning targets come from scenes rather than charts: tunnel entrances and exits, low sun behind traffic, a dark underpass with a lit sign, headlights at night.

Pitfall

Local tone mapping changes the relationship between scene brightness and pixel value from place to place and from frame to frame. A grey car looks lighter in a dark part of the image than in a bright one, and its appearance changes when a bright truck enters the frame next to it. People rarely notice; networks and classical algorithms that rely on brightness constancy do. Strong LTM also amplifies noise in lifted shadows. For perception outputs the usual compromise is a mild, slowly adapting LTM with strict limits on frame-to-frame change.

6.3Temporal stability and tone for networks

Tone mapping is computed from statistics of previous frames (histograms, zone means), so it is a control loop like AE and has the same stability problems: if the curve reacts fully to every change, the image "pumps" when a bright object passes. Tone and AE are often tuned together, because both decide the mean brightness and they can fight each other. For perception, three requirements are typical. The output should change smoothly over time with a bounded rate. The mapping should be monotonic, so that a brighter object stays brighter. And small contrasts in dark areas (a pedestrian in dark clothes against a dark background) should survive, which is exactly what the contrast detection metrics of IEEE 2020 test (Section 13.6). Some programmes avoid local tone mapping for perception altogether and feed the network a 12- or 16-bit companded linear image, leaving range compression to the network's first layers.

Talk track

"Tone mapping decides where 120 dB of scene range lands in 8 or 12 output bits. A global curve is stable but flattens local contrast; local tone mapping compresses illumination and keeps detail, but needs an edge-aware base layer or it draws halos, and it breaks brightness constancy. For the driver display we use a stronger LTM; for perception we use a gentle, rate-limited one, or none, and we verify small-contrast detection in dark regions rather than just looking at pictures."

73A: the control loops around the ISP

The ISP processes pixels; the 3A algorithms decide how. Auto exposure sets integration time and gain, auto white balance picks the light, and in automotive fixed-focus cameras auto focus is absent, so in practice it is 2A plus flicker handling. They run as feedback loops with a delay of one or two frames, and tuning them is control engineering as much as image quality.

7.1Statistics in, settings out, with a delay

The ISP's statistics engine produces, for every frame, a compact summary that software can afford to read: zone means of R, G and B on a grid, luminance histograms, sometimes a separate histogram of the brightest pixels or a count of clipped pixels. On TI this is the H3A block (named after AE, AWB and AF statistics); Qualcomm's front end produces Bayer grid and histogram statistics. The 3A algorithms run on a CPU, a real-time core or a DSP, compute new exposure, gain, white-balance gains, CCT and often tone-curve parameters, and write them back. White-balance gains and the colour matrix act on the next frame the ISP processes. Exposure and gain go to the sensor over I2C, and sensors apply them with a pipeline delay, typically one to two frames, so a decision made on frame n shows up in frame n+2 (Figure 7.1). The outputs also go into the frame metadata (exposure, gain, CCT, a lux estimate), which downstream perception and recording use.

7.2Auto exposure: what to meter and what target to hit

AE first decides what "correctly exposed" means. A metering strategy weights the zones: centre-weighted, a weighted region of interest for the road ahead, or the lower part of a side camera's image where the road is. The weighted mean luma is compared with a target, typically 15–25 % of full scale for a linear output, then adjusted for the scene: in high-contrast scenes the target is lowered so highlights do not clip, in very dark scenes it is lowered to limit noise and gain. A good AE also watches the histogram's top: if more than a set fraction of pixels clip, it reduces exposure even if the mean is low. Once AE has a target brightness, an exposure table decides how to reach it: first integration time up to a maximum (limited by motion blur and frame time, and quantised for flicker, Section 7.4), then analog gain, then digital gain. HDR sensors add exposure ratios between their exposures, which AE adjusts with the scene's dynamic range.

Numbers

Qualcomm's AE uses a "lux index", a logarithmic measure of exposure times gain that is high in dark scenes and low in bright ones; an open-source converter of Qualcomm tuning files reports about 23.45 index steps per doubling of exposure times gain (label: reported by a third-party tool, verify on your SDK). Tuning parameters such as noise reduction strength are selected by this index (Section 8.3). The Raspberry Pi tuning file stores a lux reference instead: the exposure, gain and image brightness measured on a chart at a known 1000 lux.

7.3Convergence: fast, stable, or both

At a tunnel exit the scene brightens by a factor of 10–100 in a few frames. AE must reduce exposure quickly enough that the image is usable within a fraction of a second, without overshooting into a dark image or oscillating. The basic update multiplies exposure by \((T/m)^{s}\), where T is the target, m the measured luma and s a speed between 0 and 1. With no delay, s = 1 would correct in one step. With a delay of two frames, a naive controller keeps correcting the same error three times before the first correction becomes visible, and overshoots. Saturation adds a second difficulty: when most pixels clip, the measured mean is far below the true brightness, so the controller underestimates the error and converges slowly.

The model behind Figure 7.2 shows the trade-off in numbers (experiments/check_interactive.js). With a 2-frame delay and a ±5 % dead band, the naive controller at speed 0.3 settles within ±10 % of the target in 12 frames with one crossing; at 0.5 it settles in 10 frames but undershoots by 26 %; at 0.8 it needs 23 frames and crosses the target 9 times; at 1.0 it never settles. The delay-aware controller does not oscillate at any speed and settles in 16 frames at 0.5 and 6 frames at 1.0. Removing the dead band at speed 0.5 does not change the settling time but makes the naive controller hunt around the target (17 crossings instead of 2), which on screen is a slow brightness flicker. Real AE algorithms combine these ideas: they account for pending exposure changes, use larger steps when many pixels are clipped (the histogram tells them how far off they are), use a dead band with hysteresis, and adapt faster for brightening than for darkening, because a burnt-out image loses information while a slightly dark one does not.

In practice

AE convergence is specified and tested: for example, maximum frames to reach ±10 % of the target after a 6 EV step, maximum overshoot, and no oscillation in a static scene. The test uses a programmable light source or a tunnel scene in a test drive, and the frame-by-frame mean luma from the metadata. For perception, AE stability also means no brightness change when nothing in the scene changes, which is checked on long static captures.

7.4Flicker: mains antibanding and LED mitigation

Lamps on 50 Hz mains flicker at 100 Hz (60 Hz mains: 120 Hz). With a rolling shutter each row starts its exposure a little later, so if the exposure time is not a whole number of flicker periods, different rows integrate different amounts of light and dark bands roll through the image (Figure 7.3). Antibanding forces exposure times to multiples of 10 ms (50 Hz) or 8.33 ms (60 Hz) whenever the exposure is long enough, and uses gain for the remaining adjustment. Flicker detection decides which mains frequency applies, from the statistics or from a dedicated row-based analysis. In bright daylight exposures are far shorter than 10 ms and antibanding cannot apply, but then no mains lamp matters either.

LED traffic lights, signs and vehicle lights are a different problem: they are driven by pulse-width modulation at frequencies from about 90 Hz to several kHz with duty cycles that can be 10 % or less. A short daylight exposure can fall entirely in the off phase, so the LED appears off in some frames. Antibanding does not help, because there is no single frequency. LED flicker mitigation (LFM) is a sensor feature (split pixels or a dedicated long, low-sensitivity exposure that always spans at least one PWM period, Camera pipeline 5.2), and the ISP engineer's part is to configure the sensor mode, tune the merge so the LFM exposure is used where LEDs are, and test it with programmable LED sources across frequencies and duty cycles (Section 13.6).

7.5AWB as a control loop

AWB has the same loop structure and the same stability problem in colour. If the estimate jumps whenever a coloured object enters the frame, the whole image changes colour, which is distracting on a display and a distribution shift for a network. AWB tuning therefore includes temporal filtering of the estimated gains, a convergence speed, and a stability zone in which small estimate changes are ignored. Scene changes need faster adaptation (driving out of a sodium-lit tunnel into daylight) while steady driving needs very slow, damped changes. Many automotive AWB implementations also limit the estimate to a plausible CCT range per lux level and freeze or slow the estimate when too few grey zones are found, instead of trusting a poor estimate.

Talk track

"3A is a feedback loop with one to two frames of delay. AE meters a weighted region, aims for a target that drops in high-contrast scenes, and walks an exposure table quantised for mains flicker. Its speed is a stability trade-off: a naive full correction with two frames of delay oscillates, so we damp it, account for pending changes, use a dead band and react faster to clipping than to darkness. LED flicker is solved in the sensor with an LFM mode; antibanding only handles mains lighting."

8The tuning workflow, step by step

Knowing every block is not enough; tuning succeeds or fails on order, bookkeeping and repeatability. This chapter puts the blocks into the sequence a tuning project actually follows, explains how parameters are organised over light level and colour temperature, and describes the regression machinery that keeps a tuning from silently breaking.

8.1The order of work

Figure 8.1 shows the dependencies. Calibration comes first and in a fixed sequence: black level, because everything else assumes it; the noise profile, because noise reduction and dynamic defect correction depend on it; the defect map; lens shading, which needs correct black level and a defect-free flat field; white points, which need shading-corrected greys; colour matrices, which need correct white balance. Tuning follows: noise reduction, then sharpening (which must be tuned on the denoised image), then tone and local contrast, and finally the AE targets, because the target brightness interacts with the tone curve. Change anything upstream and everything downstream has to be checked again, which is why late changes to black level or shading are expensive.

Vendor tools encode the same order. TI's AM6xA tuning guide lists black level subtraction, H3A, PCID (only for RGB-IR sensors), AWB, colour correction, edge enhancement, NSF4 noise filter, mesh LDC, CFA interpolation with WDR, and lens shading, and notes that for a plain RGB sensor the minimum set is BLC, H3A, AWB, CCM, NSF4 and EE. The step order in that list differs slightly from Figure 8.1 (sharpening before noise filtering), which reflects the tool's plug-in sequence for getting a first good image, not a different dependency; experienced tuners iterate between the two anyway.

8.2Step by step through a first tuning

A first tuning of a new sensor-lens combination, in the order a team would do it:

  1. Bring-up checks. Confirm resolution, bit depth, Bayer order and black level from the datasheet against real captures: a capture of a red object must make the expected pixel positions bright. Confirm that exposure and gain registers do what the driver thinks (linear response in an exposure sweep).
  2. Default profile. Generate a neutral starting tuning with the vendor's script or template: correct black level, flat shading, unity CCM, mild default noise reduction. The goal is a stable, unbeautiful image to work from.
  3. Characterisation. Dark frames over gain and temperature, flat-field pairs for the PTC at each gain, defect maps. Fill in black level and noise model tables.
  4. Calibration. Flat fields at each light source for lens shading; ColorChecker captures in the booth under each light at a good exposure for white points and CCMs. Check that shading is symmetric and that the white points form a smooth locus.
  5. Objective tuning per gain. For each gain step (or lux bin), tune noise reduction and sharpening on charts lit to the matching light level, against targets for SNR, SFR, texture and overshoot. Tune AE metering and targets, AWB grey zone and priors.
  6. Subjective and task tuning. Real scenes, viewed by a panel for viewing cameras; recorded scenes run through the perception stack for CV cameras. Adjust tone, local contrast, colour saturation by lux, temporal filters.
  7. Regression and release. Run the raw library through the new tuning, compare against the baseline, write release notes, tag the tuning file.

Every step produces data that the next step depends on, and every step is repeated per sensor mode (linear, HDR, different resolutions or frame rates), because each mode has its own black level, noise and timing.

Pitfall

The most common cause of lost weeks is tuning on images that were not what you thought: a capture with AE still running, a raw file saved after some ISP block, a chart lit at a different level than recorded, a module whose IR-cut filter differs from production. Lock exposure and white balance for lab captures, record the light level and source with each file (the Raspberry Pi tool even parses colour temperature and lux from the file name), and check the first and last frames of a capture series for drift.

8.3Parameters over gain, lux and colour temperature

Almost no parameter has one value. Noise reduction must be stronger at gain 16 than at gain 1; the CCM should be desaturated in low light; shading and colour matrices depend on the light's colour temperature. ISPs therefore store most parameters as small tables indexed by an operating point and interpolate between the entries (Figure 8.2). The light axis is total gain, exposure index or lux index; the colour axis is the CCT reported by AWB. Qualcomm's tuning organises this as "trigger" regions, selected by values such as the AE lux index or gain, the DRC gain and the CCT, with interpolation between neighbouring regions; other vendors call them gain bins, ISO tables or dynamic parameters. The 3A software computes the operating point each frame and the ISP driver writes the interpolated values.

Pitfall

Interpolation boundaries are where tuning bugs hide. If neighbouring sets differ a lot, a car driving under street lamps crosses the boundary every few seconds and the image visibly pumps between two looks. Keep neighbouring sets close, use hysteresis on the operating point, and test with slow light ramps rather than only at the grid points.

8.4Objective first, subjective second

Objective tuning optimises measurable KPIs on charts: SNR at given lux levels, SFR, texture acutance, colour error, shading uniformity, dynamic range, AE and AWB convergence. It is repeatable, so it is done first and produces a baseline that can be regression-tested. Subjective tuning starts from that baseline with real scenes and human judgement: do skin tones, vegetation and sky look natural, is the noise texture acceptable, does the image look flat or harsh. For viewing cameras it is done with a panel of reviewers on calibrated displays, comparing against a reference camera or the previous release; for camera monitor systems it also involves the customer's own reviewers and regulatory requirements. Subjective changes are kept small and recorded, and objective KPIs are rerun after each change so the subjective tuning does not quietly break a KPI.

8.5Raw libraries, simulators and tuning files under version control

A tuning team's most valuable asset is its raw library: lab captures of every chart under every light and gain, plus a curated set of road scenes (tunnels, night, rain, low sun, LED signs, snow), each stored as raw frames with full metadata. Vendors provide a bit-exact software model of their ISP (TI's tuning tool can run the VPAC model on a PC; Qualcomm's tools include simulation; the open libcamera ISPs have software implementations), so every tuning change can be applied to the whole library offline and compared against the previous release (Figure 8.3). The comparison is numeric (KPIs on charts, perception metrics on recorded scenes) and visual (difference images and side-by-side review of flagged scenes).

Tuning files are released like code. They live in version control as the editable source (XML or JSON parameters, calibration data, the tool project), and the build produces the binary the target loads. Each release has a version that the camera reports in its metadata, release notes that list which parameters changed and why, and the regression report. For perception cameras the tuning version is recorded with every training sample, so a dataset can always be traced to the ISP configuration that produced it.

8.6Tuning for networks: different goals, different process

The camera pipeline chapter explains the difference in goals (Camera pipeline 10.4): viewing tuning optimises appearance, perception tuning optimises information and stability. The workflow consequences are what an ISP engineer has to manage. KPIs come partly from the perception team: detection recall at a given distance and light level, false positives on noise, stability of colour classification for signal lights. Tuning changes are evaluated by running the perception stack on the raw library, not only by charts. The tuning has to be frozen before large-scale data recording, because every change after that is a shift between training and deployment data; late changes need a retraining or at least an evaluation of the impact. Many programmes record raw (or lightly processed, companded) data in the fleet precisely so that the ISP can be re-run with a new tuning later and the dataset regenerated. And the perception output is tuned for consistency across units and temperature, so per-unit calibration of shading and white balance matters more than for a viewing camera.

Talk track

"We tune in a fixed order: calibrate black level, noise, defects, shading, white points and CCMs, then tune noise, sharpness, tone and AE per gain and colour temperature, objective first, then subjective. Parameters live in tables over gain and CCT with interpolation and hysteresis. Every change runs through a raw library on the vendor's bit-exact simulator and is released as a versioned tuning file. For perception the tuning is frozen before data recording, and later changes are treated as a dataset shift."

9Tuning on Qualcomm platforms

Qualcomm's Snapdragon SoCs, including the automotive Snapdragon Ride and cockpit platforms, contain the Spectra ISP and are tuned with Qualcomm's Chromatix tools. Detailed documentation and the tools themselves are available to licensees under agreement; this chapter uses only public sources and marks what comes from third parties.

9.1The Spectra hardware path

Public descriptions of the Spectra ISP (for example of the Spectra 480 on the Robotics RB5 platform) name three main processing engines. The Image Front End (IFE) receives the sensor data through its CSI decoders, performs the first raw processing and produces statistics; it can output raw Bayer frames to memory, YUV streams for preview and video, and raw snapshots. Smaller IFE-lite units handle additional, simpler cameras with little or no processing. The Bayer Processing Segment (BPS) performs high-quality raw processing for snapshots and offline paths, and the Image Processing Engine (IPE) does YUV-domain processing such as noise reduction, sharpening, scaling and colour adjustments before frames go to the display, encoder or compute engines (Figure 9.1). On the RB5 description the Spectra 480 has two full IFEs for large sensors and five IFE-lite units for 2 MP sensors. Which engines a stream passes through depends on the use case: a video or perception stream typically goes IFE → IPE, a snapshot IFE → BPS → IPE.

9.2Software and where tuning lives

On Android and Qualcomm Linux the camera stack is CamX (the camera driver framework) with CHI (Camera Hardware Interface) for customisation, the CHI-CDK being the customer-modifiable part. The 3A algorithms (AEC, AWB, AF) run in this stack and select tuning data by the current operating point. Tuning data is delivered as binary files per sensor; an open-source project that converts Qualcomm tuning data to libcamera describes them as com.qti.tuned.<sensor>.bin and com.qti.sensormodule.<sensor>.bin, self-describing binaries with a symbol table and modules identified by name and version (for example cc13 for colour correction, gamma15, lsc34 and lsc35 for lens shading), whose XML schemas Qualcomm publishes. The same project reports that most tables are selected by exposure (lux index), DRC gain and flash state as well as colour temperature, and that AWB uses a set of reference white points per illuminant type. These details are useful for orientation; treat them as third-party observations, not as Qualcomm documentation. Automotive camera stacks on Snapdragon platforms may use a different, automotive-specific camera API and QNX or Linux, but public material indicates the same Spectra hardware and Chromatix-based tuning.

9.3The Chromatix workflow

Qualcomm's public Linux documentation lists the tuning flow in six steps: review the tuning process; install Chromatix and choose the tuning tool (Chromatix Developer Edition or Chromatix 7); capture raw images for IQ tuning; tune the camera in Chromatix; generate the tuning binary; evaluate image quality. That is the same structure as Section 8.2. In practice Chromatix projects are organised by sensor and by module, each module (black level, lens shading, AWB, CCM, noise filters, sharpening, gamma, tone) having its parameter set with trigger regions, plus calibration wizards for the measured parts (shading from flat fields, white points and CCMs from charts under several illuminants). After tuning, the tool generates the binary that the camera stack loads for that sensor. Because the format is versioned per module, a tuning made for one ISP generation or software release may need migration when the platform changes.

In practice

Expect to get Chromatix, the tuning guides and the per-block documentation through your company's agreement with Qualcomm, often together with support from Qualcomm or a design-house tuning team for the first sensor. Job descriptions for camera tuning roles on Qualcomm platforms ask for exactly the skills of this chapter: lab calibration, 3A tuning, noise and sharpness trade-offs, objective metrics with Imatest-type tools and subjective evaluation. The tool is new to you; the method is not.

10Tuning on TI Jacinto and AM6xA platforms

TI's automotive and industrial vision processors (TDA4x/Jacinto 7 and the AM6xA family) contain a hardware ISP in the VPAC (Vision Processing Accelerator). TI publishes application notes that describe the tuning flow in unusual detail, which makes it the best public example of a vendor workflow end to end.

10.1VPAC and the VISS blocks

The VPAC contains the VISS (Vision Imaging Subsystem), which is the ISP proper, and further blocks that follow it: LDC for lens distortion correction (dewarping through a mesh), MSC, a multi-scaler that produces image pyramids, and on some devices a bilateral noise filter (Figure 10.1). TI's TDA4VM tuning overview (SPRACU7A) lists the VISS sub-blocks. The Raw Front End (RAWFE) performs WDR decompanding and merge, defect pixel correction, lens shading correction, white balance and produces the H3A statistics. NSF4 is the fourth-generation raw noise filter. The Flexible Colour Processing block (FCP) contains the CFA interpolation (demosaic), colour correction, gamma and RGB-to-YUV conversion. GLBCE is global and local brightness and contrast enhancement (local tone mapping), and EE is the edge enhancer. The camera pipeline chapter gives TI's throughput figures for the VISS (Camera pipeline 12.1).

10.2DCC: tuning as loadable binaries

TI calls its tuning container DCC (Dynamic Camera Configuration). The tuned parameters are written into VPAC registers through the DCC library in the SDK, and the DCC data is delivered as binaries per sensor and mode, for example dcc_viss.bin for the ISP, dcc_2a.bin for the AE/AWB algorithms and dcc_ldc.bin for distortion correction. The TDA4VM note describes two ways to use them: compiled into the sensor driver (compile-time update) or loaded from the file system at run time. On AM6xA the example paths are under /opt/imaging/<sensor>/. Because the binaries contain parameter sets for several operating points, the same DCC mechanism also carries the gain- and CCT-dependent tables of Section 8.3.

10.3The tuning flow in TI's own words

TI's AM6xA ISP tuning guide (SPRAD86A) describes the procedure in four stages: verify that the camera captures correctly; create an initial ISP configuration with an automated script; tune in the lab under controlled lighting, split into basic tuning and fine tuning; and production tuning on real-world scenes, which the note leaves out. The concrete steps are worth knowing because they are typical:

  1. Sensor integration. Add the sensor driver to the SDK and update the TIOVX and GStreamer VISS modules, including the exposure and gain interface for the 2A algorithm.
  2. Default profile. Write a configuration file with the sensor ID, resolution, Bayer pattern, WDR mode, bit depth and black level; a script (ctt_def_xml_gen.py) generates default XML files per block plus a generate_dcc.sh script, which produces the DCC binaries. Stream with this configuration to confirm a stable image.
  3. Tuning tool project. The PC tuning tool (run as DCC.exe, selecting the VPAC version) opens a project with the raw image properties; plug-ins exist per block and each has its own documentation.
  4. Black level. Capture with the lens covered and read the measured value in the black-level plug-in (TI's example: about 63 for an IMX219 in 10-bit mode).
  5. H3A. Configured by the default script for AE and AWB on fixed-focus cameras; usually no further tuning.
  6. AWB. One well-exposed raw image of an X-Rite ColorChecker per illuminant (the note lists D65, D50, TL84 and A), chart upright and centred; enter the colour temperature and black level, click the chart's four corners, and the tool locates the 24 patches and builds the white-point reference.
  7. Colour correction. Reuse the AWB images and a reference chart image to fit the CCMs.
  8. Fine tuning. Edge enhancement on a YUV capture after colour correction (threshold, slope and gain generate the EE lookup table; gain 0 disables it), and the NSF4 noise filter on a raw chart image, adjusting the threshold curve until the measured noise samples follow the fitted curve, with overall strength at most 1.0.
  9. Export and iterate. Each plug-in exports XML; replace the default XML, rerun generate_dcc.sh and stream again.
Interesting detail

The lab list in TI's note is short and honest: the EVM or a custom board with the camera, a light box with calibrated sources typically covering 2700–6500 K, an X-Rite ColorChecker, a light meter and/or chroma meter, and tripods or mounts. That is enough for a first tuning; Chapter 12 explains what a team adds for full IQ validation.

10.4Live tuning on the target

Both TI notes describe live tuning: the tuning tool connects to the board over the network while a camera pipeline runs. It can capture raw frames (unprocessed sensor output) and YUV frames (the VISS output before LDC and scaling), read the current AE and AWB results or switch them to manual and set exposure, gain, white-balance gains and colour temperature directly, read and write sensor registers (for supported sensors), and send the DCC binary of the plug-in being edited to the board, where it takes effect immediately without restarting the pipeline. Live tuning shortens the loop from minutes to seconds for subjective adjustments, and the manual AE/AWB controls are what you use to make lab captures at a defined exposure and white balance.

11An open reference: the libcamera tuning tool

Vendor tools are licensed and their internals undocumented. The libcamera project, which runs the camera stack on Raspberry Pi and other Linux devices, publishes its tuning tool and its tuning-file format in the open. Running it shows the whole calibration flow with nothing hidden.

11.1What the tool does

The Raspberry Pi Camera Tuning Tool (package rpi-ctt, version 1.4.0 used here) takes DNG raw captures and writes a libcamera JSON tuning file for the Raspberry Pi ISPs (VC4 and the newer PiSP). The captures follow a naming convention that carries the lab conditions: ColorChecker images as <CCT>k_<lux>l.dng, flat fields for lens shading as alsc_<CCT>k_<n>.dng, dark frames as dark_<n>.dng and, optionally, dot-chart images for chromatic aberration. The tool finds the chart in each image by template matching, then calibrates black level, ALSC (its lens shading), the noise profile, green equalisation (GEQ), a lux reference, the AWB colour-temperature curve and the CCMs, and fills the remaining algorithms (AGC, contrast, sharpening, denoise, HDR) from a template.

11.2Running it on the simulated camera

To see real output, the simulated sensor of this chapter was used to render 15 DNG files: the ColorChecker under A (2856 K), F11/TL84 (4000 K), D50 (5003 K) and D65 (6504 K) at a nominal 1000 lux, two flat fields per colour temperature and three dark frames. The tool accepted them unchanged, found all four charts and produced a tuning file with 16 algorithm blocks (Figure 11.1). Its results agree with the simulation's ground truth and with this chapter's own calibrations: black level 4096 in its 16-bit scale (256 DN at 12 bits); an AWB curve with four points from (2856 K, R/G 1.054, B/G 0.489) to (6504 K, 0.580, 0.921), matching Figure 4.1; lens shading tables of 32×32 cells with a luminance strength of 0.8, a maximum luminance gain of 1.95 and colour tables up to 1.10 for red and 1.09 for blue; and four CCMs with mean ΔE improving from 23–25 to 1.3–2.0 and maximum ΔE from 33–37 to 2.8–6.0. Like our own fit, its matrix for warm light is the hardest, with the largest remaining error at 2856 K.

Why it matters

The tool is a compact, readable implementation of everything in Chapters 2–4: chart detection, black level from darks, shading tables from flats, white points from greys, ΔE-optimised matrices per colour temperature. Reading its source and its JSON output is the fastest way to understand what vendor tools do behind their GUIs, and the same structure (calibrations per CCT, interpolation by CCT and lux, priors in AWB) appears in every commercial ISP.

Pitfall

The tool calibrates; it does not tune. AGC targets, contrast curves, denoise strength and sharpening come from templates, and those are exactly the parameters that decide whether the camera is good. Its own documentation also expects real lab conditions: correctly exposed charts that fill a reasonable part of the frame, uniform flats, and the colour temperature and lux in the file names measured, not guessed.

12The IQ lab

Calibration and objective tuning are only as good as the light, charts and instruments behind them. An ISP lab is mostly about control: known light levels and spectra, uniform illumination, charts whose reflectance is certified, and nothing else in the room that adds light. This chapter lists what a team needs and why each item is there.

12.1The room

The lab is a dark room: matt black or dark grey walls and ceiling, no windows, a light-tight door, and no screens or indicator LEDs facing the test area, because stray light lifts black levels and reduces measured contrast. The temperature is kept stable (for example 23 ± 2 °C), since black level, dark current and sometimes focus depend on it. The layout in Figure 12.1 is typical: a chart wall with two lamps at 45° on either side (the standard geometry that avoids specular reflections toward the camera), a camera mounted on a rail or a heavy tripod facing the chart at a distance where the chart fills the specified part of the frame, a light booth for colour work, a uniform source for flat fields, a corner for transmissive and HDR targets, a climate chamber, and a capture PC with the target board.

12.2Light sources and why there are so many

Light booth. A viewing booth with several standard sources: a D65 daylight simulator, a D50 or horizon source, CIE A (incandescent, 2856 K), TL84 and often CWF fluorescent (about 4000 K and 4100 K) and sometimes UV. It is used for white points, CCMs and AWB tuning, because each needs the camera's response under the real spectra the car will meet. Spectral quality matters: a "6500 K" LED panel with a poor spectrum produces different white points from a filtered daylight simulator of the same CCT.

Reflective chart illumination. Two or four lamps (LED or halogen with daylight filters) with dimmers or calibrated intensity control, giving uniform illumination across the chart from about 1 lux to several thousand lux. Low-light performance is measured at defined levels, so the source must reach down to a few lux without changing colour, and the uniformity across the chart should be within a few percent.

Uniform source. An integrating sphere or a high-uniformity LED panel with a diffuser, large enough to fill the lens's field of view, for lens shading calibration, PRNU and photon transfer measurements. For wide-angle and fisheye lenses only a sphere port or a dome really covers the field.

Transmissive and HDR sources. Backlit charts (grey scales with very dense patches, contrast targets) and DC-driven LED sources covering very high luminances are needed to measure dynamic range beyond what reflective charts can provide (a reflective chart spans at most about 30:1 to 100:1). Automotive test suppliers offer flicker-free sources reaching sunlight-like luminance for HDR tests, and programmable PWM LED sources with adjustable frequency and duty cycle for flicker and LFM tests.

Numbers

A white reflective patch under 1000 lux has a luminance of roughly 300 cd/m²; a dark object inside a tunnel may be below 1 cd/m², and the sun's disc is about 1.6 × 109 cd/m². That spread, which exceeds 180 dB from the tunnel to the sun, is why HDR testing needs active transmissive sources and why a reflective chart can never test the full range of an automotive sensor.

Never trust the lamp's setting; measure. A lux meter verifies illuminance at the chart. Fixtures matter as much as instruments: a rail or mount that puts the camera at a repeatable position and orientation, alignment aids (lasers or a reference image) so the chart is centred and square, and a light-tight enclosure for dark frames. A climate chamber (automotive cameras are specified from about −40 °C to +85 °C or beyond) is needed for black level, dark current and hot-pixel characterisation over temperature and for checking focus shift; some labs add a vibration fixture. Finally, software: capture tools for raw and processed frames with full metadata, the vendor's tuning tool, and an analysis package (commercial tools such as Imatest or Image Engineering's iQ-Analyzer, or in-house Python) that computes the metrics of Chapter 13 in a scripted, repeatable way.

In practice

A team starting from zero can get a first tuning done with a light booth, a ColorChecker, a lux meter, a uniform light panel and a sturdy mount, which matches TI's minimal list. Full validation adds the slanted-edge and texture charts, controllable low-light illumination, transmissive HDR targets, flicker sources, a climate chamber and analysis software. Many companies outsource parts (HDR and flicker measurements, IEEE 2020 test campaigns) to test labs that own the expensive sources.

Objective IQ testing turns "the image looks fine" into numbers that can be specified, compared between suppliers and regression-tested. Each metric has a standard procedure that fixes the chart, the light, the camera settings and the analysis, because small differences in any of them change the result.

The standards chapter introduces IEEE 2020's KPI families (Standards 9.1); this chapter focuses on how the measurements are done and what they tell the tuning engineer.

13.2Tone response, noise and dynamic range

The OECF is measured with a grey-scale chart of known densities, evenly illuminated, with exposure and processing as in use (or with a locked AE for repeatability). The mean output of each patch against its luminance gives the tone curve; the standard deviation of each patch gives noise per level, from which SNR curves are plotted. Two pitfalls matter for tuning. Noise must be measured on the output the customer uses, after noise reduction, sharpening and tone mapping, and patches must be large enough that noise reduction and the analysis region do not interact; but the same measurement on raw data gives the sensor's capability, and comparing the two shows how much the ISP adds or removes. And visual noise depends on its spatial frequency: fine-grained noise is less visible than blotchy noise at the same standard deviation, which is why standards define visual noise metrics that weight the noise spectrum by the eye's contrast sensitivity.

Dynamic range is usually defined as the ratio between the brightest unclipped luminance and the luminance at which SNR falls to a threshold (SNR = 1 is the classic choice; stricter thresholds such as SNR = 10 give a "usable" DR). For HDR cameras it is measured with a transmissive chart or a combination of active sources, because no reflective chart spans 120 dB. IEEE 2020 addresses two problems that make naive DR numbers meaningless: flare in the lens raises dark patches when a bright source is in view, and an unknown tone curve hides what the sensor really captured.

13.3Sharpness: the slanted edge

The slanted-edge method (ISO 12233) measures the spatial frequency response from a single edge tilted a few degrees from vertical or horizontal. Because the edge crosses pixel rows at different sub-pixel positions, the rows together sample the edge profile at a much finer spacing than the pixel pitch. The procedure: locate the edge in each row, fit a line, project every pixel onto the distance from that line and average them into four times oversampled bins (the edge spread function), differentiate to get the line spread function, window it and take the Fourier transform. The magnitude, normalised to one at zero frequency, is the SFR. The reference ISP in this chapter implements exactly this (experiments/isp_lib.py) and Figure 13.1 shows its result on the test target of Figure 5.2 for four processing settings.

Common summary numbers are MTF50 (the frequency at which SFR falls to 0.5, in cycles per pixel or line pairs per picture height) and the SFR at a fixed fraction of Nyquist. Both must be read with the processing in mind: a strongly sharpened camera can have a high MTF50 and an SFR above one, which is not "better than perfect" but a measure of edge enhancement. That is why IQ specifications pair an MTF target with limits on overshoot and on SFR above one, and why edge SFR is measured across the field (centre, mid-field, corners), where lens performance and shading-related noise differ.

13.4Colour, white balance and shading

Colour accuracy is measured on a ColorChecker under each standard light, as ΔE00 per patch with mean and maximum, after white balance and CCM, and separately for the white-balance error on the grey patches (as a ΔE in chroma only, or as an angular error like Figure 4.2). For AWB the more important test is in scenes: a set of real or staged scenes under mixed and difficult light, evaluated for grey-patch error where a grey card is in the scene. Shading is measured on a flat field after correction: luminance uniformity (corner over centre, in percent) and colour uniformity (maximum colour difference across the field), at each light source and at low light where partial correction applies.

13.5Texture, flare and other artifacts

Texture loss is measured on a dead-leaves chart: a pattern of overlapping random discs whose power spectrum is known and scale-invariant, like natural textures. The ratio of the measured spectrum to the chart's spectrum gives a texture MTF, which falls when noise reduction smears fine low-contrast texture even if edges stay sharp; noise must be subtracted from the measured spectrum, or noise would masquerade as preserved texture. Flare (veiling glare) is measured with a bright source or a bright field next to black patches (ISO 18844, IEEE 2020's flare KPI): the light scattered in the lens lifts the black patches and reduces contrast near bright objects, which in automotive use means low sun and headlights. Other artifacts are checked with targeted charts and scenes rather than single numbers: moiré and false colour on Siemens stars and fine patterns, halos from tone mapping and sharpening on high-contrast edges, colour fringes near the corners.

13.6Flicker and contrast metrics for automotive

Flicker testing uses programmable LED sources at a range of PWM frequencies (typically from about 90 Hz up to a few kHz) and duty cycles, synchronised or free-running relative to the camera, and evaluates how much the LED's brightness in the image varies from frame to frame and whether it ever disappears. IEEE 2020 defines flicker KPIs for this, and test suppliers offer metrics such as a modulation mitigation probability (the probability that the LED's modulation is suppressed below a threshold). The test is run in each sensor mode and at several ambient light levels, because the camera's exposure, and with it the mitigation, changes with the scene.

The contrast metrics address the question perception teams care about: can a given contrast still be detected after noise and processing? Contrast detection probability (CDP) takes two patches with a known nominal contrast, computes the contrast for many random pixel pairs (one from each patch) on the processed output, and reports the share of pairs whose contrast falls within a tolerance interval around the nominal value. Noise spreads the pixel distributions and processing can shift them, so CDP falls in dark, noisy conditions even when the mean contrast is preserved. Figure 13.2 illustrates it with two simulated patch pairs of nominal 20 % contrast: with little noise 92 % of pairs fall within ±50 % of the nominal contrast; with three times the noise only 44 % do. Image Engineering's material also names contrast transfer accuracy and contrast signal-to-noise ratio as IEEE 2020 contrast metrics.

14Validation, debugging and your first months

A tuning is never finished in the lab. It is validated on scenes and drives, debugged when someone reports a problem, and maintained across hardware changes until the end of production. This chapter collects the practices and the symptom-to-cause knowledge that make that efficient.

14.1Validation beyond the lab

Lab KPIs prove that the tuning meets its specification on charts. Validation asks whether that specification was the right one. For viewing cameras, structured drives cover the scene classes that matter (day, dusk, night with and without street lighting, tunnels, low sun, rain and spray, snow, LED-lit urban scenes, car parks with fluorescent lighting) and reviewers rate each class against the previous release and competitor references. For perception cameras the validation is the perception KPIs on recorded raw data reprocessed with the new tuning, together with targeted tests at the conditions where the camera is weakest: low light, high gain, high temperature, strong flare. Units from several production lots and the temperature extremes are part of validation, because a tuning that is perfect on one golden sample may not be on the population.

In practice

Debugging starts with the raw frame and the metadata, never with the processed image. Capture raw at the failing condition, confirm what the sensor delivered (exposure, gain, black level, clipping), then run the frame through the ISP simulator with the release tuning and switch blocks off one at a time. Most problems are found in the first two steps, and the ones that remain are reproducible offline, which makes them fixable.

14.3Production: what tuning hands over

Tuning is done on a few golden samples; production builds millions of modules with spread in lens shading, filter transmission and sensor sensitivity. The ISP engineer defines which calibrations are design-level (in the tuning file) and which are per unit (measured at the camera supplier's end of line and stored in the module's EEPROM or the sensor's OTP): typically lens shading or a correction to it, white-balance ratios under one or two lights, and the defect map, plus the geometric intrinsics, which belong to a different team (Camera geometry 6.2). The ISP software must read and apply the per-unit data at start-up and fall back safely if it is missing or corrupt. The EOL procedures themselves are part of topic 8. Over the production life, tuning updates follow hardware changes (a second-source lens, a new sensor revision) and field findings, each with the regression process of Section 8.5.

14.4Your first months as an ISP engineer

A practical path for someone new to the role: learn to read raw files and their metadata in Python and compute means, variances and histograms per Bayer channel; measure a photon transfer curve on a real sensor; calibrate black level, shading, white points and a CCM for one camera, ideally on an open platform such as a Raspberry Pi camera with the libcamera tool, and compare your results with the tool's; tune one block at a time on charts with an objective metric next to the image; then sit with the people who consume the images, the display reviewers and the perception team, to learn what they actually need. The vendor tools, Chromatix or TI's DCC tool, are learned on the job; the measurement discipline and the understanding of trade-offs are what make a tuning engineer trusted.

In production we split calibration into design-level data in the tuning file and per-unit data from end of line, and every tuning update goes through the same raw-library regression before release."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

Walk me through the ISP pipeline from raw to YUV.Basic

Decompanding of HDR data, black level subtraction, defect pixel correction, HDR merge if the sensor sends separate exposures, lens shading correction, white balance gains, raw noise filtering, demosaic, colour correction matrix, then in luma-chroma space noise reduction, tone mapping/local contrast, sharpening, gamma and conversion to YUV, followed by scaling and distortion correction. Raw-domain blocks repair the sensor and lens while values are linear; later blocks shape the look. Statistics for 3A are taken early and drive exposure, white balance and tone.

What is the difference between calibration and tuning?Basic

Calibration parameters are measured and have a correct value: black level, noise model, defect map, lens shading tables, white points, colour matrices. Tuning parameters are chosen as trade-offs: noise reduction strength, sharpening, tone curves, AE targets and speeds, CCM saturation in low light. Calibration comes first, because tuning on top of a wrong calibration hides errors that later reappear.

How do you measure the black level and the read noise of a sensor?Basic

Capture several frames with no light (lens capped, dark box), at each gain and over temperature. The mean per Bayer channel is the black level; the standard deviation of the difference of two dark frames divided by √2 is the temporal read noise in DN, which the conversion gain converts to electrons. Also check whether the sensor clamps black level with optically black rows and whether it drifts with temperature.

What is a photon transfer curve and what do you get from it?Intermediate

Noise against mean signal from pairs of flat fields over the whole exposure range. In the shot-noise region variance grows linearly with the mean; the slope is the conversion gain K in DN per electron. The floor is the read noise, the peak of the variance marks saturation, and full well over read noise is the dynamic range. It needs no datasheet, and it gives the noise model the ISP's noise filters use. On the simulated sensor it returned K = 0.420 DN/e− (true 0.427) and 69 dB DR at gain 1.

How is lens shading calibrated, and why not correct it fully?Intermediate

Capture averaged flat fields from a uniform source at several colour temperatures, compute per-channel gain meshes as centre value over cell value, and interpolate between mesh points at run time. Full luminance correction multiplies corner noise by the same gain (1.88× in the simulated wide lens), so colour shading is corrected fully and luminance partially (about 70–85 %), with less correction at high gain.

How does AWB work and when does it fail?Intermediate

The ISP reports zone statistics; AWB keeps zones whose chromaticity lies in the calibrated grey zone around the white-point locus and searches for the light that makes most of them neutral, weighted by brightness and by priors that depend on lux (daylight is likely outdoors). It fails when nothing grey is visible or when large coloured areas fall into the grey zone: in the simulation, grey search was within 0.2° on scenes with some grey but 4.9–11.3° off on a scene with only foliage and sky. Grey world fails on any dominant colour (30° on a red bus).

How do you derive a colour correction matrix?Intermediate

Photograph a ColorChecker under the light, apply black level, shading and white balance, average each patch, and fit a 3×3 matrix to the patches' reference values in linear sRGB. Constrain each row to sum to one so white stays white, and minimise a perceptual error (ΔE00) rather than RGB least squares. Repeat for each calibrated light and interpolate by CCT. On the simulation the D65 matrix reduced mean ΔE00 from 6.7 to 1.0.

Why does a CCM increase noise, and what do you do about it?Intermediate

Each output channel is a weighted sum of input channels with large positive and negative weights; for independent noise the output noise grows with the length of the matrix row, about 1.5–1.8× for typical matrices. In low light ISPs blend the matrix toward identity or a less saturated one, trading colour accuracy for noise, and chroma noise reduction cleans up what remains.

Compare bilinear and edge-directed demosaicing.Basic

Bilinear averages neighbours of the missing colour regardless of structure, which misaligns colours across edges and creates zippers and false colour. Edge-directed methods interpolate along edges and use colour differences, which are smoother than the channels. On a zone plate bilinear reached 16 dB PSNR with strong false colour, Malvar-He-Cutler 27.5 dB, a directional method 37.9 dB. On coarse content the difference is small.

How do you tune noise reduction against sharpness?Intermediate

Per gain step on charts: measure SNR on flat patches, SFR on a slanted edge, texture on a dead-leaves chart and overshoot, and choose settings that meet all limits, not one. Tie NR thresholds to the noise model so a strength means the same at every brightness, use coring and overshoot limits in sharpening, and then check real scenes. Sharpening without NR raised SFR but dropped SNR from 21.8 to 15.4 dB in the experiment.

What is local tone mapping and what artifacts does it cause?Intermediate

It compresses large-scale illumination differences while keeping local detail, usually by splitting log luminance into a smooth base and a detail layer and compressing only the base. A non-edge-aware base creates halos (dark or bright bands next to strong edges); strong settings amplify shadow noise, look unnatural and break brightness constancy, which matters for perception. Edge-aware filters and rate limits over time are the standard countermeasures.

How does auto exposure converge, and why can it oscillate?Intermediate

Each frame AE compares metered luma with a target and scales exposure by (target/measured)speed. Exposure changes reach the image one to two frames later; a controller that ignores corrections already in flight applies the same correction several times and overshoots. In the model, speed 0.8 with a 2-frame delay crossed the target 9 times. Fixes: account for pending changes, damp the step, use a dead band with hysteresis, and use the histogram to take larger steps when pixels clip.

What is antibanding and why doesn't it solve LED flicker?Basic

Mains lamps flicker at twice the mains frequency; with a rolling shutter, exposures that are not whole multiples of the flicker period make rows integrate different light, giving rolling bands. Antibanding quantises exposure to 10 ms (50 Hz) or 8.33 ms (60 Hz). LEDs are PWM-driven at many frequencies and duty cycles and short daylight exposures can miss the on-pulse entirely, so they need sensor LFM modes, not exposure quantisation.

What charts and lights do you need for a first tuning?Basic

A light booth with D65, D50 or horizon, TL84/CWF and A; a ColorChecker; a uniform source for flat fields; a lux meter and ideally a chroma meter or spectroradiometer; a stable mount. For full validation add slanted-edge and dead-leaves charts, OECF/grey-scale charts (transmissive for HDR), controllable low-light illumination, PWM LED sources and a climate chamber.

Explain the slanted-edge MTF measurement.Intermediate

An edge tilted a few degrees crosses rows at different sub-pixel offsets; projecting all pixels onto the distance from the fitted edge line gives an oversampled edge spread function. Differentiating gives the line spread function; its windowed Fourier magnitude, normalised at zero frequency, is the SFR. Report MTF50 or SFR at a fraction of Nyquist, together with overshoot, because sharpening can push the SFR above one.

What is IEEE 2020 and how does it differ from ISO camera standards?Basic

IEEE 2020-2024, published in 2025, is the automotive system image quality standard. It defines test methods and KPIs for flare, geometric calibration validation, noise, dynamic range, SFR, flicker and contrast performance, for both human viewing and computer vision. ISO standards (12233, 14524, 15739, 17321, 18844) were written for consumer photography; IEEE 2020 handles HDR, unknown tone curves, LED flicker and contrast detectability in noise, which matter for automotive perception.

How does tuning for a neural network differ from tuning for a display?Intermediate

Display tuning optimises appearance: natural colour, low visible noise, pleasant contrast, crispness. Perception tuning optimises information and stability: preserved small contrasts in dark regions, no invented edges from sharpening, mild and rate-limited tone mapping, stable AE and AWB, consistency across units and temperature. The process differs too: KPIs include detection metrics, the tuning is frozen before data recording, and later changes are treated as a domain shift.

Describe the tuning workflow on TI Jacinto/AM6xA.Advanced

Integrate the sensor driver, generate a default DCC profile with TI's script (XML per block, then dcc_viss.bin, dcc_2a.bin, dcc_ldc.bin), stream to confirm. In the PC tuning tool tune black level from a covered-lens capture, keep the default H3A, calibrate AWB and CCM from ColorChecker raw captures under D65, D50, TL84 and A, then fine-tune EE and the NSF4 noise curve. Export XML, regenerate the binaries, and use live tuning to push DCC updates to the running board without a restart.

What do you know about Qualcomm camera tuning?Advanced

The Spectra ISP has an Image Front End (plus IFE-lite units) for capture, first processing and statistics, a Bayer Processing Segment and an Image Processing Engine for YUV processing. The camera stack is CamX/CHI with 3A that looks up tuning by operating point. Tuning is done in Chromatix (Developer Edition or Chromatix 7): capture raw, calibrate and tune modules with trigger regions over lux index/gain and CCT, generate the tuning binary, evaluate. Details of the tools and blocks come under NDA, so I'd confirm specifics on the SDK in use.

How do you manage tuning files across a project?Intermediate

As released software: editable source (XML/JSON, calibration data) in version control, binaries built from it, a version string reported in camera metadata, release notes with what changed and why, and a regression report from reprocessing a fixed raw library on the vendor's bit-exact simulator. For perception, the tuning version is stored with each recorded sample so datasets are traceable.

A customer reports a green cast in a car park. How do you debug it?Advanced

Get a raw capture with metadata from the scene. Check the CCT and gains AWB chose, and whether the light is fluorescent or LED with a green-shifted spectrum off the blackbody locus. If AWB snapped to a daylight point, the grey zone or the fluorescent reference points and priors at that lux level need work; if AWB was right, check the CCM interpolation at that CCT and gain. Reproduce offline in the simulator, fix, and add the scene to the regression library.

Q.2Why and what if

Why must black level be subtracted before white balance?Why

White balance multiplies R and B by different gains. Any offset left in the data is multiplied too, so a remaining offset becomes a colour cast that is strongest in dark areas, where the offset is large relative to the signal. Subtracting first keeps the gains acting on light only.

Why is raw noise reduction easier than noise reduction after demosaic?Why

In the raw mosaic each pixel's noise is independent and follows the measured noise model exactly. Demosaic and the CCM mix neighbouring pixels and channels, so noise becomes spatially correlated and coloured and its level no longer follows a simple curve, which makes it harder to separate from texture.

Why does the measured full well come out below the true full well in a PTC?Why

Saturation is taken at the variance peak. As the mean approaches full well, the brightest pixels of the noisy distribution clip first and stop fluctuating, so the variance starts to fall while the mean is still below full well. The simulated sensor with 9000 e− showed its peak at 7761 e−, which is the usable maximum.

Why can't a smarter threshold alone fix dynamic defect correction at night?Why

Because a distant light blurred onto one pixel and a hot pixel look the same to a same-colour comparison: both are one bright pixel among dim same-colour neighbours. Any threshold that removes hot pixels removes such lights. The information that separates them is spatial: a real light also brightens the adjacent pixels of other colours through the lens blur.

Why does a colour matrix fitted under D65 give worse colours under incandescent light?Why

White balance only fixes neutrals. The camera's spectral mismatch with the target colour space affects saturated colours differently when the light's spectrum changes, so the best matrix depends on the light. On the simulation the D65 matrix under A gave mean ΔE00 4.4 against 2.1 for a matrix fitted under A, hence matrices per CCT with interpolation.

Why do fluorescent lights need their own AWB reference points?Why

Their spectra have strong lines and their white points lie off the blackbody/daylight curve, toward green. An AWB that only searches along the blackbody locus would pick a nearby daylight point and leave a green or magenta cast.

What if you correct lens shading at 100 % at gain 16?What if

The corners become as bright as the centre but their noise is multiplied by the shading gain (around 2× for a wide lens), so the corners look visibly noisier, and a noise filter tuned on the centre under-filters them. SNR in the corners does not change, only visibility. Partial correction at high gain, or feeding the shading gain into the noise filter, avoids it.

What if AE has a two-frame delay and you set its speed to 1?What if

A naive controller corrects the full error, sees no change in the next frame because its correction is still in flight, corrects again, and overshoots; the loop oscillates and, in the chapter's model, never settles. A delay-aware controller that corrects the exposure that produced the measured frame settles in about 6 frames at the same speed.

What if the tuning changes after the perception dataset was recorded?What if

The deployed network sees inputs from a different distribution than its training data: different colour, contrast, noise texture or sharpness. Performance can drop in ways that only show on specific conditions. The change needs an impact evaluation on reprocessed data and possibly retraining; recording raw data lets you regenerate the dataset with the new tuning.

What if you tune noise reduction only to maximise SNR on the grey patch?What if

You get a plastic image: flat areas are clean, but fine low-contrast texture (asphalt, grass, distant pedestrians' clothing) is removed with the noise, and at strong settings even edges soften. Texture MTF on a dead-leaves chart and contrast-detection metrics catch this; SNR alone rewards it.

What if the light booth's D65 lamp has aged?What if

Its spectrum and CCT shift, so the measured white point and the CCM fitted under it are wrong for real daylight, and every camera calibrated in that booth inherits the error. That is why sources are measured with a spectroradiometer or chroma meter on a schedule and lamps replaced by hours of use.

Why do interpolation boundaries in the parameter tables need hysteresis?Why

The operating point (gain, lux index, CCT) fluctuates with noise and scene changes. Without hysteresis, a scene near a boundary switches between two parameter sets frame by frame, and the image visibly pumps in noise, sharpness or colour.

What if the Bayer order in the driver is wrong by one pixel?What if

Red and blue (or green and red) are swapped in demosaic: colours are wildly wrong, white balance gains are applied to the wrong pixels and a maze or checkerboard appears. It looks like a catastrophic tuning failure but is a configuration error, which is why bring-up checks Bayer order with a coloured object before any tuning.

Why measure noise on the processed output and not only on raw?Why

The consumer sees the processed output, after noise reduction, sharpening and tone mapping, which can change noise by several dB and change its texture. Raw measurements tell you what the sensor can do; processed measurements tell you what the camera delivers; the difference tells you what the ISP added or removed.

Why does an SFR above one not mean a better lens?Why

A lens cannot increase contrast at any frequency. SFR above one comes from sharpening, which boosts edge contrast and creates overshoot; it improves apparent crispness but also amplifies noise and can create false edges. That's why sharpness specs pair MTF targets with overshoot and noise limits.
