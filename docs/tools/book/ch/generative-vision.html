Generative and vision-language models

How models that make images (VAEs, GANs, diffusion and flow models) and models that connect images with language (CLIP, vision-language models, open-vocabulary detectors and SAM) work, what each part is for, and where each one breaks. Three running scenarios carry the chapter: a text-to-image product with editing, a VLM that reads documents and screenshots, and a perception team that auto-labels a new object class and generates rare scenes.

1The big picture: models that make images and models that read them

Until about 2020 computer vision meant discriminative models: an image goes in, a label, a box or a mask comes out. Two things changed the field since then. Models learned to generate convincing images, and models learned to connect images with free-form language. Both rest on a small number of ideas, and once those ideas are clear the product names (Stable Diffusion, FLUX, CLIP, LLaVA, Qwen-VL, Grounding DINO, SAM) stop being a zoo.

1.1A generative model learns a distribution, not an answer

A classifier answers one question: given this image, which label? It only has to learn the boundary between classes. A generative model has to learn something much bigger: what images of this kind look like at all, in the form of a probability distribution \(p(x)\) over images, or a conditional one \(p(x \mid c)\) given a text prompt, a class or a sketch. Learning a distribution is useful in two ways. You can sample from it (draw a new image that looks like it came from the training data), and you can use the knowledge inside it for other tasks (fill a hole in a photo, remove noise, judge whether an image is plausible).

The difficulty is the size of the space. A 512 × 512 colour image is a point in a space with 786 432 dimensions. Almost every point in that space is noise; real photos occupy a thin, curved region of it, often called the data manifold. A generative model has to learn where that region is and how probability mass is spread over it, from a finite set of examples. Every family in this chapter is a different answer to the question "how do we represent and sample such a distribution without ever writing it down?"

Conditional generation adds a second axis. "A red fox in snow, golden hour" names a tiny sub-region of the image space, and the model must land inside it. That needs a way to turn text into numbers that the image model understands, which is where the image-text models of the second half of the chapter come in: the text encoder of CLIP was, for several years, the part of Stable Diffusion that understood the prompt.

Why it matters

The same mathematical object, a learned distribution over images, sits behind image generators, inpainting, super-resolution, denoising in camera pipelines, synthetic training data and the "world models" used in robotics and driving research. Understanding how it is learned and sampled explains both the impressive results and the typical failures: blur, missing modes, extra fingers, ignored prompt words, invented objects.

1.2Three running scenarios

The chapter follows three teams. Each uses a different part of the field, and each shows where the models work and where they fail.

A text-to-image product. A design tool lets users type a prompt, get four images in a few seconds, then edit them: change the background, replace an object, keep a person's pose, apply the company's illustration style. Under the hood it is a latent diffusion model with classifier-free guidance, an inpainting variant, ControlNet for pose and layout, and LoRA adapters for styles. Its failure modes are prompt words that get ignored, text in images that comes out garbled, too little variety at high guidance, and cost per image.

A VLM that reads documents and screenshots. An expense-processing service receives photos of receipts and PDF invoices; a support tool receives screenshots of a mobile app and must say which button the user should press. A vision-language model turns the image into visual tokens, an LLM reads them together with the question and answers with text, numbers and box coordinates. Its failure modes are small text it cannot resolve, numbers it confidently invents, and boxes that point at the wrong element.

A perception team that needs a new object class. A team that builds a detector for delivery robots and city vehicles must add e-scooters, which were not in the original label set, and must cover rare situations such as a scooter lying across a bike lane at night. It auto-labels fleet images with an open-vocabulary detector and SAM, has humans review a part of them, and uses a diffusion model to insert scooters into real scenes. Its failure modes are confident wrong labels, prompt sensitivity, and synthetic images whose labels do not match what was generated.

1.3The map: four ways to generate, three ways to connect image and text

Figure 1.1 is the map for the rest of the chapter. The top row shows the four families that generate images. A VAE compresses an image into a small latent code and decodes it back; sampling a code from a simple Gaussian and decoding it produces a new image. A GAN trains a generator against a discriminator until the generator's images cannot be told apart from real ones. An autoregressive model cuts the image into discrete tokens and predicts them one after the other, like a language model predicts words. A diffusion or flow model learns to remove noise, then creates an image by starting from pure noise and denoising step by step.

The bottom row connects images with language. CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder so that an image and its caption land close together in a shared vector space. A vision-language model (VLM) feeds an image encoder's output into a large language model as extra tokens, so the LLM can talk about the image. Open-vocabulary detectors and promptable segmenters (SAM) find and outline whatever a text or a click asks for. Vision-language-action models (VLA) take a VLM and make it output actions instead of words.

No family wins on everything. The usual way to compare them is the "generative trilemma": sample quality, sample diversity (mode coverage) and sampling speed. GANs are fast and sharp but tend to drop modes; VAEs are fast and cover the data but blur; diffusion models are sharp and diverse but need many network evaluations per image. Most of the engineering in the last five years has been about escaping the trilemma, for example by distilling a diffusion model into a few-step generator with a GAN loss.

2Autoencoders and variational autoencoders

Autoencoders exist because raw pixels are a wasteful representation: neighbouring pixels are strongly correlated, and most of the 786 432 numbers of a photo can be predicted from a much smaller description. Compressing images into a compact latent space makes everything after it cheaper, and the variational autoencoder adds the one property that makes such a space usable for generation: you can sample from it.

2.1An autoencoder compresses through a bottleneck

An autoencoder is two networks trained together. The encoder maps an image to a short vector (or a small grid of vectors), the latent code \(z\); the decoder maps the code back to an image. The training signal is only "make the output equal to the input". Because the code is much smaller than the image, the networks cannot simply copy; they have to learn which structure in the data is worth keeping. A plain autoencoder learns a good compressor, and its latent space is often used for retrieval, anomaly detection (a frame the decoder cannot reconstruct well is unusual) and denoising.

It is not a generator, though. Nothing in the training says what the latent space looks like between the codes of training images. If you pick a random \(z\), or interpolate halfway between two codes, the decoder may produce garbage, because that region of the latent space was never used. The codes of real images can sit on scattered islands with empty sea in between.

2.2The VAE makes the latent space samplable

The variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling, 2013) fixes this with two changes, shown in Figure 2.1. First, the encoder does not output one code but a small Gaussian distribution: a mean \(\mu(x)\) and a spread \(\sigma(x)\) per latent dimension. During training a code is sampled from that Gaussian, so nearby codes must all decode to similar images, which fills the sea between the islands. Second, a penalty term, the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the encoder's Gaussian and a standard normal \(N(0, I)\), pulls all the little Gaussians towards the origin and towards unit spread. Together they force the codes of the whole dataset to fill a ball-shaped region without holes. To generate, you throw the encoder away, draw \(z \sim N(0, I)\) and run the decoder.

Sampling inside a network creates a technical problem: you cannot back-propagate through a random draw. The reparameterisation trick moves the randomness out of the way. Instead of "sample \(z\) from \(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\)", the network computes \(z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\) drawn from \(N(0, I)\) as an external input. Now \(z\) is a differentiable function of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and gradients flow to the encoder. The loss is the negative of the evidence lower bound (ELBO), a lower bound on the log-likelihood of the data:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)}\big[-\log p(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \beta\, \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,N(0, I)\big)}_{\text{keep the latent space regular}} \]

With a Gaussian decoder the reconstruction term becomes a squared error, and the KL term has a closed form for two Gaussians. In PyTorch the whole thing fits in a few lines:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in=784, d_z=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_in, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 2 * d_z))
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_z, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, d_in))

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.enc(x).chunk(2, dim=-1)
        z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)   # reparameterisation trick
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar

def vae_loss(x, x_hat, mu, logvar, beta=1.0):
    rec = F.mse_loss(x_hat, x, reduction='sum') / x.shape[0]
    kl = -0.5 * torch.sum(1 + logvar - mu ** 2 - logvar.exp()) / x.shape[0]
    return rec + beta * kl
Interesting detail

The weight \(\beta\) is a dial between two goods. A large \(\beta\) (the β-VAE) gives a very regular latent space whose dimensions often line up with single factors of variation (pose, lighting, size), at the price of worse reconstructions. A tiny \(\beta\) gives sharp reconstructions but a latent space that is no longer close to a Gaussian, so you cannot sample it directly. The autoencoder of Stable Diffusion uses a KL weight of about 10⁻⁶: it is a VAE in name, but in practice a compressor whose latent space a diffusion model learns to sample instead.

2.3Why VAE samples are blurry

VAE samples look soft, and the reason is worth understanding because the same reason appears again in diffusion models and in every regression network. The decoder is trained with a squared error. When the latent code does not fully determine the image, for example the exact position of a hair or of an edge, the output that minimises the expected squared error is the average of all images consistent with the code. Averaging sharp images with slightly different edge positions gives a grey ramp (Figure 2.2). Fine detail is exactly the part of the image that the small latent code cannot pin down, so fine detail is what gets averaged away.

The fixes all replace "be close on average" with "look real". The autoencoders used for latent diffusion are trained with a perceptual loss (distances between deep features instead of pixels) plus an adversarial loss from a patch discriminator; together these push the decoder to commit to a plausible texture rather than an average. Another fix is to keep the VAE for compression only and let a stronger generative model (diffusion) choose the latent code.

Pitfall: posterior collapse

If the decoder is powerful enough to model the data without help (an autoregressive decoder is the classic case), the cheapest way to minimise the loss is to make the KL term zero: the encoder outputs \(N(0, I)\) for every input and the decoder ignores \(z\). The latent code then carries no information. The symptom is a KL term near zero early in training. Remedies are KL warm-up (start with \(\beta = 0\) and raise it), a "free bits" floor per dimension, or a weaker decoder.

2.4VQ-VAE and the autoencoder's job today: the image tokenizer

A VQ-VAE (vector-quantised VAE, van den Oord et al., 2017) replaces the continuous latent by a grid of entries from a learned codebook: each latent vector is snapped to its nearest codebook vector, so an image becomes a grid of integer token ids, for example 32 × 32 tokens from a vocabulary of 8 192. That turns an image into something a transformer can model exactly like text, which is how the first autoregressive text-to-image models (DALL·E in 2021, Parti in 2022) worked, and how some natively multimodal LLMs emit images today.

The continuous version won for diffusion. The autoencoder of Stable Diffusion 1.x and 2.x maps a 512 × 512 × 3 image to a 64 × 64 × 4 latent (8× downsampling in each direction, 48× fewer numbers). SDXL keeps the 4 channels at 1024 × 1024 (a 128 × 128 × 4 latent); Stable Diffusion 3 and FLUX moved to 16 channels, which keeps more detail (text, small faces) at the cost of a harder latent for the diffusion model to learn. In 2026 almost nobody samples from a VAE directly, but nearly every image and video generator contains one, as the component that defines the space the generator works in.

Talk track

"An autoencoder compresses an image into a small code and decodes it back. A VAE adds noise to the code during training and a KL penalty that keeps all codes inside a Gaussian ball, so you can sample a random code and decode it. On its own it gives blurry images, because squared error averages away detail it cannot pin down. Its main job today is the compressor of latent diffusion: Stable Diffusion runs its diffusion process on a 64 × 64 × 4 VAE latent instead of 512 × 512 pixels."

3GANs and their training dynamics

Generative adversarial networks exist because squared-error losses produce blur, and nobody knows how to write down a loss for "looks like a real photo". Goodfellow and colleagues' idea (2014) was to learn that loss: train a second network to tell real from generated images, and use its judgement as the training signal. For about six years GANs produced the sharpest images of any method, and their training problems explain much of why the field then moved to diffusion.

3.1The game between generator and discriminator

The generator \(G\) maps a random vector \(z\) (for example 512 numbers from a Gaussian) to an image in one forward pass. The discriminator \(D\) takes an image and outputs the probability that it is real. Training alternates: one step updates \(D\) to label a batch of real images as real and a batch of generated images as fake; the next step updates \(G\) so that \(D\) labels its images as real (Figure 3.1). The generator never sees a training image. Everything it learns about the data comes through the gradient of the discriminator's verdict.

Formally it is a minimax game, \(\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]\). Two details matter in practice. First, the generator is not trained on \(\log(1 - D(G(z)))\) as written, because that term is flat when the discriminator confidently rejects the fakes, which is exactly the situation at the start of training. The standard "non-saturating" generator loss maximises \(\log D(G(z))\) instead, which has strong gradients when the fakes are bad. Second, if the discriminator were trained to optimality at each step, the generator would be minimising the Jensen–Shannon divergence between the data distribution and its own. That connection is what explains the instability.

3.2Why GAN training is unstable

Real images live on a thin manifold in pixel space, and early in training the generator's images live on a different thin manifold. Two thin manifolds in a space with hundreds of thousands of dimensions almost never overlap. When the two distributions do not overlap, a discriminator can separate them perfectly, and the Jensen–Shannon divergence is stuck at its maximum value, \(\log 2\), no matter how far apart they are. A constant has no gradient: the generator learns nothing about which direction to move. Figure 3.2 shows this with two narrow one-dimensional Gaussians: as soon as the generated distribution is about six standard deviations away from the real one, the JS curve is flat (its slope falls below 0.14 at θ = 0.3 and is 0.0001 at θ = 0.5), while the Wasserstein distance, the "earth mover's" cost of moving one distribution onto the other, keeps growing linearly.

Three practical failure modes follow from the game structure. Vanishing gradients: a discriminator that becomes too good too fast leaves the generator without signal. Mode collapse: the generator finds a few outputs that fool the current discriminator and produces only those (all faces with the same pose, all digits a "1"); the discriminator then learns to reject them, the generator jumps to another small set, and the two chase each other around without converging. Nothing in the loss rewards covering all the modes of the data, only fooling the discriminator. Oscillation: simultaneous gradient steps on a two-player game can circle around the equilibrium instead of reaching it, the way two people adjusting a shared thermostat in opposite directions never settle.

Pitfall: the losses do not tell you how training is going

In classification a falling loss means progress. In a GAN the two losses measure a game against a moving opponent: a generator loss that goes up can mean the discriminator got better, not that the images got worse. Teams monitor sample grids and a sample-quality metric such as FID (Section 11.1) every few thousand steps, and keep checkpoints, because a run that looked fine can collapse late.

3.3What made GANs work, and where they still win

A series of fixes made GANs reliable enough for products between 2017 and 2020. The Wasserstein GAN replaced the discriminator by a "critic" whose output estimates the Wasserstein distance, which has useful gradients even without overlap; it needs the critic to be Lipschitz-continuous, enforced first by clipping weights and then by a gradient penalty (WGAN-GP). Spectral normalisation enforces the same smoothness by normalising each layer's weights by their largest singular value. The R1 penalty penalises the discriminator's gradient on real images. Architecture mattered as much: progressive growing (train at low resolution first, then add layers), and StyleGAN (2019), whose mapping network and per-layer style inputs produced the photorealistic faces that made "this person does not exist" famous.

Diffusion models overtook GANs on diversity and on text-conditioned generation around 2021 to 2022. GANs did not disappear; they moved inside other systems, wherever a single fast pass is needed or where "make it look real" has to be added to another loss:

  • the adversarial loss that keeps the VAE decoder of latent diffusion sharp (Section 2.3);
  • super-resolution and restoration models (Real-ESRGAN and successors) that must run in milliseconds;
  • distilling a diffusion model into a generator that needs one to four steps: adversarial diffusion distillation, used by SDXL-Turbo, trains the student with a GAN loss plus a distillation loss (Section 4.4);
  • real-time avatars, face animation and style transfer on phones.
Talk track: diffusion versus GAN

"A GAN is one network pass from noise to image, trained as a game against a discriminator. That makes it fast and sharp, but the game is unstable, and since nothing in the loss rewards covering every mode, GANs tend to drop parts of the data: mode collapse. A diffusion model is trained with a plain regression loss, denoise this image, which is stable and covers the whole distribution, but sampling takes 20 to 50 network passes. Today diffusion is the default for quality and controllability, and GAN losses are used to make diffusion fast again through distillation."

4Diffusion models and flow matching

Diffusion models exist because both earlier routes had a fatal flaw: VAEs blur, GANs are unstable and drop modes. Diffusion turns generation into a long sequence of small, easy regression problems: "here is a slightly noisy image, predict the noise". Each problem is trained with a plain squared error, which is as stable as training gets, and chaining many small denoising steps produces sharp, diverse images. The price is paid at sampling time, in network evaluations.

4.1The forward process: destroying an image on purpose

Start with a training image \(x_0\) and add a little Gaussian noise, then a little more, for \(T = 1000\) steps, until nothing of the image is left (Figure 4.1). This forward process is fixed, not learned. Its useful property is that you do not have to run the steps one by one: the noisy image at any step \(t\) has a closed form,

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim N(0, I), \tag{4.1}\]

where \(\bar\alpha_t\) falls from almost 1 at \(t = 1\) to almost 0 at \(t = T\). The image is scaled down while the noise is scaled up, so the total variance stays about constant ("variance preserving"). The ratio \(\bar\alpha_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\) is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of step \(t\), and the noise schedule is simply the choice of how SNR falls with \(t\).

The original DDPM paper (Ho et al., 2020) used a linear schedule of per-step noise from \(\beta_1 = 10^{-4}\) to \(\beta_T = 0.02\). Figure 4.2 compares it with two others. The choice matters more than it looks, because the interesting part of generation happens in a band of SNR values: the experiment of Section 4.3 shows the global layout being decided between ln SNR ≈ −8 and −2. A schedule that rushes through that band wastes steps elsewhere. The cosine schedule (Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021) spends more steps at mid SNR. Stable Diffusion 1.x to XL used a "scaled linear" schedule that ends at SNR 0.0047 instead of zero: at the last step about 7 % of the image amplitude survives, so the model never learns to start from pure noise. Since the sampler does start from pure noise, the model is always nudged towards average brightness, which is why those models struggle to make very dark night scenes or pure white backgrounds (Lin et al., 2024, "Common diffusion noise schedules and sample steps are flawed").

4.2The reverse process: learning to denoise

If we could run the forward process backwards, we would turn noise into images. For small steps the reverse of "add a little Gaussian noise" is itself approximately Gaussian, and its mean depends on one unknown thing: which clean image the noisy one came from. A neural network is trained to supply that missing piece. Training is remarkably simple:

  1. take a training image \(x_0\) and pick a random step \(t\);
  2. draw noise \(\varepsilon\) and form \(x_t\) with Equation 4.1, in one jump;
  3. ask the network \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)\) to predict the noise that was added, given the noisy image, the step and the condition \(c\) (for example the text embedding);
  4. minimise the squared error \(\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) \rVert^2\).
T = 1000
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)
abar = torch.cumprod(1 - betas, dim=0)

def diffusion_loss(model, x0, cond):
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (x0.shape[0],))
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    a = abar[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
    xt = a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps                 # jump straight to step t
    drop = torch.rand(x0.shape[0]) < 0.1                       # 10 % without condition, for CFG (4.5)
    cond = torch.where(drop.view(-1, 1, 1), torch.zeros_like(cond), cond)
    return F.mse_loss(model(xt, t, cond), eps)                # predict the added noise

Predicting the noise and predicting the clean image are the same thing in disguise: given \(x_t\) and \(\varepsilon\), Equation 4.1 gives \(x_0\), and vice versa. Models differ in which one they output (\(\varepsilon\)-prediction in DDPM and SD 1.x, "v-prediction", a mix of both, in SD 2.x, velocity in flow models), because the choice changes how the loss is weighted across noise levels. What they all learn is a denoiser: the best estimate of the clean image given the noisy one, which for a squared-error loss is the conditional mean \(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t]\). There is a deeper reading of the same quantity. The predicted noise is, up to a scale factor, the gradient of the log-density of noisy images, \(\nabla_{x}\log p_t(x)\), called the score. Sampling therefore climbs towards higher probability while noise is gradually removed, and diffusion models are also called score-based models (Song et al., 2021).

Why it matters

The training loss is a plain regression, with no adversary and no bound to balance. That is why diffusion training is stable, why it scales smoothly with model size and data like LLM training does, and why every noise level of every training image contributes to learning the whole distribution, which is why diffusion models do not drop modes the way GANs do.

4.3What a perfect denoiser does: from the average to one image

The quantity at the centre of everything, the denoiser \(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t]\), can be computed exactly when the "dataset" is small and known: it is a weighted average of the training images, each weighted by how well it explains the noisy input. The experiment behind Figure 4.3 does that for a toy dataset of 2 000 small landscape images (sky, ground, a sun or moon, sometimes a house) and runs a 50-step deterministic sampler from pure noise. No network is trained, so what you see is what an ideal network would do. Step through it: the prediction starts as the blurry average of the whole dataset and then commits, coarse decisions first.

Three lessons from this experiment carry over to real models. First, generation is coarse to fine: the low-SNR steps decide composition and colour, the high-SNR steps decide detail. That is why image-to-image editing with low strength (starting from a partly noised input, Section 5.1) keeps the composition and changes only details, and why a schedule must spend enough steps in the low-SNR band. Second, a single denoising step returns an average, which is blurry; the multi-step chain is what lets the model commit to one sharp sample. Third, memorisation is the default for a perfect fit: Carlini et al. (2023) extracted near-copies of training images from Stable Diffusion for images that were duplicated many times in its training set, which is why deduplication of training data is a standard step.

4.4Samplers: from 1000 steps to one

A sampler decides how to walk from noise to an image using the trained denoiser. The original DDPM sampler follows the reverse Markov chain: at each of the 1000 steps it moves towards the predicted clean image and adds a little fresh noise. It works, but 1000 network evaluations per image is far too slow for a product.

DDIM (Song, Meng and Ermon, 2020) noticed that the same trained network supports a deterministic update that can skip steps. At each step it predicts \(\hat x_0\) from the current \(x_t\), computes the noise direction that is consistent with it, and jumps directly to a much smaller noise level:

\[ \hat x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}, \qquad x_{t'} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\hat x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\hat\varepsilon . \tag{4.2}\]

Because DDIM is deterministic, the same starting noise always produces the same image (that is what a "seed" fixes in a product), and small changes to the starting noise give small changes in the image. The deterministic sampler is a numerical solver of an ordinary differential equation, the probability-flow ODE, whose solution transports the noise distribution into the data distribution. Once generation is an ODE, the whole toolbox of numerical analysis applies: higher-order solvers such as DPM-Solver++, UniPC and Heun's method reach good quality in 15 to 30 steps. In 2026, 20 to 50 steps is the normal range for a full-quality model.

Going below that needs distillation: training a student that does in one to four steps what the teacher does in fifty. Progressive distillation halves the step count repeatedly; consistency models (Song et al., 2023) and latent consistency models (LCM, with LCM-LoRA as an add-on) train the network to map any point of a trajectory directly to its end; adversarial diffusion distillation (SDXL-Turbo, 2023) adds a GAN loss so that the one-step output looks sharp instead of averaged. Distilled models trade some diversity and prompt adherence for 10× to 50× lower latency, and are what "real-time" image generation runs on.

Numbers

DDPM: 1000 training steps, linear β from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02. Typical sampling: 20–50 steps with DDIM, DPM-Solver++ or Euler; 1–4 steps after distillation (SDXL-Turbo: 1 step at 512 × 512; LCM: 4–8 steps). With classifier-free guidance each step costs two network evaluations (batched together), so a 30-step image is 60 denoiser passes plus one VAE decode.

4.5Guidance: trading diversity for prompt adherence

A model trained on captioned images learns \(p(x \mid c)\), but sampling it directly gives images that follow the prompt only loosely: the caption data is noisy, and the model hedges. Classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021) sharpened the conditional distribution by adding the gradient of a separate classifier during sampling. Classifier-free guidance (CFG, Ho and Salimans, 2022) removed the need for that classifier and is used by nearly every text-to-image model. During training the condition is dropped for about 10 % of the examples (the drop line in the training code of Section 4.2), so one network learns both the conditional prediction \(\hat\varepsilon(x_t, c)\) and the unconditional one \(\hat\varepsilon(x_t, \varnothing)\). At sampling time both are computed and extrapolated:

\[ \hat\varepsilon_w = \hat\varepsilon(x_t, \varnothing) + w\,\big(\hat\varepsilon(x_t, c) - \hat\varepsilon(x_t, \varnothing)\big). \tag{4.3}\]

With \(w = 1\) this is the plain conditional model; \(w = 0\) ignores the prompt; \(w > 1\) pushes the sample further in the direction in which the prompt changes the prediction. In probability terms it samples from something proportional to \(p(x)\,p(c \mid x)^{w}\): images for which the condition is more obviously true than in typical data. Stable Diffusion's default is \(w = 7.5\). The negative prompt in many user interfaces is the same formula with the unconditional prediction replaced by the prediction for the negative text, so the sample is pushed away from it.

    null = torch.zeros_like(cond)
    for i, t in enumerate(ts):
        tt = t.repeat(shape[0])
        e_c, e_u = model(x, tt, cond), model(x, tt, null)       # batched together in practice
        eps = e_u + w * (e_c - e_u)                            # classifier-free guidance (4.3)
        a = abar[t]
        x0 = (x - (1 - a).sqrt() * eps) / a.sqrt()              # predicted clean image
        a_next = abar[ts[i + 1]] if i + 1 < steps else torch.tensor(1.0)
        x = a_next.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a_next).sqrt() * eps       # DDIM step (4.2), deterministic
    return x

Figure 4.4 lets you see samplers and guidance at work on a problem small enough to compute exactly: a two-dimensional "dataset" made of four Gaussian blobs, two belonging to class A and two to class B, with one blob of each class close to each other. The denoiser is exact, so everything you see comes from the sampler, the number of steps and the guidance scale.

What the toy shows matches what practitioners see with real models. With one step, every sample lands on the data mean, between all the blobs (100 % of samples are more than three standard deviations from any blob): the averaged, blurry output of Section 4.3. With 3 DDIM steps 39 % of samples are still off the data; with 10 steps 3 %; from about 20 steps the samples have the data's spread. The flow-matching sampler with uniform time steps gets there faster (12 % off the data at 3 steps, 2 % at 5), partly because of its straighter paths (Section 4.6) and partly because its time steps are spread more evenly over the SNR range than the DDIM steps on the linear schedule, which waste early steps at almost pure noise. For conditioning, \(w = 0\) ignores the class (50 % of samples in class A), \(w = 1\) puts 99 % in class A, and large \(w\) pushes samples to the far side of class A, away from class B: at \(w = 7.5\) they are spread 1.6× wider than the data and 4 % fall outside it. In images that is the familiar look of over-guided samples: oversaturated colours, exaggerated features, and less variety between seeds.

Pitfall: guidance is not free

CFG doubles the compute per step and reduces diversity: at high scales, different seeds give similar images. Products expose it as a "prompt strength" slider and keep it in the 3–8 range; distilled models often bake guidance into the student and run without it. Guidance applied at every step also burns out colours; schedules that apply it only in the middle part of sampling (guidance intervals) keep most of the adherence with fewer artefacts.

4.6Flow matching and rectified flow

Once generation is seen as solving an ODE from noise to data, an obvious question is: why use the curved paths that the variance-preserving process defines? Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) and rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) choose the simplest path: a straight line between a data point and a noise sample,

\[ x_t = (1 - t)\,x_0 + t\,\varepsilon, \qquad t \in [0, 1], \tag{4.4}\]

and train the network to predict the velocity along it, \(v = \varepsilon - x_0\). Sampling integrates \(dx/dt = v_\theta(x_t, t)\) from \(t = 1\) (noise) to \(t = 0\) (data) with a few Euler steps. The training code is even shorter than for diffusion:

def flow_matching_loss(model, x0, cond):
    t = torch.rand(x0.shape[0]).view(-1, 1, 1, 1)             # SD3 samples t from a logit-normal instead
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt = (1 - t) * x0 + t * eps                               # straight line between data and noise
    return F.mse_loss(model(xt, t.flatten(), cond), eps - x0) # target: the velocity

It is important not to oversell the difference. Each training pair is a straight line, but many lines cross the same point \(x_t\), so the network learns their average velocity, and the actual sampling trajectories bend near the data, as Figure 4.4 shows. Flow matching and diffusion are close relatives: with the right choice of time variable and output parametrisation, they are the same model with a different loss weighting over noise levels. The practical gains are a simpler formulation, straighter trajectories (rectified flow additionally "reflows": it re-trains on the model's own noise-image pairs to straighten paths further) and good scaling. Stable Diffusion 3 (2024) and FLUX.1 (12 billion parameters, 2024) are rectified-flow transformers; flow matching also drives the action head of robot policies such as π0 (Section 10.1).

4.7Latent diffusion: the architecture of real products

Running 50 denoising steps on 512 × 512 × 3 pixels is expensive, and most of those pixels carry imperceptible detail. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022, the paper behind Stable Diffusion) splits the work. A VAE, trained once, compresses images by 8× in each direction; the diffusion model runs entirely in that latent space; the VAE decoder turns the final latent into pixels in one pass (Figure 4.6). The perceptual detail is the decoder's job, and the diffusion model spends its capacity on content and composition.

The pieces and why each is there:

Text encoder. The prompt is tokenised and encoded into a sequence of vectors. SD 1.x used the text tower of CLIP ViT-L/14 (77 tokens × 768 dimensions); SDXL concatenates two CLIP text encoders; SD3 and FLUX add a T5 language-model encoder, because CLIP's text embedding behaves like a bag of words (Section 6.4) and does not capture "a red cube on top of a blue sphere" or the exact letters of text to be rendered. Better text encoders were the main reason prompt adherence and in-image text improved between 2022 and 2024.

Denoiser. The SD 1.x denoiser is a U-Net of about 860 million parameters: a convolutional encoder-decoder with skip connections (the same shape as segmentation networks, see Deep learning for vision 10.2), with self-attention and cross-attention layers at the lower resolutions. Cross-attention is where the prompt enters: queries come from image positions, keys and values from the text tokens, so each region of the image can "look up" the words relevant to it. Since 2023 the field has moved to diffusion transformers (DiT): the latent is cut into patches and processed by a transformer, as in a ViT. SD3's MMDiT processes text and image tokens in one sequence with joint attention; FLUX.1 is a 12-billion-parameter transformer of this kind. Transformers scale more predictably with compute, which is what large labs need.

Timestep and other conditioning. The step \(t\) (or noise level) is embedded and added to every block, usually by modulating normalisation layers (adaptive layer norm), because the network must behave very differently at SNR 0.001 and SNR 100. Image size, crop coordinates and aspect ratio are passed the same way in SDXL so the model does not learn cropped compositions from cropped training images.

Video uses the same recipe with a spatio-temporal latent: a video VAE compresses space and time, and a diffusion transformer denoises "spacetime patches". The cost grows with the number of frames, which is why video generation is still measured in seconds of compute per second of output.

In practice: the text-to-image product

The product of scenario 1 runs a distilled 4-step model for the live preview while the user types and a 30-step model with CFG 5–7 for the final four images, batched together (8 latents per batch, conditional and unconditional). The seed is stored with every image so that "make variations" and edits start from the same noise. Latency is dominated by the denoiser passes; the VAE decode is one pass but at full resolution it is memory-heavy, so it runs tiled for large outputs. Quantising and compiling the denoiser for the serving GPU belongs to Edge AI 5.

4.8Diffusion, GAN or autoregressive: how to choose

The families are now more complementary than competing. Diffusion and flow models are the default for open-ended, text-conditioned image and video generation because they train stably, cover the distribution and accept many kinds of conditioning. GANs remain the choice when one pass must suffice and the domain is narrow: super-resolution, face reenactment, on-device filters. Autoregressive token models are attractive when one model should read and write both text and images, because images become ordinary tokens in the LLM's sequence; OpenAI described the image generation in GPT-4o (2025) as native to the multimodal model, and research models such as VAR (next-scale prediction) narrowed the quality gap. Hybrids are common: autoregressive models that predict continuous tokens with a small diffusion head, or diffusion models distilled with an adversarial loss.

5Editing, inpainting and conditioning

Users of an image product rarely want a random image; they want this image with one thing changed, or a new image that keeps a given pose, layout or style. Diffusion models are well suited to that, because the sampling process can be entered half-way, constrained region by region, and steered by extra inputs. This chapter is the toolbox of scenario 1.

5.1Image-to-image: entering the process half-way

Image-to-image editing (SDEdit, Meng et al., 2021) uses the coarse-to-fine property of Section 4.3 directly. Encode the user's image into the latent space, add noise up to some intermediate level, and run only the remaining denoising steps with the new prompt. The strength parameter is the fraction of the noise schedule that is redone: strength 0.6 with 50 steps adds noise to the level of step 30 and runs 30 steps. Whatever survives that much noise, mainly the layout and dominant colours, is kept; everything finer is regenerated to match the new prompt. Low strength (0.2–0.4) changes style and texture; high strength (0.7–0.9) keeps only a rough composition. Turning a sketch into a rendered image is the same operation with high strength.

Edits that must keep the identity of a person or object exactly need more than that. Inversion methods run the deterministic sampler backwards to find the noise that reproduces the input image exactly, then re-run forward with a modified prompt; attention-based editors (Prompt-to-Prompt) additionally keep the cross-attention maps of the unchanged words, so "a cat sitting on a sofa" can become "a dog sitting on a sofa" with the same sofa. Instruction-based editors (InstructPix2Pix and its successors, and the editing modes of large multimodal models) are trained on (image, instruction, edited image) triples and take "make it winter" directly.

5.2Inpainting and outpainting

Inpainting regenerates a masked region while keeping the rest. The simplest method works with any diffusion model: at every denoising step, replace the latent outside the mask by the original image noised to the current level, and let the model generate only inside the mask. Because the model sees consistent context at every step, the new content blends in. Its weakness is that the model never knew which pixels were fixed, so it can produce content that fights the border and leaves seams. Dedicated inpainting models are fine-tuned with the mask and the masked image as extra inputs (Stable Diffusion's inpainting U-Net takes 9 input channels: 4 for the noisy latent, 4 for the encoded masked image, 1 for the mask) on randomly masked training images, so they learn to complete rather than to overwrite. Outpainting, extending an image beyond its border, is inpainting with the mask outside the original frame.

Pitfall: the mask is in latent space

The latent is 8× smaller than the image, so a mask is downsampled to 1/8 resolution before it is applied: a 4-pixel-wide crack becomes half a latent cell. Fine masks get dilated, and the VAE round-trip also slightly changes pixels outside the mask. Products paste the original pixels back outside the mask after decoding and blend a feathered border, otherwise every edit subtly degrades the whole photo.

5.3ControlNet and adapters: conditioning on structure

A text prompt cannot say "this exact pose" or "this exact room layout". ControlNet (Zhang, Rao and Agrawala, 2023) adds such spatial control to a frozen pretrained model. It makes a trainable copy of the denoiser's encoder, feeds it the control image (an edge map, a depth map, a human-pose skeleton, a segmentation map, a scribble) and adds the copy's features into the frozen model's decoder through zero convolutions: 1 × 1 convolutions whose weights start at zero. At the first training step the ControlNet therefore contributes exactly nothing and the base model's quality is untouched; gradually the copy learns how to steer the frozen model. Because the original weights never change, one base model can be combined with several ControlNets at inference (pose plus depth, for example), each with its own strength.

Lighter adapters exist for other kinds of control. T2I-Adapter does the same with a small separate network instead of an encoder copy. IP-Adapter conditions on a reference image instead of a structure map: it encodes the image with a CLIP image encoder and injects those features through extra cross-attention layers, which gives "in the style of this picture" or "this product, in a new scene" without fine-tuning.

5.4Personalisation: LoRA, DreamBooth and textual inversion

The product's customers want their own illustration style or their own product to appear reliably. Fine-tuning a whole model for each customer is expensive to train and to store, and three lighter methods cover most needs. Textual inversion learns only a new word embedding (a pseudo-token such as "<acme-style>") from 3–20 example images, leaving the model untouched; it is tiny but limited in what it can express. DreamBooth fine-tunes the model on a few images of a subject bound to a rare token, with a "prior preservation" loss on generated images of the general class so that "dog" still means any dog afterwards. LoRA (low-rank adaptation, Hu et al., 2021, originally for LLMs) is the usual way to make any of these fine-tunes cheap.

LoRA freezes each weight matrix \(W\) and learns a low-rank update \(\Delta W = BA\), where \(A\) has \(r\) rows and \(B\) has \(r\) columns with a small rank \(r\) (Figure 5.2). For a 768 × 768 attention projection and \(r = 8\), the update has 12 288 trainable parameters instead of 589 824, about 2 %. \(B\) starts at zero, so training starts from the base model exactly; after training the update can be kept separate (and swapped per request, or several LoRAs mixed with weights) or merged into \(W\) for zero extra inference cost. LoRA files for image models are a few megabytes to around a hundred, which is why there are public libraries with tens of thousands of them. The method itself is covered in Generative AI 5.3; this is how it looks as a layer:

class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r=8, alpha=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base.requires_grad_(False)                         # frozen W
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, base.in_features) * 0.01)  # small random
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(base.out_features, r))       # zero: no change at start
        self.scale = alpha / r

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.base(x) + self.scale * (x @ self.A.t() @ self.B.t())
Pitfall: overfitting a handful of images

With 10 training images, a LoRA or DreamBooth run quickly starts to reproduce the training images' backgrounds, lighting and framing instead of the subject alone, and "language drift" makes the class word lose its general meaning. The usual guards are varied training images, captions that name what should not be learned ("on a white table"), prior-preservation images, a low learning rate, a few hundred to a few thousand steps, and a fixed set of evaluation prompts checked at each checkpoint.

5.5The product around the model

The model is perhaps half of the text-to-image product. Around it sit a prompt rewriter (often an LLM that expands "a fox" into a detailed caption, because models are trained on detailed captions), input and output safety classifiers (for sexual content, violence, real people, trademarks), provenance marking, and the serving system. Two points come up in customer discussions. Provenance: C2PA "content credentials" attach signed metadata stating that an image was generated or edited, and invisible watermarks such as Google's SynthID embed a signal in the pixels that survives moderate edits; metadata is easy to strip, watermarks are harder but not impossible to remove, so neither is proof on its own. Training-data rights: what the model was trained on, whether outputs can reproduce copyrighted works (Section 4.3 showed why memorisation happens), and whether customers are indemnified. These are business questions that the architecture of the model directly affects.

6CLIP and contrastive image-text learning

Supervised vision models learn from labels that someone defined in advance: 1 000 ImageNet classes, 80 COCO classes. Every new concept needs new labels. CLIP (Radford et al., OpenAI, 2021) learned instead from 400 million image-caption pairs collected from the web, where the "label" is whatever text people wrote next to the picture. The result is a pair of encoders that map images and text into one shared space, and that shared space became the glue of multimodal AI.

6.1Why learn from captions

Captions are noisy, but they are free, plentiful and open-ended. A caption like "my grandmother's 1970s kitchen in Lisbon" carries information that no fixed label set has a slot for. The obvious way to use captions, training a model to generate the caption from the image, turned out to be slow to learn: the model must predict the exact words, while many different captions are correct. CLIP's key choice was to make the task easier and better matched to what we want: not "write the caption", but "pick the right caption from a batch". That is a contrastive task, and it learns from the same data several times more efficiently than caption generation did in the authors' comparison.

6.2How contrastive training works

CLIP has two encoders: an image encoder (a ResNet or a ViT) and a text encoder (a transformer). Each ends with a linear projection into the same embedding space (512 dimensions for ViT-B, 768 for ViT-L), and both embeddings are normalised to unit length, so the similarity of an image and a text is their cosine. A training batch holds \(N\) image-caption pairs. All \(N\) images and \(N\) captions are encoded, and the \(N \times N\) matrix of cosine similarities is computed (Figure 6.1). The \(N\) diagonal entries are the true pairs; the other \(N^2 - N\) entries are negatives that come for free. The loss treats each row as an \(N\)-way classification ("which of these images belongs to this caption?") and each column as the reverse question, and averages the two cross-entropies. This symmetric loss is a form of InfoNCE.

def clip_loss(img_emb, txt_emb, logit_scale):
    img = F.normalize(img_emb, dim=-1)
    txt = F.normalize(txt_emb, dim=-1)
    logits = logit_scale.exp().clamp(max=100) * img @ txt.t()      # N x N, cosine / temperature
    labels = torch.arange(img.shape[0], device=img.device)        # the diagonal is correct
    return (F.cross_entropy(logits, labels) + F.cross_entropy(logits.t(), labels)) / 2

logit_scale = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(1 / 0.07).log())          # learned temperature, starts at 0.07

Two design details carry much of the result. The temperature \(\tau\) divides the cosines before the softmax. Cosines between unit vectors in high dimensions differ only by a few tenths, and without scaling the softmax would be nearly uniform and the gradients weak. CLIP learns \(\tau\), starting at 0.07 and clipped so that the scale \(1/\tau\) never exceeds 100. The batch size sets how hard the task is: with \(N = 32\,768\), each image must find its caption among 32 768 candidates, which forces fine distinctions. A model that has learned nothing has a loss of \(\ln N = 10.4\) at that batch size. Large batches need the similarity matrix to be computed across many GPUs, which is the main engineering cost of contrastive training.

Interesting detail: SigLIP

The softmax couples every pair in the batch: each row's normalisation needs all the other similarities, which means gathering all embeddings on every device. SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) replaces the softmax with an independent sigmoid per pair, "is this pair a match, yes or no", with a learned bias for the heavy class imbalance. It needs no global normalisation, works well already at batch sizes of a few thousand, and gives slightly better encoders at the same compute. SigLIP and SigLIP 2 image encoders are the vision towers of many current VLMs.

6.3Zero-shot classification and retrieval

A trained CLIP can classify images into classes it was never trained on. Write each class name into a sentence such as "a photo of a {class}.", encode the sentences with the text encoder, and use the resulting vectors as the weights of a linear classifier: the predicted class is the one whose text embedding has the highest cosine with the image embedding (Figure 6.2a). No training images are needed. CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 px reached 76.2 % top-1 accuracy on ImageNet this way, matching the original supervised ResNet-50 without using any of its 1.28 million labelled training images, and it was far more robust on shifted versions of ImageNet (sketches, renditions, adversarially filtered photos), where supervised models lose much of their accuracy.

def zero_shot(image_emb, class_names, encode_text, template='a photo of a {}.'):
    txt = F.normalize(encode_text([template.format(c) for c in class_names]), dim=-1)
    img = F.normalize(image_emb, dim=-1)
    return (100.0 * img @ txt.t()).softmax(dim=-1)                  # class probabilities

The prompt template matters more than you would expect. "A photo of a {}." beats the bare class name because web captions are sentences; for satellite images "a satellite photo of {}." works better. In the paper the single template added 1.3 points of ImageNet accuracy over the bare class name, and averaging the text embeddings of 80 templates ("a blurry photo of a {}", "a close-up photo of a {}", "a drawing of a {}" ...) added another 3.5. Class names themselves can be ambiguous ("crane" the bird or the machine, "boxer" the dog or the athlete), and rewriting them is an easy win. The same embeddings give retrieval in both directions: text-to-image search over a photo collection, image-to-image similarity, and deduplication, using a vector index over precomputed image embeddings.

In practice: the perception team

Before any labelling, the perception team of scenario 3 embeds every frame of its fleet archive with a CLIP-style image encoder and searches with text such as "an e-scooter lying on the pavement at night". The top few thousand hits, plus visually similar frames found by image-to-image search from a few known examples, become the candidate pool for auto-labelling (Section 9.1). Embedding millions of frames is a one-off batch job; each query is a vector-index lookup of milliseconds.

6.4Where CLIP fails

CLIP learned what distinguishes a caption from 32 767 random other captions, and that defines its blind spots. Random captions almost never differ only in word order, in a count or in a negation, so CLIP's text embedding behaves much like a bag of words: "a red cube on a blue sphere" and "a blue cube on a red sphere" get nearly the same embedding, counting beyond small numbers is unreliable, and "a street with no cars" matches images of cars. Benchmarks such as Winoground and ARO measure exactly this and found CLIP-style models close to chance on compositional pairs. Fine-grained distinctions that captions rarely mention (car model years, plant species, medical findings) are weak, and so are abstract or systematic tasks such as reading the time on a clock or counting objects.

The embedding space also has a geometry that surprises people. Image embeddings and text embeddings occupy two separate narrow cones (the modality gap, Figure 6.2b): every image is closer to every other image than to any caption, and a cosine of 0.3 between an image and its caption is a good match. Thresholds on CLIP similarity must therefore be calibrated on real data per use case, not chosen as "0.5 sounds right". Finally CLIP inherits web biases: associations between professions, genders and ethnicities in captions reappear in its similarities, which matters when it is used to filter datasets or rank people.

Pitfall: CLIP as a dataset filter

Large image-text datasets (LAION-5B, DataComp) were filtered by keeping pairs whose CLIP similarity is above a threshold. That removes junk, but it also bakes CLIP's blind spots into the next generation of models: pairs whose captions describe things CLIP does not recognise are dropped. Models trained on CLIP-filtered data inherit what CLIP could see.

6.5CLIP as a component

Most uses of CLIP today are as a part inside something else. Its text encoder conditioned Stable Diffusion 1.x and 2.x and is still one of the encoders in SDXL, SD3 and FLUX. Its image encoder (or SigLIP's) is the vision tower of most VLMs (Chapter 7), because its features are already aligned with language, which makes the bridge into an LLM cheap to train. Open-vocabulary detectors such as OWL-ViT are CLIP models with a detection head (Chapter 8). CLIP score, the cosine between a generated image and its prompt, is a standard prompt-adherence metric (Section 11.2). And CLIP embeddings drive data mining, deduplication and near-duplicate detection in almost every large training pipeline. For self-supervised image-only encoders such as DINOv2, which are better for dense tasks like segmentation and depth but have no text side, see Deep learning for vision 4.4.

Talk track: how does CLIP work?

"CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder together on 400 million image-caption pairs from the web. In each batch it computes the cosine similarity between every image and every caption, and a contrastive loss pushes the true pairs up and all other pairs down, in both directions, with a learned temperature. The result is a shared embedding space. Zero-shot classification falls out of it: embed 'a photo of a dog', 'a photo of a cat' and so on, and pick the class whose text vector is closest to the image vector. Its weak spots come from the same training: it behaves like a bag of words, so counting, negation and spatial relations are weak."

7Vision-language models

CLIP can say how well an image matches a sentence, but it cannot answer "what is the total on this receipt?" or "which button turns off Bluetooth?". Those questions need open-ended language output conditioned on fine visual detail. Vision-language models get it by giving an LLM eyes: an image encoder turns the picture into a sequence of vectors that the LLM reads like extra words. This is scenario 2.

7.1Why VLMs: documents, screenshots and open questions

Before VLMs, reading a receipt meant a pipeline: detect text regions, run OCR, then parse the text with rules or a layout model that knew where fields usually sit. Every new layout broke something, and anything that was not text (a chart, a stamp, a checkbox, a crossed-out amount) was invisible. A VLM replaces the pipeline with one model that sees the image and reads the question: "List the line items and the total as JSON", "Is the invoice signed?", "What does the error dialog say and what should the user click?". The same model handles photos, charts, slides and app screenshots. That generality is why VLMs spread so quickly into document processing, customer support, accessibility (describing images for blind users), content moderation and GUI agents that operate apps by looking at the screen.

The cost of that generality is that the VLM's behaviour is that of an LLM: fluent, usually right, occasionally and confidently wrong. Most of this chapter is about where the visual information gets lost on its way into the LLM, because that is where the wrong answers come from.

7.2Architecture: vision encoder, projector, language model

The dominant design, popularised by LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) and used by Qwen-VL, InternVL, Gemma 3, Pixtral and most open models, has three parts (Figure 7.1a). A vision encoder, usually a CLIP or SigLIP ViT, cuts the image into 14 × 14-pixel patches and turns each into a vector; with a 336 × 336 input that is a 24 × 24 grid, 576 vectors of 1 024 dimensions. A projector, typically a two-layer MLP, maps each vector into the LLM's embedding width (4 096 for a 7–8 B model), often after merging 2 × 2 neighbouring patches into one vector to cut the count by four. The resulting visual tokens are placed into the LLM's input sequence between marker tokens, next to the text tokens of the question, and the LLM attends over all of them with its ordinary self-attention and generates the answer token by token.

def pixel_shuffle_merge(feats, k=2):            # (B, H*W, C) -> (B, H*W / k^2, C * k^2)
    B, N, C = feats.shape
    H = W = int(N ** 0.5)
    x = feats.view(B, H // k, k, W // k, k, C).permute(0, 1, 3, 2, 4, 5)
    return x.reshape(B, (H // k) * (W // k), k * k * C)

projector = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4 * 1024, 4096), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(4096, 4096))
# 336 px image -> ViT-L/14 gives 576 x 1024 -> merge 2x2 -> 144 x 4096 -> projector -> 144 visual tokens

The alternative design, from DeepMind's Flamingo (2022) and used by Llama 3.2 Vision, keeps image features out of the input sequence (Figure 7.1b). A resampler compresses them to a fixed number of vectors (64 in Flamingo), and new cross-attention layers, inserted between the frozen LLM's blocks with a gate that starts at zero, let the text tokens attend to them. The LLM's text abilities are untouched and long image sequences do not eat context, which suits video and interleaved documents. The adapter style won in open models because it is simpler, trains well, and lets fine detail reach every layer of the LLM. A third, "early fusion" design drops the separate vision encoder altogether and feeds patches (Fuyu) or discrete image tokens (Chameleon) directly into one transformer trained on both modalities from the start.

Why it matters

The projector is a few dozen million parameters between a vision encoder and an LLM with billions. That is why VLMs could be built quickly on top of existing LLMs, and why a VLM inherits both parents' strengths and weaknesses: the encoder decides what can be seen (resolution, what CLIP-style training made salient), the LLM decides what is said about it (reasoning, language, and the tendency to fill gaps with plausible text).

7.3Visual tokens and resolution: the central trade-off

The vision encoder sees a fixed number of pixels, and documents and screenshots contain small text. A full A4 page scanned at 300 dpi is 2 480 × 3 508 pixels, and 10-point body text is about 42 pixels tall. Squeezed into the 336 × 336 input of an early VLM, that text is 4 pixels tall: no human could read it either. Getting more pixels to the model means more visual tokens, and every visual token costs LLM compute and context just like a word. The interactive below shows the trade-off for three typical inputs and five resolution strategies used by real model families.

The strategies are the history of document VLMs in five steps. A fixed low resolution (LLaVA-1.5: 336 px, 576 tokens) is fine for photos and hopeless for pages: the A4 text is 4.0 px tall. Fixed 448 px with a 2 × 2 merge costs only 256 tokens but still leaves 5.3 px text. Tiling cuts the image into encoder-sized crops plus a downscaled thumbnail for global context: LLaVA-NeXT's grid of up to four 336 px tiles brings the page to 8 px text for 2 880 tokens; InternVL's up to twelve 448 px tiles with a 2 × 2 merge reach 16 px text for 1 792 tokens. Native resolution (Qwen2-VL, 2024) drops fixed sizes altogether: the ViT runs on the image at roughly its own aspect ratio, with one token per 28 × 28 pixels after merging, and a configurable pixel budget; 2D rotary position encodings tell the LLM where each token sits. At about 1 280 tokens the A4 page becomes 840 × 1 176 pixels with 14 px text, readable. The desktop screenshot is the hard case: 13 px UI text on a 2 560 × 1 440 screen drops to 6.6 px at the same budget, which is why GUI agents run with several thousand visual tokens per screenshot.

Numbers

ViT-L/14 at 336 px: 24 × 24 = 576 patch tokens. 448 px with 2 × 2 pixel-shuffle merge: 1 024 → 256 tokens. Qwen2-VL: one token per 28 × 28 px, so a 1 120 × 1 568 page costs 40 × 56 = 2 240 tokens. A 1 280-token image costs as much prefill compute as roughly 1 000 words of text, and a 20-page PDF at that rate is 25 600 tokens before the question is asked. Text below about 6 px is unreadable for people and models alike; 12 px and above is comfortable (a rough rule used in this chapter's interactive, not a published threshold).

In practice: the document service

The expense service of scenario 2 classifies each upload first (receipt photo, single-page invoice, multi-page PDF, screenshot) and picks a pixel budget per class instead of one global setting. Multi-page PDFs are rendered page by page at about 150 dpi; pages whose text layer is extractable skip the VLM's reading entirely and pass the text plus a low-resolution page image. Visual token count is the main cost driver, so it is logged per request alongside accuracy.

7.4How VLMs are trained, briefly

Training usually happens in stages that move from cheap alignment to expensive behaviour. First, only the projector is trained on image-caption pairs (LLaVA-1.5 used about 558 000) so that visual tokens land where the LLM can use them; encoder and LLM stay frozen. Second, the projector and LLM (and often the encoder) are trained on instruction data: visual question answering, OCR and document QA, charts, multi-turn conversations, grounding with boxes, and increasingly synthetic data generated from documents and code-rendered charts whose answers are known exactly. LLaVA-1.5's instruction set had 665 000 examples; current models use tens of millions. Third, preference tuning (RLHF, DPO) rewards answers that are faithful to the image and penalises invented details. Large proprietary models are now trained natively on interleaved image-text data from the start rather than bolted together. The stages themselves, and why each exists for LLMs, are in Generative AI 3.

Pitfall: instruction data written without the image

The first LLaVA instruction data was generated by a text-only LLM from captions and box lists. Whatever the caption mentioned, the generated answers asserted, even when it was barely visible; a model trained on it learns to answer from what is typical for a scene. Much of early VLM hallucination traces back to training data whose author never saw the picture.

7.5Grounding: boxes and points as text

An answer is much more useful, and checkable, when the model can say where. Grounded VLMs output coordinates as ordinary text tokens (Figure 7.3): Qwen2-VL writes boxes as two corner points normalised to 0–1000, PaliGemma uses special location tokens for 1 024 bins per axis, Qwen2.5-VL switched to absolute pixel coordinates, and Molmo outputs points. Because coordinates are just tokens, they are learned with the same next-token loss as words, from datasets of referring expressions ("the man in the red jacket"), detection data turned into text, and GUI screenshots annotated with element boxes. The application parses the coordinates back and maps them to the original image.

Grounding turns two classic failure modes into checkable ones. A document assistant that returns "total: €1 284.50" together with the box it read it from can be verified by cropping that box and re-reading it, or by showing the highlight to a human reviewer. A GUI agent that clicks a box can take a new screenshot and check that the expected screen appeared. The flip side is that a VLM's boxes are less precise than a dedicated detector's, and coordinate conventions differ between model families: a box parsed with the wrong convention (normalised versus pixels, x-y versus y-x) silently points at the wrong element, a common integration bug.

7.6Hallucination: why VLMs see things that are not there

A VLM hallucinates when its answer is not supported by the image: it mentions a dog that is not in the photo, reads "€1 284.50" where the receipt says "€1 264.50", or describes a chart trend that the data does not show. The causes are specific and worth knowing, because each suggests a mitigation.

The language prior is strong and the visual signal is weak. The LLM was trained on trillions of text tokens, the vision bridge on millions of images. When the visual evidence is ambiguous, the answer follows what usually co-occurs: a kitchen photo "has" a refrigerator, a dining table "has" forks. The POPE benchmark (Li et al., 2023) measures exactly this by asking yes/no questions about objects, with the absent objects chosen at random, by frequency, or by co-occurrence; models are worst on the co-occurrence ("adversarial") split. Lost pixels. At low resolution small text and small objects are simply not in the visual tokens, but the model is never trained to say "I cannot read that", so it produces the most plausible string. Digits are the classic case: a receipt total is read correctly in its format and wrong in one digit. Training data that over-claims, as described in Section 7.4. Long answers drift: the further a description runs, the more it is driven by the text already generated rather than the image, so later sentences hallucinate more than earlier ones.

Mitigations follow from the causes: give the model enough resolution for the task (Section 7.3); ask for grounded answers with boxes and verify them; prefer extraction into a fixed schema over free description; allow and reward "not visible" as an answer; use preference tuning on hallucination-labelled pairs (RLHF-V and similar); and for numbers that matter, cross-check (line items must sum to the total, the date must parse, the IBAN checksum must hold). Decoding tricks such as visual contrastive decoding, which subtracts the prediction made from a distorted image to cancel the language prior, help in benchmarks but are rarely worth the doubled cost in production. Hallucination in LLMs generally, and how to evaluate it, is in Generative AI 10.4.

Talk track

"A VLM is a vision encoder, a small projector and an LLM. The encoder turns the image into patch vectors, the projector maps them into the LLM's embedding space, and the LLM reads them as extra tokens. For documents the critical parameter is resolution: a page squeezed into 336 pixels has 4-pixel text. Modern models tile the image or run at native resolution with a token budget, and every visual token costs like a word. Hallucination comes mostly from the strong language prior plus missing pixels, so you fix it with resolution, grounded outputs you can verify, schema extraction and consistency checks."

7.7VLM or OCR pipeline? Building the document product

The expense service does not have to choose one or the other. A classic OCR engine is cheap, deterministic, gives exact character boxes and confidence per character, and never invents a digit that is not there; it fails on layout, handwriting, stamps and anything that needs understanding. A VLM understands layout and context and handles messy inputs, but costs more per page and can produce plausible wrong values. Production systems therefore often combine them: OCR provides the text layer and boxes, the VLM receives the image plus the OCR text and maps it into the schema, and validation rules plus confidence thresholds route uncertain documents to people. The VLM's output is constrained to JSON with a schema (structured-output decoding), every extracted field carries the box it came from, and a held-out set of a few hundred real documents per document type is the regression test for every model or prompt change.

8Open-vocabulary detection and promptable segmentation

A conventional detector knows the classes it was trained on and nothing else; adding a class means collecting and labelling thousands of boxes and retraining. Open-vocabulary detectors accept the class list as text at run time, and promptable segmenters such as SAM outline whatever a click, a box or a phrase points at. Neither is usually what ships in a real-time product, but together they changed how training data is made. This is the toolset of scenario 3.

8.1Why closed-set detectors are not enough

A closed-set detector ends in a classification layer with one output per known class plus background (see Deep learning for vision 5 for how detectors work). An e-scooter that was never labelled is either missed (it was background in every training image that contained one) or forced into the nearest class, "bicycle" or "person". The long tail of rare objects (scooters, mobility aids, fallen loads, animals of unusual species) is exactly where this hurts. Labelling every possible class up front is impossible, and each new class costs a labelling campaign.

8.2How open-vocabulary detectors work

The idea is CLIP's zero-shot trick applied to regions. The detector proposes boxes and produces an embedding for each box; the prompt's words or phrases are encoded by a text encoder; the score of box \(i\) for phrase \(j\) is the similarity of the two embeddings, passed through a sigmoid (Figure 8.1). The text embeddings play the role of the classification layer's weights, so changing the prompt changes the classes. OWL-ViT (Google, 2022) adds lightweight box and embedding heads to a CLIP ViT's output tokens and fine-tunes on detection data; OWLv2 (2023) scaled it by self-training: an existing model pseudo-labelled boxes on billions of web images using their captions, and the next model was trained on those. GLIP (2022) reframed detection as phrase grounding so that grounding and caption data could be used for training. Grounding DINO (2023) adds deep fusion: image and text features attend to each other in several layers before the boxes are decoded, which helps with referring phrases such as "the person holding an umbrella"; it reported 52.5 AP on COCO without training on COCO. YOLO-World (2024) brought the idea to real-time speed by encoding the vocabulary once offline and folding the text embeddings into the detector's weights, reporting 35.4 AP on LVIS at 52 FPS on a V100.

8.3SAM: segmentation from a click

The Segment Anything Model (Kirillov et al., Meta, 2023) answers a different question: not "which objects of class X are here" but "what is the object at this point / in this box". It splits the work so that interaction is cheap (Figure 8.2). A heavy image encoder, a ViT-H with 632 million parameters pretrained as a masked autoencoder, runs once per image and produces a 64 × 64 grid of 256-dimensional embeddings for a 1024 × 1024 input. A small prompt encoder turns clicks, boxes or a rough mask into tokens, and a light mask decoder of two transformer layers combines prompt and image embedding into masks in milliseconds, fast enough to run in a browser while the user clicks.

Two design decisions make SAM work. The first is ambiguity-aware output: one click on a jacket could mean the jacket, the person or the logo, and a model trained to output a single mask would learn the blurry average of the three (the same averaging problem as in Section 2.3). SAM outputs three masks at different granularities with a predicted IoU each, and is trained with only the loss of the best one. The second is the data engine: SAM was trained on SA-1B, 1.1 billion masks on 11 million images, produced in three phases from annotators correcting model proposals, to semi-automatic labelling, to fully automatic masks generated by the model itself from a grid of point prompts. SAM 2 (2024) extended the model to video with a memory of past frames, so a click in one frame becomes a mask track through the clip. SAM 3 (2025) added "promptable concept segmentation": given a short noun phrase or an example box, it finds and segments all instances of the concept, which closes much of the gap to open-vocabulary detection. For edge use, distilled variants (MobileSAM, EfficientSAM) replace the heavy encoder.

8.4Failure modes and how teams use these models

Open-vocabulary models are impressive in demos and treacherous in production. Scores are not comparable across phrases: "car" may produce scores around 0.8 for clear cars while "e-scooter" peaks at 0.4 for clear scooters, because the text embedding of a rarer word is less well aligned with images. One global threshold is wrong for most classes; each prompt needs its own threshold, chosen on a small labelled sample. Prompt sensitivity: "scooter", "e-scooter", "electric kick scooter" and "kick scooter" give different recall and different false positives (mopeds, children's scooters). Absent objects still get boxes: grounding models are trained on phrase-image pairs where the phrase is present, so they tend to put the box on the most similar thing when it is not. Small and occluded objects and crowded scenes are weaker than in a specialised detector. And they are slow: Grounding DINO with a Swin backbone or SAM's ViT-H encoder run at a few frames per second on a data-centre GPU, not at the 30 Hz of an embedded perception stack.

So teams use them offline, as teachers: to mine data, propose labels, segment objects for review, and bootstrap a new class quickly, while the shipped model remains a compact closed-set detector trained on the result (Chapter 9). Where an open-vocabulary model does run online, it is usually in an analysis or search tool, or with a small fixed vocabulary folded into the weights as YOLO-World does.

Talk track

"An open-vocabulary detector is CLIP applied to boxes: it embeds each proposed box and each prompt phrase and scores their similarity, so the class list is just text. Grounding DINO fuses image and text features deeply and handles referring phrases; YOLO-World makes it real-time by baking the vocabulary into the weights. SAM is the segmentation counterpart: a heavy image encoder once per image, a tiny decoder per click or box, three masks to handle ambiguity, trained on 1.1 billion masks from a model-in-the-loop data engine. In practice both are teachers for labelling, not the model that ships."

9Auto-labelling and synthetic data with foundation models

The perception team of scenario 3 must add e-scooters to a real-time detector. The slow path is a labelling campaign: find images, draw tens of thousands of boxes by hand, train. Foundation models shorten both halves. Open-vocabulary detectors and SAM propose the labels, people correct a fraction of them, and diffusion models fill in the rare situations that the fleet has barely seen. Both shortcuts work, and both have a specific way of fooling you.

9.1Auto-labelling a new class

Figure 9.1 shows the pipeline. Mine: text and image search over CLIP embeddings of the archive finds frames that probably contain scooters (Section 6.3), so labelling effort is not spent on empty frames. Detect: an open-vocabulary detector runs on the candidates with a few tuned prompts ("e-scooter", "electric kick scooter", "person riding an e-scooter"), and boxes from the different prompts are merged. Segment: SAM turns each box into a mask, and in video SAM 2 propagates it through the clip, which also fills frames where the detector missed the object. Filter: per-prompt score thresholds, plausible size and aspect ratio for the camera geometry, and temporal consistency (an object that flickers in and out between frames is suspicious). Review: people check a random sample of accepted labels to measure their precision, and all labels in the uncertain score band, where the model is least reliable. Then the real-time student detector is trained on the result and evaluated on an audit set labelled entirely by people.

The economics are what make it attractive. Reviewing and correcting a proposed box takes a fraction of the time of drawing it, and the teacher models can be as large and slow as needed because they run offline. Offline teachers can also cheat in ways the product cannot: they can look at future frames of a recording, use several cameras and lidar at once, and run test-time augmentation, so their labels can be better than what any real-time model could produce. The general process view, including active learning to choose which frames to label, is in Neural network training 14.4.

9.2Trusting auto-labels: what to measure

The risk of auto-labelling is that its errors are systematic, not random. If the teacher misses scooters that are folded, lying down or partly hidden by a parked car, then the student never sees those as scooters, learns that they are background, and the error is now baked in twice. Worse, if the test set is labelled the same way, the student will look perfect on exactly the cases where it fails. Four habits keep this under control. Measure the auto-labels' precision and recall per slice (night, rain, distance band, occlusion level, pose) on a sample labelled by people, not overall. Keep the test set and the audit set human-labelled, and never use a model family's labels to evaluate the same family. Track the share of labels that people changed during review; if it falls to near zero, reviewers may be rubber-stamping. And keep a random, not model-selected, sample in every labelling batch so that the data does not drift towards the cases the teacher finds easy.

Pitfall: the threshold decides the dataset

A low threshold floods the training set with false positives (mopeds, bicycles, children's scooters labelled as e-scooters); a high one keeps only the easy, large, clearly visible scooters and silently drops the hard cases the student most needs. The threshold is a dataset-design decision: choose it per prompt from a precision-recall curve on a labelled sample, route the band between "surely yes" and "surely no" to human review, and record the threshold with the dataset version.

9.3Generating rare scenes with diffusion

Some situations are too rare to mine: a scooter lying across a bike lane at night in the rain might appear a handful of times in a year of driving. Generative models can produce more, in three ways of increasing ambition. Inpainting into real frames: choose a plausible location and size from the scene geometry, mask it, and inpaint "an e-scooter lying on its side" (Section 5.2); the rest of the frame stays real sensor data, and the mask gives the label. Controlled generation from layouts: generate whole images from a segmentation map, depth map or box layout with ControlNet-style conditioning (Section 5.3), so the label is defined before the image exists. Variation of real scenes: image-to-image with moderate strength turns day into night or dry into rain while keeping the geometry, so existing labels remain valid. Figure 9.2 shows the pipeline; the step that is easiest to skip and most important is the filter.

"The labels come free" is only true if the generator obeyed. Diffusion models regularly ignore the mask edge, generate two objects or none, change the object's size, or produce a scooter-like object that no detector would recognise. The filter re-runs a strong detector on every generated object and checks size, position and class against the request, and a CLIP-style score rejects images that do not match the prompt. Artefacts matter too: generated images carry statistical fingerprints (texture, noise, colour statistics) that differ from the camera's, and a detector can learn to associate "scooter" with "generated-looking" instead of with the shape of a scooter. That is why the synthetic share is kept a minority of the training data for that class, and why the only test that counts is on real images.

9.4When synthetic data helps and when it hurts

Synthetic data from generative models helps most where real data is not just scarce but absent for a combination: a known object in an unusual context, lighting or pose, when the generator already knows both the object and the context well. It helps little when the generator itself has never seen the object (it cannot produce a convincing rare industrial machine it never saw), when the task depends on exact sensor physics (glare, rolling shutter, HDR artefacts and lens distortion of a particular camera), or when labels need geometric precision beyond what the generator respects, such as 3D boxes or exact lane geometry. The same domain-gap reasoning as for simulator data applies (Simulation and validation 7.2), and so does the evaluation method: train with and without the synthetic data, test on real data, and look at the target slice and at the rest, because synthetic data that helps the rare slice can quietly hurt the common one.

In automotive

Driving perception teams use these tools mainly offline: foundation models as auto-labellers on fleet data, CLIP-style search for scenario mining, and generative models for rare-case augmentation and sensor-realistic variations. Generated images and auto-labels then fall under the same dataset-management rules as other training data in a safety argument: provenance, versioning, and evidence that the test data is independent and representative (see Neural network training 9.2 on domain shift). Neural reconstruction and generative world models for closed-loop simulation are covered in Simulation and validation 7.4.

10Vision-language-action and end-to-end driving models

If a VLM can describe a scene and point at objects, the next step is to let it act: output robot motor commands or a vehicle trajectory instead of words. Vision-language-action models try to transfer what large models learned from the web, about objects, instructions and common sense, into control. This chapter covers how such models produce actions and what world models are; whether a driving stack should be built this way is a system design question owned by Planning and control 12.

10.1From VLM to VLA: three ways to output an action

A VLA starts from a pretrained VLM and is fine-tuned on demonstrations: camera images and an instruction in, the demonstrated actions out (Figure 10.1). The models differ mainly in how actions come out. Actions as tokens: RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023) and OpenVLA (2024, 7 billion parameters, trained on about 970 000 robot episodes from the Open X-Embodiment collection) discretise each action dimension (end-effector x, y, z, rotations, gripper) into 256 bins and map the bins to tokens, so an action is a short "sentence" decoded by the language model's ordinary head. It is simple and reuses everything, but it is slow (one decoder step per dimension) and coarse for smooth, fast motion. An action expert with flow matching: π0 (Physical Intelligence, 2024) attaches a smaller transformer to a 3-billion-parameter VLM that generates a chunk of 50 future continuous actions by flow matching (Section 4.6) in about 10 integration steps, which gives smooth control at up to 50 Hz. Diffusion Policy (2023) showed earlier that denoising action sequences handles multi-modal demonstrations (go left or right around an obstacle) where a regression head would average into the obstacle. Trajectories as text: driving research models such as Waymo's EMMA (2024) write future waypoints as numbers in the output text, together with detections and road-graph elements.

The averaging argument from Section 2.3 explains why generative action heads matter. Human demonstrations are multi-modal: at the same obstacle one demonstrator steers left, another right. A model trained with squared error predicts their mean, straight ahead. A diffusion or flow head samples one of the modes, the same reason diffusion images are sharp where VAE images are blurry.

10.2World models: generating the future

A world model predicts what the world will look like next, given the past and an action. Generative video models make this concrete: GAIA-1 (Wayve, 2023, 9 billion parameters) generated realistic driving video conditioned on past frames, text and ego actions; NVIDIA's Cosmos (2025) released "world foundation models" for robotics and driving; Genie 2 and 3 (Google DeepMind) generate interactive environments from a prompt. Three uses are discussed. As a simulator, to generate rare and dangerous situations for testing and training, with the same caveats as any synthetic data plus a new one: the generated future must be physically consistent, and video models still violate object permanence and physics in ways that are hard to detect automatically. As a training signal, predicting the future is a self-supervised task that teaches a model dynamics from unlabelled video. As a planner component, imagining the outcome of candidate actions before choosing one. In 2026 the first two are in use in research and data pipelines; the third remains mostly research.

10.3What is different about driving

End-to-end driving models, from planning-oriented networks such as UniAD (2023) to production stacks that their makers describe as end-to-end neural networks, learn from fleet demonstrations to map sensor data to a trajectory. Adding a VLM brings general knowledge ("a ball rolling into the street may be followed by a child") and the ability to explain a decision in words. The obstacles are specific: the real-time loop (a 7-billion-parameter model is far from 10 Hz with margin on vehicle hardware), causal confusion (a model can learn that "the car ahead's brake lights come on" predicts braking and miss the reason), the gap between open-loop scores on logged data and closed-loop behaviour, and the difficulty of arguing the safety of a model whose failure modes cannot be enumerated. The practical pattern, a slow reasoning model beside a fast planner inside a rule-based safety envelope, and how such planners are evaluated, are in Planning and control 12.3 and 12.4.

Talk track

"A VLA is a VLM fine-tuned on demonstrations to output actions. RT-2 and OpenVLA turn each action dimension into 256 bins and decode them as tokens; π0 adds a flow-matching action expert that produces smooth chunks of 50 continuous actions, because demonstrations are multi-modal and a regression head would average them. World models are video generators conditioned on actions, used as simulators and as a self-supervised training signal. In driving the open questions are latency, causal confusion and the safety argument, so they run beside a conventional fast planner."

11Evaluating generative models and VLMs

A classifier is right or wrong; a generated image has no single right answer, and a VLM's paragraph can be fluent and wrong in one detail. Evaluation of these models therefore measures distributions (do the samples look like real data, all of it?), agreement with the prompt, and faithfulness to the image, and every metric measures only part of what people mean by "good".

11.1FID and its relatives

The Fréchet Inception Distance (Heusel et al., 2017) compares the distribution of generated images with that of real images in a feature space. Both sets, conventionally 50 000 images each, are passed through an Inception-v3 network trained on ImageNet; the 2 048-dimensional pooled features of each set are summarised by their mean \(\mu\) and covariance \(\Sigma\); and the distance between the two Gaussians is

\[ \mathrm{FID} = \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\,(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2}\right). \tag{11.1}\]

Lower is better, and FID responds to both quality (features of bad images sit elsewhere) and diversity (a collapsed generator has a too-small covariance). It is also full of traps. It is biased by sample size: estimated from finite samples, even a perfect model gets FID above zero, and the bias shrinks roughly like \(1/N\). In the experiment behind Figure 11.1, a model whose true FID is 10 measures 12.6 with 1 000 samples per side, 10.6 with 5 000 and 10.1 with 20 000, and a perfect model measures 2.6, 0.6 and 0.1. FIDs computed with different N are not comparable. It is sensitive to implementation details: the image resizing filter and JPEG compression change scores by several points, which is why "clean-FID" fixed the pipeline. And it measures closeness in the eyes of an ImageNet classifier, whose features under-weight things people care about (text, hands, faces at small scale); FD computed with DINOv2 features agrees better with human judgement. Kernel Inception Distance (KID) is an unbiased alternative for small sample sizes. Precision and recall for generative models (Figure 11.1, left) split FID's single number into fidelity (what fraction of generated samples lies within the real data's manifold) and coverage (what fraction of real data is near some generated sample), which makes mode collapse visible as high precision with low recall.

11.2Prompt adherence and human preference

FID says nothing about whether the image shows what the prompt asked for. CLIP score is the cosine between the image's and the prompt's CLIP embeddings (reported as 100 × cosine, or as 2.5 × cosine in the original CLIPScore paper); it is cheap and correlates with adherence, but inherits CLIP's bag-of-words blindness (Section 6.4): "a red cube on a blue sphere" gets the same score as the swapped scene, and it can be gamed by models trained against CLIP. Compositional benchmarks such as GenEval and T2I-CompBench generate images for structured prompts ("two dogs and a red bench to the left of a tree") and check counts, colours and positions with detectors, which measures exactly what CLIP score misses. Learned preference models (HPSv2, ImageReward, PickScore), trained on millions of human comparisons between images for the same prompt, predict which image people would pick. And human evaluation remains the final word: side-by-side comparisons by raters on a fixed prompt set, or public arenas that rank models by Elo-style ratings from pairwise votes. Products track a dashboard of all of these on a fixed internal prompt set, because each single metric can be improved while images get worse.

11.3Evaluating VLMs and measuring hallucination

VLM benchmarks fall into a few groups: general knowledge and reasoning with images (MMMU, MMBench), reading-heavy tasks (DocVQA, scored with ANLS, a string similarity that forgives small OCR differences; ChartQA; TextVQA; OCRBench), grounding (RefCOCO-style box accuracy, ScreenSpot for GUI elements) and hallucination. For hallucination, POPE asks "Is there a {object} in the image?" for present and absent objects and reports accuracy and F1, with absent objects chosen at random, by frequency or by co-occurrence; CHAIR measures the share of objects mentioned in free captions that are not in the image. Public benchmark scores need care: test images and questions leak into web-scale training data, multiple-choice formats can be partly answered without the image (a useful check is to run the benchmark with a blank image), and LLM-as-judge scoring of free answers has its own biases. For the document service of scenario 2 the evaluation that matters is a private set of a few hundred real documents per type, scored field by field with exact-match rules for amounts and dates, with a separate count of confidently wrong values, because those are the errors that get paid. Evaluation and observability of LLM systems in general are in Generative AI 10.

Talk track

"FID compares Inception-feature statistics of 50 000 generated and real images; it captures quality and diversity but is biased at small sample sizes, sensitive to implementation details, and blind to text and hands, so you compare only at equal N with the same code, and add precision-recall to see mode collapse. Prompt adherence needs CLIP score plus compositional benchmarks like GenEval, because CLIP is a bag of words; preference models and human side-by-side tests decide in the end. For VLMs I would use public benchmarks for orientation, POPE and CHAIR for hallucination, and a private field-level test set for the product."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

What is the difference between a discriminative and a generative model?Basic

A discriminative model learns p(y | x), the boundary between classes given an input. A generative model learns the data distribution p(x) or p(x | c) itself, so it can sample new examples, fill in missing parts and judge plausibility. Generative modelling is harder because it must describe everything about the data, not only what separates classes.

How does a VAE work, and what is the reparameterisation trick?Basic

The encoder outputs a mean and variance per latent dimension; a latent is sampled from that Gaussian and decoded. The loss is reconstruction error plus a KL term pulling each encoder Gaussian towards N(0, I), so the whole latent space is filled and random latents decode to plausible images. Sampling is written as z = μ + σ·ε with ε from N(0, I), which moves the randomness into an input and lets gradients flow through μ and σ.

Why are VAE outputs blurry?Intermediate

The decoder is trained with squared error. When the latent does not determine a detail (an exact edge position, a hair), the squared-error-optimal output is the average of the possibilities, which is a soft blur. Fixes replace the pixel loss with perceptual and adversarial losses, or use the VAE only for compression and let a diffusion model choose the latent.

Explain how a GAN is trained and why it is unstable.Basic

A generator maps noise to images and a discriminator classifies real versus fake; they are updated in alternation, the generator using the gradient of the discriminator's verdict. It is a two-player game rather than a minimisation, so updates can oscillate. When the real and generated distributions do not overlap, which is typical in high dimensions, a strong discriminator separates them perfectly and the JS divergence saturates at log 2, giving no useful gradient. Nothing in the loss rewards covering all modes, so mode collapse is common.

What is mode collapse and how do you detect and reduce it?Intermediate

The generator produces only a small subset of the data's variety, for example the same face pose for every seed. Detect it with sample grids, low recall in precision-recall metrics, or a too-small feature covariance (FID). Reduce it with Wasserstein losses with gradient penalty, spectral normalisation, minibatch statistics given to the discriminator, or by using a diffusion model instead.

Diffusion versus GAN: what are the trade-offs?Basic

Diffusion trains with a stable regression loss and covers the whole distribution, handles text conditioning and editing naturally, but needs 20 to 50 network passes per image (1 to 4 after distillation). A GAN produces an image in one pass and is sharp, but trains unstably and drops modes. Today diffusion is the default for open-ended generation and GAN losses are used to distil diffusion models into few-step generators and in super-resolution.

Describe the forward and reverse process of a diffusion model.Basic

The forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise over T steps until the image is pure noise; it has a closed form x_t = √ᾱ_t x₀ + √(1−ᾱ_t) ε, so any step can be sampled directly. The reverse process is learned: a network predicts the noise in x_t given t and the condition, which is equivalent to predicting the clean image. Sampling starts from noise and repeatedly applies the network to move to lower noise levels.

What does the network in a diffusion model actually predict, and why is that a regression?Intermediate

Usually the added noise ε (or the clean image, or a velocity mixing both). With squared error, the optimum is the conditional mean E[x₀ | x_t], the best estimate of the clean image given the noisy one; the predicted noise is proportional to the score, the gradient of the log-density of noisy data. Since each training example is "noisy input, known target", it is plain supervised regression, which is why training is stable.

What is the difference between DDPM and DDIM sampling?Intermediate

DDPM follows the stochastic reverse Markov chain step by step and adds fresh noise each step, needing hundreds to a thousand steps. DDIM uses the same trained network but a deterministic update: predict x₀, then jump to a much lower noise level along the consistent noise direction. It needs 20 to 50 steps, makes the result a deterministic function of the starting noise (seeds, interpolation, inversion), and corresponds to solving the probability-flow ODE.

How does classifier-free guidance work?Basic

During training the condition is dropped for about 10 % of examples, so one model learns both conditional and unconditional predictions. At sampling time both are computed and combined as ε_u + w (ε_c − ε_u). w = 1 is the plain conditional model; larger w pushes samples towards what makes the prompt obviously true, improving adherence and reducing diversity, and very large w oversaturates. It costs two passes per step.

What is latent diffusion and why is it used?Basic

A VAE compresses images (SD 1.x: 512×512×3 to 64×64×4, 48× fewer values), the diffusion model runs entirely in that latent space, and the decoder produces pixels once at the end. It cuts compute per step dramatically and lets the diffusion model focus on content while the decoder handles perceptual detail. The text enters through cross-attention from a text encoder.

What is flow matching and how does it relate to diffusion?Intermediate

Flow matching trains a network to predict the velocity along straight-line interpolations x_t = (1−t)x₀ + tε, with target ε − x₀, and samples by integrating that velocity from noise to data with an ODE solver. Diffusion uses a curved variance-preserving path and predicts noise. They are closely related (equivalent up to time reparametrisation and loss weighting); flow matching is simpler and its paths are straighter, so fewer steps are needed. SD3 and FLUX use it.

How do image-to-image editing and inpainting work with a diffusion model?Intermediate

Image-to-image encodes the input, noises it to an intermediate level set by the strength and runs the remaining denoising steps with the new prompt; coarse structure survives the noise, detail is regenerated. Inpainting regenerates only inside a mask, either by pasting the noised original back outside the mask at every step or, better, with a model that receives the mask and masked image as extra input channels.

What is ControlNet and why are zero convolutions used?Intermediate

ControlNet adds spatial control (edges, depth, pose) to a frozen diffusion model through a trainable copy of its encoder that reads the control image; the copy's features are added to the frozen model via 1×1 convolutions initialised to zero. Zero initialisation means the model is unchanged at the start of training, so the base quality is preserved and the control is learned gradually, without destroying the pretrained weights with random noise.

What is LoRA and why is it popular for image models?Basic

LoRA freezes each weight matrix W and learns a low-rank update BA with small rank r; B starts at zero so training begins from the base model. It trains a few percent of the parameters (12 288 instead of 589 824 for a 768×768 matrix at r = 8), produces files of megabytes, allows swapping and mixing styles per request, and can be merged into W for no inference overhead.

How does CLIP work, and how is it used for zero-shot classification?Basic

CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder on 400 million web image-caption pairs. In each batch it computes the N×N cosine similarities between all images and captions, and a symmetric cross-entropy (InfoNCE) loss with a learned temperature pushes true pairs up and the rest down. For zero-shot classification, each class name is put into a template such as "a photo of a {}", embedded, and the image is assigned to the closest class embedding.

Why does CLIP need a temperature and large batches?Intermediate

Cosines between unit vectors differ only by tenths, so without scaling the softmax is nearly uniform and gradients are weak; dividing by τ (learned, starting at 0.07, capped at 1/100) sharpens it. The batch provides the negatives: with 32 768 pairs, each image must beat 32 767 wrong captions, which forces fine-grained features. SigLIP's sigmoid loss reduces the dependence on huge batches.

Describe the architecture of a typical vision-language model.Basic

A vision encoder (usually a CLIP or SigLIP ViT) turns image patches into vectors; a projector, typically a two-layer MLP, often after merging 2×2 patches, maps them into the LLM's embedding width; these visual tokens are placed in the LLM's input next to the text tokens, and the LLM generates the answer. The alternative inserts cross-attention layers into the LLM (Flamingo, Llama 3.2 Vision). Training aligns the projector first, then instruction-tunes on QA, documents and grounding data, then applies preference tuning.

How do VLMs handle high-resolution documents?Intermediate

A fixed 336 px input leaves 10 pt text about 4 px tall, unreadable. Models tile the image into encoder-sized crops plus a thumbnail (LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL) or run the encoder at native resolution with a pixel budget (Qwen2-VL, one token per 28×28 px), and merge neighbouring patches to reduce tokens. The trade-off is LLM cost: every visual token costs like a text token, so budgets are chosen per input type.

Why do VLMs hallucinate and how do you reduce it?Intermediate

The LLM's language prior dominates when visual evidence is weak: too low resolution, small or ambiguous objects, training data written from captions that over-claim, and long answers that drift from the image. Reduce it with sufficient resolution, grounded answers with boxes that can be verified, schema-constrained extraction, allowing "not visible", preference tuning against hallucinated answers, and consistency checks such as line items summing to the total. Measure with POPE, CHAIR and a private field-level test set.

How does an open-vocabulary detector differ from YOLO?Basic

A closed-set detector like YOLO ends in a classification layer with fixed classes. An open-vocabulary detector produces an embedding per box and scores it against text embeddings of prompt phrases, so the classes are given at run time. Grounding DINO fuses text and image features deeply; YOLO-World folds a fixed vocabulary into the weights for real-time speed. Open-vocabulary models are slower, less precise and need per-phrase thresholds, so they are mostly used as offline teachers.

How does SAM work, and why does it output three masks?Intermediate

A heavy ViT image encoder runs once per image; a prompt encoder embeds clicks, boxes or masks; a light two-layer mask decoder produces masks in milliseconds per prompt. A click is ambiguous (object, part, sub-part), and a single-output model trained on ambiguous targets would average them; SAM outputs three masks with predicted IoU and trains only on the best one. It was trained on SA-1B, 1.1 billion masks from a model-in-the-loop data engine.

How would you add a new object class to a production detector using foundation models?Advanced

Mine candidate frames with CLIP text and image search; run an open-vocabulary detector with several tuned prompts; refine with SAM and propagate through video; filter with per-prompt thresholds, geometry and temporal consistency; send a random sample and the uncertain score band to human review; train the compact closed-set student on the result; evaluate on a human-labelled audit set per slice, never labelled by the same teacher. Optionally add inpainted rare cases, filtered by re-detection, and judge them on real data only.

How is FID computed and what are its weaknesses?Intermediate

Pass 50 000 real and 50 000 generated images through Inception-v3, fit a Gaussian (mean and covariance) to the 2 048-d features of each set, and compute the Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians. Weaknesses: biased upwards at finite N (compare only at equal N), sensitive to resizing and compression details, based on ImageNet features that ignore text, hands and fine detail, and blind to prompt adherence. Use KID for small samples, DINOv2 features, precision-recall and prompt-adherence metrics alongside it.

What is a vision-language-action model?Intermediate

A VLM fine-tuned on demonstrations to output actions. RT-2 and OpenVLA discretise each action dimension into 256 bins and decode them as tokens; π0 adds a flow-matching action expert that generates chunks of 50 continuous actions; driving research models write trajectories as text. Generative action heads matter because demonstrations are multi-modal and regression would average them. Latency and safety arguments are the main obstacles in driving.

Q.2Why and what if

Why can't you generate images by sampling random latents from a plain autoencoder?Why

Nothing in its training constrains the latent space between the codes of training images; random codes or interpolations can land in regions the decoder never saw and decode to garbage. The VAE's sampling noise and KL term are what force the codes to fill a known region that you can sample from.

Why does a one-step diffusion sampler produce a blurry average, and why does distillation fix it?Why

With one step the sampler outputs the denoiser's prediction from pure noise, E[x₀ | x_T], which is the mean of the data (Section 4.3). Distilled models are trained differently: consistency training maps any point on a trajectory to its end point, and adversarial distillation adds a GAN loss that forbids averaged outputs, so the one-step student commits to a sharp sample.

Why does image-to-image with low strength keep the composition?Why

Noise destroys fine detail first and large structures last. At a low noise level the layout and colours are still determined by the noisy input, so the remaining denoising steps only re-decide details. At high strength little survives except the coarse layout.

What if you set the CFG scale to 0, to 1 and to 20?What if

At 0 the prompt is ignored (unconditional samples). At 1 you get the plain conditional model, which follows the prompt loosely. At 20 samples are pushed far beyond typical images of the prompt: oversaturated colours, exaggerated features, artefacts and very little variety between seeds. The interactive in Section 4.5 shows the same in 2D: samples move to the far side of the class and partly off the data.

Why did Stable Diffusion 1.5 struggle to make a pure black image?Why

Its training schedule ends at SNR 0.0047, so even at the last step about 7 % of the image's amplitude is present and the model learns that low frequencies like mean brightness are always partly visible. At sampling it starts from pure noise with average brightness zero and reads that as "average brightness", so it stays near medium. Rescaling the schedule to zero terminal SNR (with v-prediction) fixes it.

What if a perfect denoiser were trained on a small dataset?What if

It would reproduce training images exactly: the exact posterior mean of a finite dataset converges to one training example, as the stepper in Section 4.3 shows (the sample equals training image 82). Real models generalise because their architecture and limited capacity smooth between examples; when a model can memorise, for example on duplicated images, it copies them.

Why does CLIP fail at "a red cube on a blue sphere" versus "a blue cube on a red sphere"?Why

Its contrastive objective only needs to separate a caption from random other captions in the batch, which almost never differ only in word order or attribute binding. The text encoder therefore learns something close to a bag of words, and both captions get nearly the same embedding. Generators conditioned only on CLIP inherit this; adding a T5 encoder improved composition.

What if you set one global score threshold for all prompts of an open-vocabulary detector?What if

Frequent words like "car" score high and rare words like "e-scooter" score low even for clear examples, so one threshold gives too many false positives for some phrases and misses most instances of others. Thresholds must be chosen per phrase on a labelled sample.

Why do VLMs often get a receipt's format right and one digit wrong?Why

The format (currency, decimal separator, plausible magnitude) is predicted well by the language prior, while the individual digits need pixels that may be lost at the chosen resolution. When the visual tokens do not resolve the digit, the model still outputs the most plausible one. Higher resolution, grounding the value with a box and arithmetic consistency checks catch it.

What if you double the image resolution given to a native-resolution VLM?What if

The number of visual tokens roughly quadruples (one token per 28×28 px in both directions), so prefill compute and memory grow about four times and the context fills faster, while text gets twice as tall in pixels. Accuracy on small text improves until the text is comfortably resolved, after which extra tokens only cost.

Why use auto-labels for training but not for testing?Why

Auto-label errors are systematic: the teacher misses the same kinds of objects every time. A student trained on them inherits the blind spots, and a test set labelled by the same teacher marks those blind spots as correct, so the evaluation cannot see the problem. Human-labelled test and audit sets break the loop.

What if synthetic images make up most of the training data for a class?What if

The detector can learn generator artefacts (texture, noise statistics) as a cue for the class and lose accuracy on real images, and any systematic mismatch between requested and generated objects becomes label noise. Keep synthetic data a minority, filter it by re-detection, and measure on real data with and without it, including on the common slices.

Why do robot policies use diffusion or flow heads instead of regressing the action?Why

Demonstrations are multi-modal: at the same state different demonstrators go left or right. Regression with squared error outputs their average, which may go straight into the obstacle. A generative head samples one of the modes, and producing chunks of actions gives smooth, consistent motion.

Why can FID improve while images look worse?Why

FID measures Inception-feature statistics, which emphasise ImageNet-relevant textures and objects and largely ignore text, hands, small faces and prompt adherence. A model can match those statistics better while degrading what people notice, and implementation or sample-size differences can move FID by more than the real change. Pair it with human or preference-model evaluation and fixed-procedure metrics.
