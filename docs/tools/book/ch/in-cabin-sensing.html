In-cabin sensing: driver and occupant monitoring

Why cars now watch the people inside them, and how they do it: near-infrared cameras and illumination, the face, eye and gaze pipeline, drowsiness and distraction detection, occupant and child-presence sensing, the EU, UN and Euro NCAP rules that ask for all of it, and the data, privacy, compute and warning design behind a product. The running example is one camera-based driver and occupant monitoring system for a 2026 European car with Level 2 assistance.

1The big picture: why the car watches the driver

For a hundred years the car assumed that the driver was looking at the road and awake. The data say that this assumption fails often enough to cause a large share of crashes, and partial automation makes it fail more often, not less. In-cabin sensing replaces the assumption with a measurement.

1.1The driver is the least monitored part of the car

A modern car checks its brake pressure, its tyre pressure, its battery cells and the temperature of its camera sensor, many times per second. Until recently it checked nothing about the person who decides where the car goes. That person looks away from the road, gets tired, drinks, takes medication, has a seizure or a heart attack, and the car simply carries on. Every driver-assistance function built on top of that blind spot inherits it: lane keeping that steers well is a hazard if the driver has stopped watching because the lane keeping steers well.

The numbers that made regulators act come from naturalistic driving studies, where ordinary drivers drive instrumented cars for months. The best known, the US 100-Car Naturalistic Driving Study (Klauer and colleagues, NHTSA, 2006), found that glances away from the forward roadway longer than two seconds roughly doubled the risk of a crash or near-crash, and that drowsiness contributed to about a fifth of the crashes and near-crashes it recorded. Later and larger studies (SHRP2 in the US, UDRIVE in Europe) refined the numbers but not the picture: long glances away and fatigue are among the strongest predictors of a crash that a sensor can actually measure. The sensor that measures them best is a camera looking at the driver's face.

There are three different failures hiding behind the word "inattention", and they need different measurements. Distraction is attention that went somewhere else: eyes on a phone, hands on a screen, mind on a conversation. It switches on and off in seconds. Drowsiness is the slow loss of the ability to stay alert; it develops over tens of minutes and ends in microsleeps, short episodes in which the eyes close and the brain stops processing the road. Impairment by alcohol, drugs or illness changes how the eyes and the steering behave over minutes, and its extreme is the unresponsive driver who no longer reacts to anything. A driver monitoring system (DMS) has to recognise all three, on time scales from a second to an hour.

Why it matters

Since 7 July 2026 every new car and van registered in the EU must have an advanced driver distraction warning that is, in practice, a camera watching the driver's eyes. Euro NCAP gives up to 25 points in its 2026 Driver Engagement protocol to driver monitoring, and Level 2 and Level 3 regulations make the monitoring a condition of approval. In-cabin sensing went from a premium option to a mandatory part of almost every car in about five years.

1.2Automation makes monitoring more important, not less

It is tempting to think that better assistance makes the driver matter less. The opposite is true for everything below full automation. At Level 2 the system steers and brakes, but the driver remains responsible and must watch the road (the levels are explained in Planning and control 1.3). Human-factors research has shown for decades that people are bad at supervising automation that works well most of the time: attention drifts, glances away get longer, and the reaction when the system finally fails is slow. The better the Level 2 system, the stronger this effect. Regulators answered by making the supervision measurable: UN R171 for driver control assistance systems lets a car offer more automation only if it checks that the driver's eyes are on the road.

At Level 3, for example under UN R157 (automated lane keeping), the driver may look away and read, but must be able to take over within seconds when the system asks. That ability is called driver availability, and the regulation requires the car to confirm it continuously: the driver is in the seat, belted, and shows signs of being awake and responsive. A sleeping driver is not available, even if the car currently drives itself perfectly, because the car may need them in ten seconds. In the minimal-risk logic of an automated car (Planning and control 11.3), the driver monitor decides whether a takeover request can succeed or whether the car must stop on its own.

1.3The other people in the car

The driver is one person; the cabin may hold four more. Occupant monitoring (OMS) answers different questions. Is someone in the front passenger seat, and is it an adult or a rear-facing child seat, so that the passenger airbag can be switched on or off automatically? Is every occupant belted, and is the belt routed correctly, across the shoulder and not behind the back? Is anyone sitting in a position where an airbag would hurt them, such as feet on the dashboard? After a crash, how many people were in the car, so that the emergency call can tell the rescue services? And after the car is locked, is a child still inside?

The last question has a grim background. Children left in parked cars die of heatstroke because a closed car in the sun heats up within minutes to temperatures a small child cannot survive. In the United States, the data collected by San José State University's NoHeatStroke.org project show an average of about 37 such deaths a year since 1998, most of them children forgotten by a parent whose routine changed. A sensor that notices a breathing child in a locked car and raises an alarm prevents exactly these cases, which is why Euro NCAP rewards child presence detection and requires it to detect the child directly, rather than infer it from a rear door having been opened at the start of the trip.

1.4The running example

Throughout the chapter we follow one product: the in-cabin system of a European mid-size car launched in 2026. It has Level 2 assistance with hands-off driving on motorways under R171, it must meet the EU General Safety Regulation (driver drowsiness and attention warning, DDAW, and advanced driver distraction warning, ADDW), and the manufacturer wants the maximum Euro NCAP points for driver engagement and occupant monitoring. The hardware is a near-infrared DMS camera on the steering column, a wide RGB-IR occupant camera next to the rear-view mirror, and a 60 GHz radar in the headliner for the rear seats. The software runs on the same system-on-chip as the cockpit or on a small dedicated ECU, which is a decision we will revisit in Section 9.

The questions the team has to answer are the ones this chapter is built around. Which camera, wavelength and illumination see the eyes of every driver, by day and night, through sunglasses? How do we turn pixels into eyelid aperture, head pose and gaze direction, and how accurate must those be? How do we turn those per-frame measurements into "distracted", "drowsy" or "unresponsive" without annoying alert drivers? How do we find children, belts and out-of-position passengers? What exactly do the rules require, and how is that tested? And how do we prove all of it on data that respects the occupants' privacy?

2Sensors and optics: seeing eyes in every light

A DMS camera must see a pupil a few millimetres wide, at night, in direct sunlight and behind sunglasses, without the driver noticing any light. That single requirement explains almost every hardware choice: infrared illumination at 940 nm, a global-shutter sensor, short exposures synchronised with LED pulses, and a careful placement.

2.1Why the camera brings its own invisible light

A visible-light camera depends on ambient light, and the ambient light in a cabin is terrible: total darkness at night, lit from below by a screen, half the face in direct sun and half in shadow through the side window, flickering stripes of light under trees. Pupils, eyelids and the corneal reflection that gaze estimation needs would come and go with the light. Lighting the driver's face with visible light at night is out of the question because it would dazzle them.

The solution is to bring a light source the driver cannot see: near-infrared (NIR) LEDs or lasers next to the camera, and a lens with a band-pass filter that lets mostly that wavelength through. The face is then lit by a known, constant source from a known direction, by day and night, and the image looks the same at midnight and at noon. Silicon sensors are still sensitive in the near infrared (less than in the visible, see Camera pipeline, the lens), so the same kind of sensor that serves a front camera works here, without its usual IR-cut filter. The NIR camera is a different device from the thermal cameras some cars use outside (Automotive sensors 2.5): it sees reflected light a few hundred nanometres beyond red, not emitted heat.

2.2850 or 940 nanometres: a choice made by the atmosphere

Two wavelengths dominate: 850 nm and 940 nm. At 850 nm silicon is more sensitive and LEDs are more efficient, so the same electrical power gives a brighter image. But 850 nm LEDs glow visibly dark red, which drivers notice at night as a red dot in the corner of their eye, and above all the sun is strong at 850 nm. At 940 nm the LEDs are invisible, and the sun is much weaker, because water vapour in the atmosphere absorbs sunlight in a band around 930–950 nm.

Figure 2.1 shows this with the ASTM G173 reference spectrum, the standard model of sunlight at ground level used for solar-panel testing. Integrated over a ±25 nm band, sunlight brings about 46 W/m² around 850 nm and only 20 W/m² around 940 nm, a factor of 2.3. Since direct sunlight on the face is the illumination the DMS must overpower, that factor goes straight into the image quality on a sunny day: the share of the image that comes from our controlled LED rather than from the sun is twice as large at 940 nm. Almost every current DMS therefore uses 940 nm and accepts the lower silicon sensitivity, compensated by sensors with thicker or specially structured pixels for better NIR quantum efficiency.

Numbers

From the reference spectrum: sun in 850 ± 25 nm 46 W/m², in 940 ± 25 nm 20 W/m², in 940 ± 5 nm 2.6 W/m². Two 940 nm LEDs with 1.5 W/sr peak radiant intensity each give 7.1 W/m² peak at a face 0.65 m away: only a third of the sunlight in a 50 nm band, but almost three times the sunlight in a 10 nm band. That is the argument for VCSEL illuminators, whose spectrum is about 1 nm wide and barely shifts with temperature, so the lens filter can be narrow. An LED's spectrum is 30–50 nm wide and shifts by roughly 0.3 nm per kelvin, so its filter must be wide.

2.3Global shutter and pulsed illumination

The trick that makes a few watts of infrared compete with the sun is timing. A DMS camera uses a global-shutter sensor, which exposes all pixels during the same short window (the difference from rolling shutter is explained in Camera pipeline, rolling versus global shutter). The illuminator is switched on only during that window, typically 0.5–1.5 ms out of a 33 ms frame at 30 frames per second (Figure 2.2). Two things follow. The sensor collects sunlight for only 1 ms, so the sunlight per frame is small. And because the LED is on for only about 3 % of the time, it can be driven with a much higher peak current than it could survive continuously, while its average power, which decides both heat and eye safety, stays low.

The ratio is what matters. In the experiment, the same average LED power spread over a continuous 33 ms exposure would reach only about one hundredth of the sunlight in the band; concentrated into a 1 ms pulse it reaches a third of it with a wide filter and nearly three times it with a narrow one. A rolling-shutter sensor cannot use this trick, because its rows are exposed at different times and the LED would have to stay on for the whole readout. This is why DMS cameras are global-shutter even though global-shutter pixels are larger and more expensive than rolling-shutter ones.

Many systems alternate between two illuminators, one on each side of the lens, from frame to frame, and some alternate between an illuminated and a dark frame. Subtracting the dark frame from the lit one removes most of the sunlight and leaves only the face as lit by the LED; the price is that motion between the two frames creates edges in the difference image, so this needs a high frame rate.

2.4Eye safety: the light shines into the eyes on purpose

A DMS illuminator is unusual among light sources: its whole job is to shine into people's eyes for hours. Infrared is invisible, so the blink reflex and the pupil, which protect the eye from bright visible light, do not react to it. The thermal and photochemical effects on the cornea, lens and retina are still real, which is why the illuminator must be shown to be safe by the photobiological safety standard IEC 62471 (lamps and LEDs) or, for laser illuminators such as VCSELs, the laser safety standard IEC 60825-1, where the target is Class 1.

IEC 62471 sorts sources into an exempt group and risk groups 1 to 3. For infrared the two relevant limits are the irradiance on the cornea and lens, summed over 780–3000 nm, which for the exempt group may not exceed 100 W/m² for exposures longer than 1000 seconds, and the radiance limit for the retina, which depends on the apparent size of the source. Pulsed sources are assessed on their time-averaged values, which is where the low duty cycle of Section 2.3 helps again. In the example, two LEDs give 75 W/m² peak at the standard's 200 mm measurement distance, but only 2.3 W/m² averaged over time with a 3 % duty cycle, far inside the exempt limit; at the driver's real distance of about 0.65 m the average is 0.2 W/m². For comparison, direct sunlight carries about 430 W/m² in the same 780–3000 nm band, which is why the standard's long-exposure limit is not about everyday sunlight but about staring into a concentrated source.

Pitfall

Eye safety must hold in the failure case, not only in the nominal one. If a software bug or a short circuit leaves the LED on continuously at its pulse current, the average irradiance rises thirty-fold. Production designs therefore limit the pulse length and duty cycle in hardware (a driver with a maximum on-time, or a watchdog in the LED driver), so that no software state can turn an exempt illuminator into a risk-group source. This is a classic safety requirement for the illuminator path in the functional safety analysis.

2.5Placement, glasses and sunglasses

Where the camera sits decides what it can see. Figure 2.3 shows the usual positions. A camera on the steering column or in the instrument cluster looks at the driver from slightly below and in front, which gives the best view of the eyes and catches the eyes even when the head is tilted down; but the steering-wheel rim and spokes cross the image when the wheel turns, and the driver's hands cover the camera. A camera at the rear-view mirror or overhead console sees the whole cabin, including the passengers, but it sees the driver's face from the side and above, so the eyes are smaller and eyelids hide more of the pupil when the driver looks down. Some cars integrate the camera into the A-pillar or the top of the central display. Many premium designs use two cameras: a narrow-field driver camera with 40–60° field of view and a wide occupant camera with 100–150°.

Glasses are the hardest everyday case. Clear spectacle lenses reflect the illuminator, and when the reflection lands on top of the eye in the image, the eye is invisible. Figure 2.4 shows the standard counter-measure: two illuminators at different positions, alternated frame by frame, so that the reflection moves; each eye is then measured in the frame where it is not covered. Placing the illuminators far enough from the lens axis that lens reflections mostly miss the camera helps as well. The same figure shows a second geometric effect: when the illuminator is very close to the camera axis, light reflected from the retina comes back into the lens and the pupil appears bright (the red-eye effect of flash photography, here in infrared); when it is off-axis, the pupil is dark. Some eye trackers use both on purpose and find the pupil in the difference image.

Sunglasses are a matter of spectral transmission. Many sunglass lenses absorb visible light but transmit much of the near infrared, so a 940 nm camera sees the eyes through them almost as if they were clear. Some lenses, especially some mirrored, polarised or high-end coated ones, block the infrared as well, and then the eyes are simply not visible. A DMS must detect that case and fall back to head pose for attention, and it must say so to the downstream logic, rather than reporting "eyes open, looking ahead". Regulations account for this: the EU distraction-warning test uses drivers without glasses unless the manufacturer chooses otherwise, while OEM specifications and consumer tests push for robustness across eyewear.

In practice

A DMS evaluation set has a separate stratum for eyewear: none, clear glasses (thin and thick frames, anti-reflection coated or not), sunglasses with high and low NIR transmission, and safety glasses for commercial vehicles. The worst cases are often not sunglasses but thick-framed clear glasses, whose frame hides the eyelid exactly where the eye-closure measurement looks.

2.6RGB-IR: one camera for monitoring and for video calls

The occupant camera has a second life as a consumer feature: video calls, cabin selfies, recording a child in the back seat for the parents in front. Those need colour, and the monitoring needs NIR at night. An RGB-IR sensor provides both by replacing some of the colour filters of the Bayer pattern with infrared-pass filters. The common 4×4 pattern in Figure 2.5 keeps half the pixels green, makes a quarter of them IR, and halves the red and blue samples.

The catch is that an RGB-IR camera cannot have an IR-cut filter, so its colour pixels also see the infrared. By day, the colour image is contaminated by NIR (foliage looks pinkish, black fabrics look purple) unless the ISP estimates the IR part from the neighbouring IR pixels and subtracts it; at night, the IR pixels alone give the monitoring image, at a quarter of the resolution unless the ISP interpolates. ISPs that support RGB-IR have a dedicated block for this decontamination and for remosaicing the pattern back to Bayer so that the rest of the pipeline can stay standard; the tuning order is described in ISP engineering 8.1. For the driver camera itself, a pure monochrome NIR sensor remains common because it gives the eyes the full resolution and sensitivity.

2.7Radar and time of flight for the rest of the cabin

Cameras need a line of sight. A baby in a rear-facing seat under a sunshade, a toddler asleep in a footwell under a blanket, or a child hidden by the front seat backrest is invisible to a mirror-mounted camera. Radar sees through fabric and plastic, and it measures motion with extraordinary sensitivity, which is why most direct child presence detection uses a 60 GHz radar in the headliner. The physics is the same as for the front radar (Automotive sensors 3.2 and 3.4): an FMCW chirp gives range, the phase of the echo changes with sub-millimetre motion, and an antenna array gives angle. At 60 GHz the wavelength is 5 mm and a sweep of 4 GHz gives 3.75 cm range resolution, enough to separate seats and footwells; Section 6.4 shows how breathing appears in the phase.

Two other sensors appear in occupant monitoring. Indirect time-of-flight (ToF) cameras measure depth per pixel by modulating their infrared light and measuring the phase of its return; they give a 3D view of the cabin at modest resolution, which makes body pose, seat occupancy and out-of-position detection easier than from a 2D image, and are also used for gesture control. And the car already has sensors in the seats: weight-sensing mats or pressure sensors for the seat-belt reminder and airbag suppression, and buckle switches. A good OMS fuses them: the camera says "something child-sized is in the passenger seat", the mat says "it weighs 14 kg", and together they decide that the airbag must be off. Some systems also reuse the ultra-wideband anchors of a smartphone digital key as a short-range radar for presence detection.

3The face and eye pipeline

Every frame, the DMS must find the driver's face, locate the eyelids and pupils to a fraction of a millimetre, estimate how the head is turned, and decide how open each eye is. Everything later, gaze, distraction and drowsiness, is built from these few numbers, so their accuracy and their honesty about failure decide the quality of the whole system.

3.1The pipeline, step by step

The per-frame pipeline in Figure 3.1 has the same shape in almost every product, whether it was built from classic computer vision ten years ago or from neural networks today. A face detector finds the face, a landmark model places points on its features, a 3D face model turns those points into a head pose, eye-specific models measure the eyelids and the pupil, a gaze model combines head and eyes into a direction, and temporal logic turns the per-frame numbers into states over seconds and minutes. Step through the figure to see what each stage hands to the next.

One property separates a production pipeline from a demo: it knows when it is blind. Each output carries flags such as face found, eyes visible, eyewear detected, camera blocked. A demo that loses the eyes keeps reporting the last value or a default; a production system reports "unknown", and the downstream logic must decide what unknown means. For a distraction timer, a driver whose face has turned out of view is most likely looking away, so unknown should count towards distraction, not reset it. The EU distraction-warning regulation even requires a failure warning if the sensor is covered.

3.2Detection and tracking in a cabin

Face detection in a cabin is easier than in the wild in one way: the camera, the seat and the steering wheel are fixed, so the driver's face appears in a predictable region and at a predictable scale. It is harder in others: the image is NIR, so detectors trained on colour photographs need retraining or at least fine-tuning; the face is often partly occluded by a hand, a cup, the wheel rim, a mask or a hat; and the driver turns far away from the camera when checking the blind spot, which at 70–90° yaw leaves only a profile. A good pipeline keeps tracking the head through such turns, even if eye measurements stop, because "head turned 80° to the left for one second" is useful information about a shoulder check, while "face lost" is not.

Modern products typically use a compact single-stage detector (a small anchor-free CNN in the style described in Perception tasks) trained on NIR cabin data, followed by a tracker that predicts the face box from frame to frame and only calls the detector when needed. The detector also gives the first rough pose, which helps choose between frontal and profile landmark models.

3.3Landmarks: a real run

To make this concrete, Figure 3.2 shows a real run of Google's MediaPipe Face Landmarker, a public model similar in spirit to what a DMS uses, on a public-domain NASA portrait. It found the face, placed 478 points, and also returned 52 "blendshape" scores (expression coefficients such as eye blink, jaw open) and a 4×4 transform from a canonical 3D face to the camera, which is the head pose. On one CPU core of the build container the whole chain took about 10 ms per frame.

The measurement that tells most about the requirements is the pixel count. In this 512×512 image the eye is 19 pixels wide and the iris 8.5 pixels across. Eyelid aperture in a normally open eye is about 10 mm, and a drowsiness system wants to resolve changes of 1 mm; on a 19-pixel eye, 1 mm is less than two pixels. That is why DMS cameras with 1–2 megapixels and a narrow field of view put 40–60 pixels across each eye, and why the eye-state models run on high-resolution crops of the eye region rather than on the whole face crop.

3.4Head pose: the face as a rigid object

Head pose is estimated by treating the face as a rigid 3D object. A mean 3D face model gives the 3D coordinates of the landmarks; the 2D landmarks found in the image and the known camera intrinsics define a perspective-n-point problem, solved for the rotation and translation that best project the model onto the image. The geometry is the same as camera calibration with a known target (Mathematics for camera systems covers rotations and projection). Networks that regress pose directly are common too, but they are trained against PnP-style labels and carry the same ambiguities.

The face is not perfectly rigid. Talking moves the jaw, smiling moves the cheeks, and a mean face model does not match a particular person's proportions, so pose estimates carry errors of a few degrees that vary with expression and identity. Two things help: using only the most rigid landmarks (eye corners, nose bridge, not the mouth) for the pose fit, and adapting the model to the driver over the first minutes of a trip. Head pose is also the main input for attention when the eyes cannot be seen, and the reference frame in which eye rotation is measured when they can.

3.5Eye openness, and why the simple ratio misleads

The best-known measure of eye openness is the eye aspect ratio (EAR) of Soukupová and Čech (2016): the two vertical distances between upper and lower eyelid divided by twice the horizontal eye width, using six landmarks per eye (Figure 3.2). It is scale-free, easy to compute from any landmark model, and works well for a frontal face: in the experiment it was 0.31 for open eyes. It is the standard method in tutorials and many papers.

Its weakness is that it is a 2D ratio, and head rotation changes 2D ratios. The experiment rotated MediaPipe's own 3D landmarks and re-projected them (Figure 3.3). Tilting the head down by 30°, as when looking at a phone in the lap, lowers the EAR of a fully open eye from 0.31 to 0.25; at 40° it drops to 0.21, the value of an eye about 35 % closed seen from the front. Turning the head sideways does the opposite: at 40° yaw the eye looks narrower, so the ratio rises to 0.41. A fixed EAR threshold therefore produces false "eyes closing" whenever the driver looks down, exactly when distraction rather than drowsiness is the issue, and misses real closures when the head is turned.

Production systems avoid the problem in three ways. They measure aperture in 3D, in millimetres, using the head pose and the eye's 3D position, so the projection is undone. They train eye-state networks directly on eye crops across head poses, with labels of open, closed and partially closed, so the network learns what a closed eye looks like from below. And they normalise to the individual: some people's open eyes are narrower than others' half-closed ones, so the system learns each driver's typical aperture during the first minutes of a trip and measures closure relative to it. Recent research follows the same direction; for example, Wolter and colleagues (arXiv, November 2025) propose an eyelid angle measured between planes fitted to the 3D eyelid landmarks, which stays within about 3° over head rotations where EAR changes strongly.

Pitfall

A demo that thresholds EAR at 0.2 works convincingly in front of a laptop, where the head is frontal and the lighting is good. In a car, the same code reports drowsiness every time the driver checks the speedometer. Whenever someone shows an eye-closure detector, ask how it behaves with the head pitched 30° down and with the face turned 45° to the side.

3.6Blinks and PERCLOS

Drowsiness changes the way people blink before it changes anything else that a camera can see. An alert person blinks around 15–20 times a minute, each blink taking roughly 100–400 ms from closing to reopening. As drowsiness builds, blinks become longer and slower, the eyelids droop between blinks, and eventually long closures of half a second or more appear; microsleeps are closures of one to several seconds during which the person does not perceive the road. The most established measure of this is PERCLOS, the percentage of time within a window during which the eyes are at least 80 % closed (Wierwille and colleagues, 1994). A US Federal Highway Administration study by Dinges and colleagues (1998) compared several candidate measures against lapses of attention in a vigilance test and found PERCLOS the most reliable, which is why it became the reference against which camera systems are still compared.

The interactive Figure 3.4 runs PERCLOS on a simulated ten-minute drive: the driver is alert for the first three minutes, with one episode of squinting into a low sun at minute 1.7, then becomes gradually drowsier, with longer blinks and more frequent long closures. The three controls are the three design choices of a real implementation. The window trades latency against noise: at the default 60 s window and a 15 % threshold the first warning comes at 7.7 minutes with no warning in the alert phase; a 20 s window warns half a minute earlier but lets the squinting episode push PERCLOS to 11 % in the alert phase, and with a threshold of 8 % that becomes a false warning; a 120 s window is calm but warns only at 8.8 minutes. The threshold moves the same trade-off. The frame rate decides how well individual blinks are measured: at 30 frames per second the measured blink durations are off by about 12 % on average, at 10 fps by 24 %, and at 5 fps 11 of 129 ordinary blinks are missed entirely.

Real systems add blink-level features to PERCLOS because PERCLOS reacts late: by the time the eyes are closed 15 % of the minute, the driver is already very drowsy. Earlier signs are the mean blink duration, the closing and reopening speed of the eyelid (slower when drowsy), the ratio of amplitude to peak velocity, and the variability of these. All of them depend on sampling the eyelid fast enough; a 150 ms blink is only four or five frames at 30 fps, which is why drowsiness research often uses 60 fps or more. The eyelid-angle paper mentioned above reports that measured closing durations changed by about a third between 30 and 50 Hz recordings, a reminder that blink features are not comparable across frame rates.

Interesting detail

The "80 % closed" in PERCLOS is relative to the individual's fully open eye, so the measure needs each driver's baseline. In the simulation, drowsy eyelids droop to about 72 % of their alert opening between blinks; that droop alone never crosses the 80 % closure line, which is why PERCLOS stays low until long closures appear. A system that measures closure against a population average instead of the driver's own baseline will see some alert people with narrow eyes as permanently drowsy.

4Gaze: where the driver is looking

Distraction is defined by where the eyes point, so gaze is the central measurement of a DMS. It is also the hardest: the eye rotates by small angles, each person's eye is a little different, and the camera sees the eye from an awkward angle. This section explains the two ways to estimate gaze, how accurate each is, and why most systems end up classifying gaze into zones rather than reporting a precise direction.

4.1Head pose is not gaze

People look at things by turning both the head and the eyes, and the share varies. To look at a mirror 40° to the left, some drivers turn the head 30° and the eyes 10°, others turn the head 10° and the eyes 30°. Looking down at the speedometer is done almost entirely with the eyes. Eye-tracking researchers sometimes call the two styles "owls" (head movers) and "lizards" (eye movers). A system that uses head pose alone therefore classifies glances to nearby targets badly, and it cannot see the classic dangerous pattern of a driver whose head faces the road while the eyes look down at a phone on the knee. Head pose is a useful fallback when the eyes are hidden by sunglasses or a steep angle, but a DMS that meets the distraction rules needs eye gaze.

4.2Model-based gaze: the geometry of the eye

The classic method, used in laboratory eye trackers for decades, builds a geometric model of the eye (Figure 4.1). The cornea is a near-spherical transparent cap over the iris; the pupil is the hole in the iris. An infrared LED makes a small bright reflection on the cornea, the glint. As the eye rotates, the pupil centre moves relative to the glint, while a small head translation moves both together. The vector from glint to pupil centre, the pupil centre corneal reflection (PCCR) method, therefore measures eye rotation in a way that tolerates head motion. With two or more LEDs and a calibrated camera, the 3D centre of the cornea and the 3D pupil centre can be triangulated, which gives the optical axis of the eye in space.

The optical axis is not where the person looks. Sharp vision happens at the fovea, which is not on the optical axis, so the visual axis differs from it by an angle called kappa, typically around 4–6° and different for each person. Laboratory eye trackers estimate kappa and the corneal radius with a calibration in which the person looks at a few known points; after that they reach accuracies around 0.5–1°. A car cannot ask every driver to calibrate. It can calibrate implicitly, by assuming that the driver looks most of the time at the road ahead and at the mirrors, and fitting the per-person offsets so that the gaze distribution matches. This kind of auto-calibration over the first minutes of a trip is standard in production systems.

Model-based gaze needs pixels: the pupil and the glint must be resolved, which brings us back to the 40–60 pixels per eye of Section 3.3. It also needs the glint, which disappears when the eye looks far away from the LED (the reflection falls off the cornea) and is confused by reflections on glasses.

4.3Appearance-based gaze: learning from images

The alternative is to train a neural network to map an image of the face or the eyes directly to a gaze direction. Appearance-based methods need no glint, work at lower resolution and with ordinary cameras, and degrade gracefully with glasses. Their typical pipeline normalises the input first: a virtual camera is rotated to look straight at the face at a fixed distance, the image is warped accordingly, and the network predicts gaze in that normalised frame, which is then rotated back. Normalisation removes much of the variation due to head position and camera placement, which is why it is used almost universally.

The accuracy is limited by the data and by the differences between people. Trained and tested on different people from the same dataset, good models reach errors of about 4–5°; tested on a different dataset (different cameras, lighting, people), errors rise to 6–10°. Large public datasets such as MPIIFaceGaze, Gaze360 and ETH-XGaze (2020, about a million images of 110 people with up to 18 cameras) drove this progress. In-vehicle data are scarcer, and recent work targets exactly that gap. IVGaze (Cheng and colleagues, CVPR 2024) collected in-vehicle gaze data from 125 people and proposed a dual-stream transformer that combines normalised and original images, plus a gaze-zone classifier that uses the geometry of the car. UniGaze (Qin, Zhang and Sugano, 2025, presented at WACV 2026) pre-trains a vision transformer self-supervised on large collections of in-the-wild face images before training for gaze, and reports clearly better cross-dataset generalisation, the property a car needs when it meets drivers that were not in any training set. DriverGaze360 (Govil, Stricker and Rambach, December 2025) moves the question outwards: a dataset of about one million frames from 19 drivers with a 360° view of the scene, for predicting which objects around the car the driver attends to.

Numbers

What a gaze error means in the car: 1° at a distance of 0.8 m is 1.4 cm, and 5° is 7 cm. A rear-view mirror about 25 cm wide and 7 cm high, some 0.7 m from the eyes, covers roughly 20° by 6°; the simplified layout of Figure 4.2 gives it 16° by 10°. Laboratory eye trackers after calibration: 0.5–1°. Calibration-free appearance-based gaze: about 4–5° within a dataset, 6–10° across datasets. Production DMS specifications typically ask for a few degrees in the central field of view and accept more at large angles.

4.4Attention zones: why most systems classify instead of measure

For distraction rules, a precise gaze direction is not the goal; the question is whether the driver looks at a driving-relevant area. Systems therefore map gaze into zones: road ahead, left and right mirrors, rear-view mirror, instrument cluster, infotainment screen, lap and footwell, side windows. The EU distraction regulation uses its own areas defined geometrically from the driver's eye point; its "area 3", where looking counts as distraction, is everything more than 30° below the eyes, which covers the lap, the phone, the centre console and the footwell (Section 7.3).

The experiment in Figure 4.2 shows why angular error matters so much more at some targets than others. Gaze samples with a Gaussian error were classified into a simple zone layout. With an error of σ = 4° a glance at the centre of the road ahead is classified correctly 98 % of the time and a glance at the centre of the cluster 91 %, but a glance at the rear-view mirror, a small zone, only 75 %, and a glance at the road just above the cluster edge only 69 %. At σ = 2° the mirror is at 99 % and the edge case still only 84 %, because a point near a boundary is ambiguous at any accuracy. The practical consequences are that small zones need good accuracy, that boundaries between relevant and irrelevant areas need a margin or a decision delay, and that the temporal logic should not react to individual frames but to glances lasting tenths of seconds.

In practice

Zone boundaries are defined per vehicle model from CAD data: the eye ellipse of the driver population, the positions of mirrors, displays and windows. When a car line gets a new interior, the zones must be updated and re-validated, which is why DMS suppliers deliver a calibration tool chain with the software, not only a trained network.

4.5Combining head and eyes over time

Gaze estimates are noisy frame by frame, and eyes move in fast saccades (20–200 ms) between fixations (200–600 ms). A gaze signal for distraction is therefore filtered into fixations and glances: a glance is the time from the moment the gaze leaves one zone until it leaves the next, including the transition. Head pose fills the gaps when eye gaze is unavailable, with a wider uncertainty. Some systems use a learned temporal model that takes the sequence of head pose, eye features and zone probabilities and outputs the current zone with a confidence; others use a hidden Markov model or simple hysteresis. Either way, the output to the distraction logic is a time series of zones with quality flags, which is what Section 5 builds on.

5Distraction and drowsiness detection

Per-frame gaze and eyelid measurements become useful only when they are turned into states: this driver has been looking at the phone for four seconds; this driver has been getting sleepier for twenty minutes. The two problems look similar but are opposite in character. Distraction is fast, well defined and testable; drowsiness is slow, individual and hard to label.

5.1Three kinds of distraction

Human-factors research separates visual distraction (eyes off the road), manual distraction (hands off the controls, for example typing) and cognitive distraction (mind off the driving, for example an intense phone conversation). Visual distraction is what a camera measures directly and what most rules are written around. Manual distraction is visible to an occupant camera that sees the hands and to steering-wheel touch sensors. Cognitive distraction is the hard one: the eyes may point at the road while the driver does not process what they see. Research has found indirect signs, such as gaze concentrating towards the road centre and fewer glances to mirrors under cognitive load, but no rule currently requires cognitive distraction detection, and products treat it as research.

The reference values for visual distraction come from long-standing guidance. NHTSA's 2013 visual-manual distraction guidelines for in-vehicle devices ask that tasks be possible with glances away from the road of no more than 2 seconds each and no more than 12 seconds in total. The naturalistic studies of Section 1.1 explain the 2 s figure: beyond it, crash risk rises steeply. A useful mental model, used by the AttenD algorithm of Kircher and Ahlström (2009), is an attention buffer of 2 seconds that drains while the eyes are away and refills while they are on the road; the driver is considered distracted when it is empty. The rules in the next subsection are variants of this idea with different parameters.

5.2Glance rules: the same eyes, different timers

Each requirement source defines distraction with its own timer. The EU advanced driver distraction warning (ADDW) counts continuous gaze into area 3, below the 30° plane, and must warn after 3.5 seconds at 50 km/h and above, and after 6 seconds between 20 and 50 km/h; a brief look back at the road does not reset the timer if it is shorter than a tolerance the manufacturer chooses (at least 50 ms). Euro NCAP's 2026 driver engagement protocol tests two transient states: a long distraction, a single glance away of 3–4 seconds, and a short distraction, a pattern of shorter glances that adds up to 10 seconds off the road within a 30-second window (called VATS, visual attention time sharing). UN R171 for Level 2 driver control assistance requires a warning when the driver has been visually disengaged for 5 seconds. The interactive Figure 5.1 runs all of these timers on the same glance pattern.

The cases worth trying show what each rule is for. A single 4-second glance at 80 km/h triggers Euro NCAP's long-distraction response after 3 seconds and ADDW after 3.5 seconds; at 30 km/h only Euro NCAP reacts, because ADDW allows 6 seconds at low speed. Eight glances of 2 seconds with a full second back on the road trigger neither long-glance rule; only the cumulative VATS rule catches them, after 20 seconds. Shorten the looks back to 0.25 s, less than the 0.3 s tolerance, and ADDW treats the pattern as one long glance and warns after 3.5 seconds. Six glances of 1.5 seconds with 1.5 seconds back on the road trigger nothing at all, which is roughly how a driver who reads a message in short looks behaves. And a single 5.5-second glance at 30 km/h triggers Euro NCAP and R171 but not ADDW. One measurement pipeline, several state machines: that is how products implement it.

Pitfall

Mirror checks, shoulder checks and looks at the navigation display during a manoeuvre are glances away from the road ahead but not distraction. ADDW sidesteps this by counting only area 3 (below the 30° plane), and mirrors and windows belong to the driving-relevant areas. A system that counts every non-forward glance will warn during every lane change, and drivers will hate it. The opposite error is to treat the instrument cluster as driving-relevant without a limit: R171's authors chose not to treat the dashboard as driving-relevant, so long looks there count.

5.3Phone use, impairment and the unresponsive driver

Phone use is the distraction that matters most in practice, and Euro NCAP scores it separately, with basic and advanced test categories. A phone held at the ear, typed on in the lap or propped on the steering wheel produces different gaze patterns, so detection combines gaze (looking into the lap repeatedly) with an occupant-camera detector for hands and phones. The phone on the steering wheel is the hard case, because the gaze stays near the road zone.

Impairment by alcohol or drugs is newer territory. Euro NCAP 2026 awards points for detecting non-fatigue impairment from about 50 km/h, with a learning period of up to 10 minutes at the start of a trip. The signs a camera can see are subtle: slower saccades, gaze instability, delayed reactions, changed blink patterns, combined with driving signals such as lane-keeping quality. In the United States, the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act directed NHTSA to set a safety standard for advanced impaired-driving prevention technology; NHTSA published an advance notice of proposed rulemaking in January 2024 and lists driver monitoring cameras among the candidate technologies.

At the far end is the driver who does not respond at all: a medical emergency, deep sleep, or a driver who has passed out. Euro NCAP defines unresponsiveness as gaze not returning to the road within 3 seconds of a distraction warning, or eyes closed for 6 seconds or more, and expects the car to start an emergency function at most 5 seconds after its distinct warning phase begins: bring the car to a stop in or beside the lane, switch on the hazard lights and, typically, place an emergency call. This is the point where the DMS stops being a warning system and becomes the trigger for a minimal-risk manoeuvre.

5.4Drowsiness: what it is and how it is scored

Drowsiness, or sleepiness, is the physiological drive to sleep. It rises with time awake, with the circadian low between about 2 and 6 in the morning and the smaller dip in the early afternoon, with sleep debt, and with monotony; long motorway drives at night are the classic case. The person feels it, but people are poor at judging how close they are to falling asleep, which is why a warning helps.

The reference scale is the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS, Åkerstedt and Gillberg, 1990), on which people rate how they feel on nine levels: 1 extremely alert, 3 alert, 5 neither alert nor sleepy, 7 sleepy but no effort to stay awake, 8 sleepy with some effort to stay awake, 9 very sleepy, fighting sleep. In driving studies KSS 7 is where performance starts to degrade measurably and KSS 8–9 is where lane departures and microsleeps become frequent. Both the EU regulation and Euro NCAP use KSS as the ground truth: the EU drowsiness warning must warn at KSS 8 or above, and Euro NCAP expects detection at KSS above 7 at the latest.

5.5Temporal models, and why the label is the hard part

Figure 5.2 shows the usual architecture. Per-frame features are aggregated over windows (PERCLOS, blink statistics, nod counts), fused with vehicle signals and context (time on task, time of day), classified into a drowsiness level, and passed through a state machine with hysteresis and warning levels. The classifier is often gradient boosting on hand-designed window features; more recent systems use temporal networks (temporal convolutions, recurrent units or transformers) on the per-frame sequence. A learning phase at the start of each trip establishes the driver's baseline, which matters because blink behaviour differs a lot between people.

The model is not the hard part. The label is. KSS is a self-rating, asked every five minutes or so, subjective, and influenced by the asking itself, and drowsiness develops over tens of minutes, so there are few independent events per hour of data. Alternatives such as EEG-based sleep scoring or trained observers rating video (observer rating of drowsiness) have their own biases. Real drowsiness is also hard to produce: studies use sleep deprivation, night drives, simulators and test tracks, each with a different relationship to real-world drowsiness. The EU regulation reflects this honestly: its acceptance criterion for drowsiness warnings is an average sensitivity above 40 % across at least ten participants who were not involved in development, or a lower bound of the 90 % confidence interval above 20 %, where a warning counts as correct if a KSS rating just before or after it was at least 7.

Pitfall

The literature is full of drowsiness detectors with 95–99 % accuracy. Almost all are frame-level classifiers trained and tested on acted drowsiness (people pretending to yawn or close their eyes) or on random splits of the same few people's video, so the test set contains the same faces as the training set (Training 9.1 explains this kind of leakage). On independent drivers and real sleepiness the numbers collapse: the eyelid-angle study of Section 3.5 reports 52.5 % accuracy for a three-class alert/low-vigilance/drowsy video classification on the UTA-RLDD dataset (a comparison method reached 65 %) and 80 % for alert versus drowsy, where chance is 33 % and 50 %. When you read a drowsiness number, ask how drowsiness was induced, how it was labelled, and whether the test drivers were unseen.

New datasets address the label problem by recording more signals at once. UL-DD (Bodaghi and colleagues, July 2025) recorded 19 people in a truck simulator for about 40 minutes alert and 40 minutes drowsy each, with depth, infrared and RGB cameras, wristband physiology, steering-grip sensors and KSS every 4 minutes, 1,400 minutes in total. Such multimodal data let researchers check camera features against physiology instead of only against self-ratings.

5.6Putting the states together

The running example ends up with a set of parallel state machines fed by one pipeline: long distraction, short distraction (VATS), phone use, drowsiness level, microsleep (eye closure of 1–2 s), sleep (closure of 3 s or more), unresponsive, and an availability state for automated driving. They share inputs but not thresholds, and they have priorities: microsleep and sleep warnings must be more urgent than distraction warnings, and an unresponsive driver triggers the emergency function regardless of other states. The state machines also feed the assistance systems: a distracted or drowsy driver gets earlier forward-collision warnings and more sensitive lane support, which Euro NCAP checks by requiring the warning timing to change by at least 200 ms for an inattentive driver.

6Occupant monitoring

Occupant monitoring asks who is in the car, where, in which posture and whether they are protected. Its outputs go to the restraint system, the belt reminder, the emergency call and the child-presence alarm, so its errors have physical consequences: an airbag that fires into a rear-facing child seat, or a child left in a hot car.

6.1What occupant monitoring is for

The restraint system was designed for an adult sitting upright, belted, in the middle of the seat travel. Reality is more varied: a rear-facing infant seat on the passenger seat, a small adult sitting close to the dashboard, a passenger with feet on the dashboard, a belt worn under the arm, a rear passenger unbelted. Seat mats and buckle switches gave the first answers (is the seat occupied, is the belt buckled), and they remain as the safety-rated basis of airbag suppression in many cars. A camera adds what they cannot know: the type of occupant, posture, belt routing, and the presence of people in seats without sensors. Euro NCAP's 2026 Occupant Monitoring protocol has 30 points for this: 10 for seat-belt use (correct routing and rear-seat occupancy detection), 10 for occupant classification (automatic passenger airbag status, out-of-position, stature classification) and 10 for occupant presence (child presence detection and crash occupancy information for the emergency call).

6.2Body pose, seat occupancy and classification

A wide occupant camera runs a body keypoint model (shoulders, elbows, hips, head) and a per-seat classifier. The classes are practical ones: empty, object, rear-facing child seat, forward-facing child seat, booster with child, child, small adult, adult. The boundary that matters most for the airbag is between a rear-facing child seat (airbag must be off) and a small adult, where Euro NCAP expects the airbag on for the 5th percentile female and larger. Stature estimation, from shoulder width and sitting height, lets the restraint system adapt airbag and belt-load-limiter settings to the occupant; Euro NCAP asks for at least two adaptive settings per occupant.

A 2D image confuses size and distance: a small person close to the camera looks like a large person far away. Depth resolves this, which is why occupant classification benefits from time-of-flight cameras, from stereo, or from fusion with the seat-mat weight and the seat-track position. Seats are also moving furniture: they slide, recline, fold and in some vans swivel, and the classifier must know the seat configuration to interpret what it sees.

6.3Seat-belt routing and out-of-position

A buckle switch knows the belt is latched, not that it is worn. Common misuse is buckling the belt behind the back, wearing the shoulder belt under the arm, latching the belt with the occupant sitting on top of it, or plugging in a separate buckle insert to silence the reminder. A camera sees the webbing: a belt is a band of known width and texture that should run diagonally from the shoulder anchor across the chest to the buckle, and a segmentation network finds it well in NIR images, where the webbing often contrasts with clothing. Euro NCAP rewards detecting buckle-only, behind-the-back and lap-only use at the driver seat, with a warning within 30 seconds.

Out-of-position (OOP) detection looks for postures in which an airbag would do more harm than good. Euro NCAP currently tests two for the outboard front passenger: the head within 20 cm of the dashboard, and feet on the dashboard, each detected at several lateral positions and answered with a visual and audible warning within 30 seconds. More ambitious systems feed posture continuously into the restraint control unit to adapt the airbag deployment, but that path requires safety-rated perception, which is why most products today warn rather than adapt.

6.4Child presence detection with radar

Euro NCAP's protocol defines the task precisely: detect a child up to six years old (up to the 95th percentile, 28 kg or 125 cm) in any likely position, including footwells and removable seats, by sensing signs of life directly (breathing, heartbeat, movement); warn the driver within 15 seconds of locking; escalate within 90 seconds with signals visible and audible outside the car or a notification to the owner's phone; repeat for at least 20 minutes; and, for extra points, intervene by running the climate control, unlocking or calling for help. The system must be on by default and cannot be switched off for good with a single button. The "direct sensing" requirement excludes the earlier indirect method of reminding the driver whenever a rear door was opened at the start of a trip.

Radar suits this well because breathing is motion, and the phase of a radar echo is extremely sensitive to motion. A reflector that moves by Δd changes the round-trip path by 2Δd, so the phase changes by 4πΔd/λ. At 60.5 GHz the wavelength λ is 4.96 mm, so a chest movement of 1 mm changes the phase by 2.5 radians and even 0.2 mm by 0.5 radians. The experiment in Figure 6.1 simulates the phase of the range bin of a rear seat for 30 seconds: an empty seat, where only the slow rocking of the parked car and noise appear, and a sleeping infant breathing 36 times a minute with 0.5 mm amplitude and a heartbeat of 0.05 mm. The breathing peak at 0.60 Hz stands 250 times above the median of the spectrum; the empty seat has nothing in the breathing band beyond noise.

Real cabins are harder than the simulation. A car parked on a windy day rocks; a passer-by outside, rain on the roof or a fan in the cabin moves things; and a child may be awake and moving, which is easy to detect, or deeply asleep with very shallow breathing, which is the case that matters. The radar must separate seats and footwells by range and angle, so that a person standing next to the car is not counted, and it must classify motion patterns, so that a bag rocking on a seat is not a child. Pets are the classic ambiguity; many OEMs decide that an animal in a hot locked car deserves an alarm too. Because the alarm escalates to horns and phone notifications, false alarms are expensive in a different way: a system that wakes the neighbourhood every time groceries are left on the back seat will be disabled.

Why it matters

Child presence detection is a rare case in automotive perception where the sensor must work while the car is parked and locked, which means the radar and its processing must run from the 12 V battery with the rest of the car asleep. Power budget and wake-up strategy (scan for the first minutes after locking, then periodically) are as much part of the design as the detection algorithm.

7.2EU drowsiness warning (DDAW)

The General Safety Regulation (EU) 2019/2144 made a driver drowsiness and attention warning mandatory, and Delegated Regulation 2021/1341 defines it. The system must warn at a drowsiness level equivalent to KSS 8 or above (it may also warn at 7), be active automatically above 70 km/h, work normally from 65 to 130 km/h, start monitoring within 5 minutes of the activation conditions being met, work by day and by night, and give a visual plus an acoustic or other warning that may escalate. The driver may switch off the warnings but not the system, and the warnings come back with every new start.

The validation is the most interesting part, because it shows how regulators deal with a measurement that has no hard ground truth. The manufacturer tests with human participants, at least ten who were not involved in developing the system, by day and by night, with KSS self-ratings about every five minutes. A warning is a true positive if the participant's KSS rating just before or just after it was 7 or more. The acceptance criterion is a mean sensitivity across participants above 40 %, or a lower bound of the 90 % confidence interval above 20 %, with adjustments for longer rating intervals and for open-road testing. Other references such as EEG, PERCLOS or video ratings by at least three trained raters are allowed if their equivalence to KSS 8 is shown. A 40 % bar looks low until one remembers Section 5.5: it is a realistic bar for a hard problem, set so that steering-based systems from 2010 could pass while still requiring evidence.

Both EU texts contain the same privacy clauses: the system must not use biometric data, including facial recognition, to identify people in normal operation, it may record and keep only the data needed to work, processed in a closed loop, and any personal data processing must follow EU data protection law. Section 8.4 explains what that means for the design.

7.3EU distraction warning (ADDW)

Delegated Regulation 2023/2590 is short and precise. The system must activate automatically above 20 km/h (a lower speed is allowed) and may use up to one minute of driving above 20 km/h to calibrate. It determines whether the driver's visual attention is directed to the driving task using geometric areas defined from an ocular reference point, 635 mm above the seat reference point: area 2 covers the windscreen and the windows with a 10° margin, and area 3, the distraction area, is everything below a plane 30° downward from the ocular point, unless the manufacturer adds more. At 50 km/h and above the system must warn when the gaze stays in area 3 for 3.5 seconds; from 20 km/h, after 6 seconds. In non-nominal situations, defined as conditions that affect the system but are within its declared limits, each threshold may grow by 1.5 seconds. The timer must not reset because of image-processing artefacts or brief glances out of area 3 shorter than a tolerance of at least 50 ms (saccades).

The warning is visual plus acoustic and/or haptic, "as soon as possible" after the trigger, and may escalate; acoustic warnings must lie mostly between 200 and 8000 Hz at 50–90 dB. A covered or blinded sensor must produce a failure warning.

The type-approval test is a spot check on a test track. The driver fixates points in up to 14 zones (knees, lap, passenger footwell and seat, glove box, air vents, cluster, steering-wheel buttons, gear shifter, climate controls, infotainment display, centre console), each at 20–35 km/h and at 50–65 km/h, after at least 15 seconds of attentive driving. A warning must come within 4 seconds at the higher speed and 6.5 seconds at the lower (the threshold plus a 0.5 s buffer); a missed point may be retested twice with a different distracting action, and the vehicle fails if a point is missed in both retests. The test driver wears no glasses, hats or masks and has no facial hair other than eyebrows, unless the manufacturer chooses to test with them. In addition, the manufacturer documents its own validation with human participants: their number, demographics, inclusion criteria and repeatability.

Pitfall

Passing ADDW type approval is not the same as working for customers. The approval test uses one cooperative driver without eyewear, fixating chosen points. A system tuned only for that test can fail with glasses, with a driver who looks down by moving the eyes rather than the head, or in low sun. Specifications therefore add performance targets per eyewear class, lighting and population stratum on top of the legal minimum.

7.4Euro NCAP 2026: driver engagement

Euro NCAP's 2026 rating has four stages along a crash timeline (Standards and regulations 8.1); driver monitoring sits in the Safe Driving stage under the Driver Engagement protocol, which has 30 points. Driver monitoring gets 25: transient states 15 (long distraction 5, short distraction or VATS 5, phone use 5) and non-transient states 10 (impairment 4, microsleep 2, sleep 2, unresponsive driver 2). The remaining 5 points assess the ergonomics of the vehicle controls, for example whether frequent functions need physical controls rather than touch-screen menus.

The protocol defines more than thresholds; it defines behaviour. The system must be on at the start of every journey and cannot be switched off with a single momentary button press; its sensitivity cannot be adjusted by the driver. Monitoring starts after one minute of driving above 10 km/h; distraction and sleep responses apply from 20 km/h; impairment from 50 km/h, with up to 10 minutes of learning. The definitions are those of Figure 7.1: a long distraction is a single glance of 3–4 seconds after at least 4 seconds on the road; a short distraction is 10 seconds off the road within 30; drowsiness must be detected at KSS above 7 at the latest; microsleep is an eye closure of 1–2 seconds, sleep 3 seconds or more; unresponsive means no return of gaze within 3 seconds of a distraction warning or eyes closed for 6 seconds or more.

The points are only earned if the car also responds. Warnings must be immediate, visual plus audible or haptic; microsleep and sleep warnings must be more urgent than distraction warnings. Forward-collision support must become more sensitive within one second of a continuous off-road glance, with warning or braking timing differing by at least 200 ms between an attentive and an inattentive driver, and lane support must become more sensitive immediately. An unresponsive driver must trigger an emergency function no later than 5 seconds after the distinct warning phase starts. For cars with assisted driving, the Assisted Driving protocol additionally requires good scores in driver monitoring and driving collaboration.

7.5Euro NCAP 2026: occupant monitoring and child presence

The Occupant Monitoring protocol (v1.1, October 2025) has 30 points, summarised in Section 6.1. A few details shape designs. Belt reminders must start early in the trip (before 40 km/h, 90 s of driving or 1000 m, among other conditions), last at least 90 seconds, and cover the rear seats, scored in proportion to the rear seats with occupancy detection. Passenger airbag status earns the full 4 points only when it is automatic; a software switch the driver is advised to use earns 3, a hardware key switch 2. Out-of-position and stature points require camera-class sensing. Child presence detection earns up to 5 points, more for coverage of all passenger seats and for an intervention beyond warnings, and crash occupancy information, the number of occupants including children in child seats sent in the eCall minimum set of data, earns another 5.

The child presence rules (Section 6.4) are worth reading as a specification example. They define the child population (up to six years), the detection principle (direct signs of life), the timing (initial warning within 15 seconds of locking, escalation within 90 seconds, at least 20 minutes of repetition), the cancellation logic (only by unlocking or opening a door nearby, not from a distance), and how hard it must be to switch off (a menu, at most 30 days, then re-acknowledgement). Almost every line becomes a testable requirement.

7.6Level 2 and Level 3: R171 and R157, and a glance at the US

UN R171 regulates driver control assistance systems, the Level 2 systems that steer and brake under constant driver supervision, and it made driver monitoring central to Level 2 (the regulation's development is summarised in Standards and regulations 7.4). The 2024 text requires the system to detect visual disengagement, gaze or head direction away from driving-relevant areas, and to warn after 5 seconds, escalating after a further 3 seconds; it also allows hands-off operation, with the first hands-off warning after 5 seconds extendable to 10 seconds when the system confirms the eyes are on the road. Road-safety organisations argued for shorter limits, closer to ADDW's 3.5 seconds; the later amendment series extend system-initiated manoeuvres and hands-off use, and with them the dependence on monitoring.

UN R157 for automated lane keeping (Level 3) lets the driver look away while the system drives, but requires continuous checks of presence and availability. The system must issue a takeover request if the driver leaves the seat for more than one second or unbuckles. The driver counts as available only if at least two availability criteria, examples given in the text are input to a driver-exclusive control, eye blinking, eye closure and conscious head or body movement, have each confirmed availability within the last 30 seconds. If the driver is deemed unavailable, the system warns immediately, and if the warning continues for 15 seconds it issues a transition demand, which escalates after at most 4 seconds and leads to a minimal-risk manoeuvre at the earliest 10 seconds after it began. A DMS that recognises blinks and head movements is the natural way to satisfy two criteria at once.

The United States has no federal requirement for driver monitoring cameras. Its closest instruments are the impaired-driving rulemaking mentioned in Section 5.3 and the US NCAP programme, which is adding driver-assistance evaluations; in practice, US vehicles get DMS because the same platforms are sold in Europe, and because Level 2 hands-free systems use it to justify hands-free operation.

8Data, privacy and validation

A DMS watches people's faces for a living, so the data it is trained on and the data it handles in the car are both sensitive. Its performance also varies with exactly the things that vary between people: eyes, faces, eyewear, behaviour. Validation is therefore about coverage of human diversity as much as about accuracy.

8.1What the data must cover

For an exterior camera, coverage means weather, light, road types and object classes (Training 2.1). For a cabin camera it means people. The variables that move DMS performance most are eye shape and eyelid anatomy (narrow palpebral fissures, epicanthic folds and age-related drooping lids all change what "open" looks like), eyewear of all kinds, headwear and masks, facial hair, make-up and eyelash extensions, age from young adults to the elderly, height (which moves the eyes relative to the camera), skin reflectance in NIR, and behaviour: how people hold their head, how they look at mirrors, whether they talk with their hands. Then come cabin conditions: seat and steering-wheel adjustments, low sun through each window, flickering shadows, night with and without interior lights, and vehicle vibration.

Public datasets cover parts of this. For gaze there are MPIIFaceGaze, Gaze360, ETH-XGaze and the in-vehicle IVGaze; for driver behaviour, the Driver Monitoring Dataset (DMD, Ortega and colleagues, 2020, several cameras with RGB, depth and IR, distraction and drowsiness sessions) and Drive&Act (2019, fine-grained activities in a car); for drowsiness, NTHU-DDD and UTA-RLDD (mostly acted or self-recorded) and the multimodal UL-DD (2025). None is large or diverse enough for a product, and licences often exclude commercial use. Products are trained on proprietary data collected with recruited participants in instrumented cars, in thousands of hours, deliberately stratified across the variables above.

8.2Ground truth for gaze and drowsiness

Gaze ground truth comes from geometry. In data collection, participants look at known targets: markers placed on the windscreen, mirrors and displays at measured positions, or lights that switch on in sequence, with the participant confirming fixation. The 3D eye position is measured by a calibrated multi-camera rig, and the gaze label is the ray from the eye to the target. For natural driving, where nobody fixates markers, a head-mounted eye tracker worn by the driver gives a reference, at the cost of its own error and of the tracker being visible in the DMS images. Synthetic data, rendered faces with exactly known gaze, fill rare conditions such as extreme poses or unusual eyewear, but rendered NIR skin and eyes must be validated against real images before they are trusted.

Drowsiness ground truth is the weak point discussed in Section 5.5. In practice, programmes combine several references: KSS self-ratings, observer ratings of the video by trained raters, physiological measures where possible, and driving performance in a simulator or on a closed track. The EU regulation accepts video ratings only with at least three raters and a concordance of 0.70 or more, which is a good rule for any internal dataset as well.

8.3Measuring performance fairly

The metrics follow the states. For distraction: the share of scripted long glances that trigger a warning within the threshold plus a buffer, per zone and per speed, and false warnings per hour of normal driving. For drowsiness: event sensitivity against the KSS reference, the time between reaching KSS 8 and the first warning, and false warnings per hour of alert driving. For gaze itself: angular error and zone accuracy per zone. For occupants: classification accuracy per class and seat, with the rear-facing child seat versus small adult confusion reported separately because of its consequence.

Every metric is reported per stratum, not only as an average. An average of 97 % can hide a stratum at 80 %, and the stratum is usually a group of people: drivers over 70, drivers with thick-framed glasses, drivers whose eyes the training data under-represented. A useful rule is to state the worst stratum next to the average in every report. Working in NIR helps compared with visible light, because skin reflectance differs less between skin tones in the near infrared, but it does not remove the effect of eye shape, which is where DMS bias usually shows.

In practice

The same metrics are tracked as regression tests across software releases on a frozen, stratified replay set of recorded NIR video, run on the target hardware or a bit-exact simulation of it. A change that improves the average and worsens one stratum is not an improvement, and the release report must show it.

8.4Privacy: processing in a closed loop

Images of faces are personal data under the EU General Data Protection Regulation, and biometric data processed to uniquely identify a person is a special category with stricter rules. The EU DDAW and ADDW texts translate this into design constraints: normal operation must not identify the driver biometrically, the system must keep only the data it needs, and the processing must happen in a closed loop. Architecturally this means the camera images are processed on the vehicle's own computer and discarded within the frame time; only derived signals (gaze zone, eye state, warning state) leave the DMS software, and nothing is stored or transmitted that could reconstruct the face. Event data recorders and the data storage system of automated driving store states and times, not images.

Features that do identify the driver, such as face-based login to load seat and mirror settings, are possible but are separate, opt-in functions with their own consent, and they must not be a precondition of the safety function. Development data are a different matter: recorded faces of test participants are collected with explicit consent, stored with access control, pseudonymised, and deleted when the purpose ends. Fleet systems that send event videos to a company (Section 10.2) need a legal basis of their own and, in several European countries, agreement with employee representatives.

Why it matters

Public acceptance of cameras pointed at drivers depends on trust that the images never leave the car. A design in which the DMS cannot export images at all, enforced in software architecture and in the cybersecurity concept (Functional safety 9.2 for TARA), is easier to defend to regulators, customers and the press than one that relies on a policy promise.

8.5Validation as a safety argument

When the DMS gates automation (R171 hands-off, R157 availability) or triggers an emergency stop, its failure is a safety question. The DMS then has two kinds of failure to argue about. Random hardware faults and systematic software faults are handled by functional safety: the camera, LED driver and processing path get the integrity their safety goals require, with diagnostics such as frozen-frame detection and illuminator monitoring. Performance limitations, the DMS that works as designed but misjudges a driver with unusual eyewear, are SOTIF territory (Functional safety 8.3): identify triggering conditions (low sun on the lens, reflective glasses, masks, extreme seating positions), test them, and show that the residual risk is acceptable. The key performance trade-off, missed warnings against false warnings, is the same one shown interactively for exterior perception in Functional safety 8.5.

Validation combines four test types, each catching what the others miss: scripted track tests that mirror the regulatory spot checks; simulator studies with sleep-deprived participants for drowsiness, under ethics approval and with a safety driver concept; open-road field tests with naive drivers that measure false-warning rates and acceptance over thousands of kilometres; and replay testing of recorded video for regression and for rare conditions. The order matters: a system that passes scripted tests and has never met a naive driver will surprise its makers with the false-warning rate.

9Embedded deployment and warning design

The DMS runs continuously, on every trip, in a car whose computer also does other things. Its compute budget is modest, its latency budget is set by what the assistance systems need, and its success with customers depends less on accuracy than on how it warns.

9.1Where it runs and what it costs

There are two architectures. In a smart camera, the image sensor, illuminator driver and a small vision processor sit in one module on the column or mirror, and only states leave over CAN or Ethernet; this keeps the images inside the module, which suits the privacy argument, and decouples the DMS from the rest of the electronics. In a centralised design, the camera streams raw or ISP-processed video over a serial link (MIPI CSI-2 directly, or GMSL or FPD-Link when the camera is far from the computer) to the cockpit or ADAS system-on-chip, whose NPU runs the networks; this saves a processor and lets the DMS share the occupant camera's video with video calls. Newer cockpit SoCs include the DMS in their reference designs, which pushes the market towards centralisation.

The networks are small by current standards. In the experiment, the whole MediaPipe chain (detector, 478-point mesh and blendshapes) took about 10 ms per frame on one CPU core; production DMS networks, quantised to 8-bit integers (Edge AI 5.6) and run on an NPU, typically need a few milliseconds per frame for the driver camera at 30–60 fps. The occupant camera adds body pose, seat classification, belt segmentation and phone or object detection at a lower rate, a few frames per second. The real constraints are elsewhere: deterministic latency when the SoC is busy with navigation and graphics, power and heat in a small camera module, and the requirement that the safety-relevant part keeps running when the infotainment software crashes, which is a reason to put it on a separate core, partition or chip.

9.2The latency budget

The legal distraction thresholds of 3.5–6 seconds suggest that latency is irrelevant, but the assistance interface tells a different story. Euro NCAP expects the forward-collision support to become more sensitive within one second of an off-road glance and to shift its timing by at least 200 ms for an inattentive driver. The DMS-to-ADAS path therefore has a budget of a few hundred milliseconds: exposure and readout (one frame, 17–33 ms), processing (a few to tens of milliseconds), the confirmation filter that turns frames into a glance (typically 100–300 ms, Section 4.5), and transport of the state to the ADAS computer (a CAN or Ethernet cycle of 10–50 ms). The filter is the largest term, and it is a deliberate trade: shorter filtering reacts faster and flickers more.

Time stamps matter as in any fusion system (Automotive sensors 7.3): the state must carry the time of the image it was computed from, so that the ADAS side can judge how old it is and fall back to "unknown" when the DMS stalls.

9.3Warning strategy: escalate, and do not cry wolf

A warning that fires too often is switched off, ignored or resented, and an ignored warning protects nobody. Warning design is therefore a trade-off between sensitivity and annoyance, and the rules leave room for it. The usual answer is escalation (Figure 9.1): a first, gentle visual cue; then a visual and acoustic warning; then a more urgent sound, a red symbol and haptics such as a seat vibration, a belt tug or a short brake jolt; and for a driver who still does not react, an intervention. At the same time, the assistance systems adjust in the background. The driver experiences a calm system that becomes insistent only when needed.

False warnings come from three sources, and each needs its own fix. Measurement errors (glasses reflections, a missed eye) are fixed in perception and in honest quality flags. Context errors (a long look at the navigation display during a complicated junction, a shoulder check, a glance down while stopped at a light) are reduced by context rules: no distraction warnings at standstill, mirror and window areas treated as driving-relevant, short grace periods during manoeuvres. Individual differences (someone whose normal blink is long, someone who reads the cluster in long glances) are reduced by per-driver baselines and by escalation that requires repeated evidence before the loud stages. The rules constrain this: Euro NCAP forbids user-adjustable sensitivity and easy deactivation, so the only way to keep drivers from hating the system is to make it right.

Interesting detail

Warning modality matters by state. A distracted driver is awake, and a chime that makes them look at the road is enough. A drowsy driver is helped little by a chime; what helps is a break, so drowsiness warnings typically show a coffee-cup symbol and suggest a rest stop, and some navigation systems offer the nearest one. A driver in a microsleep needs to be woken, which is why microsleep warnings are louder and more physical than distraction warnings.

9.4Failure behaviour

The DMS must degrade visibly. If the camera is covered (a sticker, a hand resting on the column, a phone mount), the illuminator fails, or the face cannot be found for a long time, the system reports itself unavailable: ADDW requires a failure warning, and Level 2 and Level 3 functions that depend on monitoring must reduce their capability or refuse to activate. A blinded DMS that silently reports "eyes on road" is the worst possible failure, because it removes the supervision on which hands-free driving relies. Detecting it requires plausibility checks: an image that has not changed for several seconds, an illuminator current outside its window, a face that is found but whose eyes are never measured, or a driver who never blinks.

10Trucks and fleets

Fatigue is a bigger and more regulated problem in commercial driving than in private cars, and the vehicles and the business add their own twists. This section is brief; the truck chapter covers the exterior sensing.

10.1Long-haul fatigue and the truck cab

Professional drivers drive long hours, often at night and on monotonous motorways, which is the textbook setting for drowsiness. In the EU, driving and rest times are regulated (Regulation (EC) No 561/2006: a break of 45 minutes after 4.5 hours of driving, normally at most 9 hours of driving per day) and recorded by the tachograph, but rules on hours do not detect a driver who slept badly. The GSR's DDAW and ADDW requirements apply to trucks and buses (categories M and N) just as to cars, and Euro NCAP's Truck Safe rating includes driver monitoring in its Safe Driving stage (Truck perception 9.4).

The truck cab changes the optics. The driver sits higher and further from the windscreen, the seat moves on air suspension by several centimetres, the steering wheel is large and nearly horizontal, and the field of view the driver uses is wider, with large mirrors or camera monitor systems (Truck perception 4.1) far to the sides. Gaze zones must be defined for that geometry, the camera often sits on the A-pillar or the dashboard top rather than the column, and validation must include the population of professional drivers, who are on average older than the young adults in many datasets.

10.2Fleet monitoring

Commercial fleets add a use case private cars do not have: the operator wants to know. Aftermarket video telematics systems combine a road camera and a driver camera, detect events such as phone use, smoking, distraction, drowsiness or harsh braking, and upload short clips to a fleet portal for coaching and insurance. That model reverses the closed-loop design of Section 8.4: images do leave the vehicle, by design. It is legal only with a clear purpose, information to the drivers, proportionality, and in several European countries the agreement of works councils or employee representatives; designs often blur the driver's face by default, upload only event clips, and let drivers see what was recorded. Mixing the two, a type-approved DMS whose signals also feed a fleet's coaching system, is common in modern trucks and requires keeping the safety function's closed loop separate from the fleet data path.

Talk track

"In a private car the DMS is a closed loop: images in, warnings out, nothing stored. In a fleet the customer often wants event video for coaching, which is a different legal basis and needs works-council agreement in many countries. We keep the type-approved function separate and treat the fleet feature as its own product with its own consent and retention rules."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

What is a driver monitoring system and why has it become mandatory?Basic

A DMS is an inward-facing perception system, usually a near-infrared camera with illumination, that estimates head pose, eye state and gaze, and turns them into driver states: distraction, drowsiness, microsleep, unresponsiveness. It became mandatory in the EU through the General Safety Regulation: the drowsiness warning (DDAW) since 2022/2024 and the camera-based advanced driver distraction warning (ADDW) for all new vehicles from 7 July 2026. Euro NCAP scores it and Level 2 and 3 regulations depend on it, because partial automation makes drivers less attentive and the car must check that a human is still supervising or available.

Why do DMS cameras use 940 nm infrared?Basic

Infrared illumination makes the image independent of ambient light and is invisible, so it does not dazzle the driver at night. Of the two common wavelengths, 940 nm is invisible (850 nm LEDs glow faintly red) and falls in a water-vapour absorption band of sunlight: in the ASTM G173 reference spectrum the sun brings 20 W/m² in 940 ± 25 nm against 46 W/m² around 850 nm. Since the LED must compete with sunlight on the face, the weaker sun outweighs silicon's lower sensitivity at 940 nm.

Why a global-shutter sensor, and how is the illumination timed?Intermediate

A global shutter exposes all pixels in the same short window, so the LEDs can flash only during that millisecond. Short exposure collects little sunlight; low duty cycle (about 3 %) lets the LEDs run at a high peak current with low average power, which keeps heat and eye exposure low. Concentrating the same average power into a 1 ms pulse instead of spreading it over 33 ms improves the LED-to-sun ratio by about 30 times. A rolling shutter would need the LED on for the whole readout.

Walk me through the per-frame DMS pipeline.Basic

Detect the face (every few frames) and track it; place landmarks; fit a 3D face model to get head pose; measure eyelid aperture and pupil on high-resolution eye crops; estimate gaze from head and eyes, usually as a zone; then temporal logic computes glance durations, PERCLOS and blink statistics and runs the state machines with hysteresis. Every stage reports quality flags so that "unknown" is not mistaken for "eyes on road".

What is PERCLOS, and what are its weaknesses?Intermediate

PERCLOS is the percentage of time in a window, typically one to three minutes, during which the eyes are at least 80 % closed (Wierwille 1994); the FHWA study by Dinges and colleagues (1998) found it the most reliable camera measure of lapses in a vigilance test. Weaknesses: it reacts late (it rises when long closures appear, by which time the driver is very drowsy), it depends on each person's baseline eye opening, and the window trades latency against noise. Products add blink duration, closing speed and long-closure events.

How does model-based gaze estimation work?Intermediate

An infrared LED creates a glint on the cornea; the camera finds the pupil centre and the glint. The glint-to-pupil vector (PCCR) changes with eye rotation but hardly with small head translations. With calibrated cameras and two or more LEDs, the 3D corneal centre and pupil centre give the optical axis. The visual axis differs by the angle kappa (about 4–6°, per person), estimated by calibration or, in cars, by implicit calibration that assumes the driver mostly looks at the road and mirrors.

Compare model-based and appearance-based gaze.Intermediate

Model-based gaze is accurate (0.5–1° after calibration) and interpretable but needs high eye resolution, a visible glint and calibration, and suffers from glasses reflections and extreme angles. Appearance-based gaze maps a normalised face or eye image to a direction with a network; it works at lower resolution, without glints and with glasses, but has 4–5° error within a dataset and 6–10° across datasets. Products mix them: networks for robustness, geometry for normalisation and per-driver auto-calibration. Recent work like UniGaze uses large self-supervised pre-training to improve cross-domain generalisation.

What does the EU ADDW regulation require exactly?Intermediate

Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2590: active above 20 km/h, up to one minute calibration; area 3 is everything below a plane 30° down from the ocular reference point; warning after 3.5 s of gaze in area 3 at 50 km/h and above, after 6 s from 20 km/h, plus up to 1.5 s in non-nominal situations; the timer is not reset by saccade-length glances back (tolerance at least 50 ms); visual plus acoustic and/or haptic warning; failure warning when blinded; no biometric identification, closed-loop processing. Type approval is a spot check of up to 14 zones at two speed ranges with a 0.5 s buffer.

How does Euro NCAP 2026 score driver monitoring?Intermediate

The Driver Engagement protocol has 30 points, 25 for driver monitoring: long distraction, short distraction (VATS: 10 s off-road in 30 s) and phone use 5 each; impairment 4; microsleep, sleep and unresponsive driver 2 each. The system must be on by default every trip, without user-adjustable sensitivity. Points require vehicle responses: immediate warnings, FCW/AEB more sensitive within 1 s with at least 200 ms timing difference, more sensitive lane support, and an emergency function within 5 s of the distinct warning for an unresponsive driver.

How does child presence detection work and what does Euro NCAP require?Intermediate

Most systems use a 60 GHz radar in the headliner that detects breathing as phase changes of the echo (1 mm of motion is 2.5 rad at λ = 4.96 mm), through blankets and seats. Euro NCAP requires direct sensing of children up to six years in all likely positions, on by default, a first warning within 15 s of locking, escalation within 90 s with external signals or a phone notification, repetition for at least 20 minutes, and gives extra points for interventions such as climate control. Indirect reminders based on rear-door opening no longer count.

What does R157 mean by driver availability?Advanced

At Level 3 the driver may look away but must be able to take over. R157 requires presence (in the seat, belted; out of the seat for more than 1 s triggers a takeover request) and availability: at least two availability criteria, such as blinking, eye closure, conscious head or body movement or input to a driver-only control, must each have confirmed availability within the last 30 seconds. Otherwise the system warns, and after 15 s of warning issues a transition demand, which escalates within 4 s and can lead to a minimal-risk manoeuvre from 10 s after it started.

How would you validate a drowsiness detector?Advanced

Define the reference first: KSS self-ratings every few minutes, ideally with observer ratings by at least three trained raters and physiological measures. Induce real drowsiness (sleep restriction, night drives, monotony) in a simulator or on a closed track under ethics approval, with participants not involved in development. Measure event sensitivity against KSS ≥ 8 (or ≥ 7 for borderline), warning latency relative to reaching KSS 8, and false warnings per hour of alert driving, per stratum. Check the EU DDAW criterion (mean sensitivity > 40 % over ≥ 10 participants) as a floor, not a target, and confirm false-warning rates on open-road drives with naive drivers.

How do you handle glasses and sunglasses?Intermediate

Clear glasses cause reflections of the illuminator that can cover the eye: alternate two illuminators so the reflection moves, measure each eye in the frame where it is visible, and place LEDs so lens reflections mostly miss the camera. Many sunglasses transmit 940 nm and are nearly transparent to the DMS; some block NIR, in which case the system must detect that the eyes are not visible, fall back to head pose with wider uncertainty, and report the reduced capability. Evaluation sets are stratified by eyewear type.

What privacy constraints apply to a DMS?Basic

Face images are personal data under GDPR; biometric identification is a special category. DDAW and ADDW require no biometric identification in normal operation, keeping only necessary data and closed-loop processing. In practice images are processed on the vehicle computer and discarded; only states leave the DMS, and nothing that could reconstruct the face is stored or sent. Face-based login is a separate opt-in function; fleet video telematics need their own legal basis and often employee representation agreements.

Smart camera or centralised DMS: what would you choose?Advanced

A smart camera keeps images in the module (strong privacy story), decouples the DMS from the cockpit software and its crashes, and is easy to carry across platforms, at the cost of a separate processor and limited headroom. Centralising on the cockpit or ADAS SoC saves cost, shares the occupant camera with video calls and gives more compute, but requires deterministic scheduling, freedom from interference with infotainment and a careful privacy architecture. With hands-off Level 2 or Level 3 depending on the DMS, I would centralise only onto a partition or core with the integrity the safety goals require and with guaranteed latency.

What is the latency budget for DMS signals to ADAS?Advanced

Legal distraction thresholds are seconds, but Euro NCAP expects forward-collision support to react within 1 s of an off-road glance and to shift timing by ≥ 200 ms. So the DMS-to-ADAS path needs a few hundred milliseconds: one frame for exposure and readout, a few to tens of ms for inference, 100–300 ms for glance confirmation filtering, and a bus cycle of 10–50 ms. States must carry the image time stamp so the consumer can detect stale data.

How do you detect seat-belt misuse?Intermediate

A buckle switch only knows that the latch is closed. A wide camera segments the webbing and checks its path: diagonal across the chest from shoulder anchor to buckle, plus the lap section. Misuse classes are buckled behind the back, under the arm, lap only, or a buckle insert without a belt. Euro NCAP rewards detecting buckle-only, behind-back and lap-only use at the driver seat with a warning within 30 s.

Why do steering-based drowsiness warnings fall out of favour?Intermediate

They need no camera and worked on monotonous motorway driving, which is why early DDAW systems used them and why the EU regulation does not require a camera. But they detect drowsiness late, depend on road and driving style, and fail when lane-keeping assistance steers the car. Since a camera is now needed for distraction anyway, eye-based drowsiness detection, which sees the cause rather than the effect, takes over, often fused with the vehicle signals.

What makes a good DMS evaluation dataset?Advanced

Coverage of people (eye shapes, ages, heights, facial hair, make-up), eyewear (clear, thick frames, sunglasses with high and low NIR transmission), headwear and masks, lighting (night, low sun per window, flicker), seat and wheel positions, and behaviours (head movers and eye movers, phone use styles). Ground truth from geometry for gaze and multiple references for drowsiness. Subject-disjoint splits; metrics per stratum with the worst stratum reported next to the average; a frozen replay set for regression across releases.

How do occupant classification and the airbag interact?Intermediate

The passenger airbag must be off for a rear-facing child seat and on for the 5th percentile female and larger; for forward-facing seats and smaller occupants the OEM defines a strategy. Euro NCAP gives the full 4 points only for automatic status. Seat mats are the classic safety-rated basis; cameras add the occupant type and posture, ideally with depth to separate size from distance. Fusion of camera, mat and seat position decides; the rear-facing seat versus small adult confusion is the critical error and is reported separately.

Q.2Why and what if

Why does tilting the head down look like closing the eyes to EAR?Why

EAR is a 2D ratio of vertical lid distances to eye width. Pitching the head foreshortens the vertical direction of the eye in the image while the width stays, so the ratio drops: in the experiment from 0.31 to 0.25 at 30° and 0.21 at 40°, the value of a 35 % closed frontal eye. Measuring aperture in 3D, training eye-state models across poses and normalising per driver avoid it.

Why do many DMS products classify gaze into zones instead of reporting a direction?Why

Because the rules count durations of glances into areas, not angles, and because calibration-free gaze has errors of several degrees. A zone classifier with temporal filtering turns noisy angles into robust glances. The simulation shows the limits: at σ = 4° a glance at the road centre is classified correctly 98 % of the time but one at a small mirror only 75 %, and points near zone boundaries remain ambiguous at any accuracy.

Why is the regulatory acceptance bar for drowsiness (40 % sensitivity) so low?Why

Because the ground truth is weak and the phenomenon slow: KSS is a subjective rating every few minutes, people differ greatly, and real drowsiness is hard to induce and rare per hour. Demanding 95 % would be unverifiable. The bar ensures evidence with independent participants by day and night; products aim higher, especially for microsleep and sleep, which are easier to define.

Why does child presence detection use radar rather than the occupant camera?Why

The child may be under a blanket, in a rear-facing seat with a sunshade, or in a footwell, all invisible to a mirror camera; radar passes through fabric and plastic. Breathing is motion, and radar phase turns sub-millimetre motion into large signals. Radar also works in darkness and with low power, which matters because CPD runs while the car is parked and locked.

What if the driver wears NIR-blocking sunglasses?What if

The eyes are invisible, so eyelid and eye-gaze measurements fail. The system must detect this (eyes never measured while the face is tracked), report reduced capability, and fall back to head pose for distraction with wider margins. For drowsiness, it falls back to head nods, yawns and vehicle signals. Level 2 hands-off modes that require eyes-on-road confirmation may have to be unavailable; ADDW's non-nominal allowance of +1.5 s exists for such conditions within declared limits.

What if the DMS loses the face for two seconds during a long glance?What if

The safe interpretation is that a face that disappeared during a glance away is still looking away, so the distraction timer should continue, not reset. ADDW even states that image-processing artefacts must not reset the timer. Resetting on loss would let exactly the most distracted drivers (turned far away) escape detection.

What if the illuminator driver fails and the LED stays on?What if

The time-averaged irradiance rises by the inverse duty cycle, about 30 times, possibly out of the exempt group. A hardware-limited maximum on-time or a watchdog in the LED driver must cut the LED regardless of software; the failure must be detected and reported, and the DMS declared unavailable if the illuminator is switched off.

What if a PERCLOS window is made very short to warn earlier?What if

It reacts faster but becomes noisy: a few seconds of squinting or a burst of blinks can push it over the threshold. In the simulation, a 20 s window warns half a minute earlier than a 60 s one but reaches 11 % in the alert phase because of a squinting episode, and with an 8 % threshold that becomes a false warning. Short windows need event-based features and context rules, not just a lower threshold.

What if the camera runs at 10 fps to save power?What if

A 150 ms blink becomes one or two frames, so blink durations and closing speeds, the earliest drowsiness signs, are measured coarsely (24 % mean duration error in the simulation versus 12 % at 30 fps), and at 5 fps some blinks are missed entirely. PERCLOS over a minute is barely affected, but it reacts late. Drowsiness research uses 60 fps or more for blink features.

Why can the same eight two-second glances pass ADDW but fail Euro NCAP?Why

ADDW times continuous gaze in area 3 and resets when the driver looks back at the road for longer than the tolerance, so 2 s glances never reach 3.5 s. Euro NCAP's short-distraction rule accumulates off-road time over a 30 s window and fires at 10 s, which the pattern reaches after five glances. The two rules target different behaviours: one long look, versus time-sharing attention with a device.

What if the passenger is a small adult and the classifier says child seat?What if

The airbag would be suppressed for an adult who needs it, which is a serious error in the other direction from deploying into a rear-facing seat. That is why classification uses depth and seat-mat weight, why the status is shown to occupants ("PASSENGER AIRBAG OFF"), and why the confusion between these classes is analysed separately with its own targets.

Why must the DMS report "unknown" honestly instead of a best guess?Why

Because consumers interpret its outputs as facts: a hands-off Level 2 function stays active if the DMS says "eyes on road". A best guess during a blinded camera removes the very supervision the automation relies on. With an explicit unknown, the consumer can apply its own fallback: continue timers, reduce automation, warn.

What if a dog is left in the locked car?What if

A radar sees a breathing animal like a breathing child; the size and motion pattern may differ, but distinguishing reliably is hard. Many manufacturers decide that an animal in a hot locked car also deserves an alarm, and design the notification accordingly. The design decision belongs in the specification, together with the false-alarm cases (bags, wind, people outside) that must not trigger.

Why are DMS warnings different for distraction and drowsiness?Why

A distracted driver is awake and only needs the eyes back on the road, so a short chime or symbol works. A drowsy driver is not helped much by a chime; the remedy is a break, so the warning proposes rest. Microsleep needs waking, so it gets the most urgent, often haptic warning. Euro NCAP requires microsleep and sleep warnings to be more urgent than distraction warnings.

What if a Level 3 driver sits motionless reading a book for a minute?What if

Reading is allowed at Level 3, but availability must still be confirmed. Blinks and eye closures are visible to a DMS even while reading, and small head and body movements occur naturally, so two criteria are usually met within 30 seconds. If the driver were truly motionless with no detectable blinks, the system would deem them unavailable, warn, and after 15 s issue a transition demand.

GGlossary

ADDW
Advanced driver distraction warning; EU requirement (Delegated Regulation 2023/2590) to warn when the gaze stays below a 30° plane for 3.5 s (≥ 50 km/h) or 6 s.
Area 3
In ADDW, everything below a plane 30° downward from the ocular reference point; looking there counts as distraction.
Availability (driver)
In UN R157, the driver's ability to take over, confirmed by at least two criteria within the last 30 s.
Blendshape
A coefficient of facial expression (for example eye blink, jaw open) output by face models such as MediaPipe.
Bright / dark pupil
The pupil appears bright when the illuminator is near the camera axis (retinal reflection) and dark when it is off-axis.
CPD
Child presence detection: detecting a child left in a locked car, by direct sensing of signs of life, and raising an alarm.
DCAS
Driver control assistance system; the Level 2 systems regulated by UN R171.
DDAW
Driver drowsiness and attention warning; EU requirement (Delegated Regulation 2021/1341) to warn at KSS 8 or above.
DMS
Driver monitoring system: estimates head pose, eye state and gaze to detect distraction, drowsiness and unresponsiveness.
EAR
Eye aspect ratio; vertical lid distances over twice the eye width from six landmarks; simple but pose-dependent.
Glint
The reflection of an infrared LED on the cornea, used with the pupil centre for model-based gaze.
Global shutter
A sensor that exposes all pixels during the same time window, allowing short synchronised illumination pulses.
IEC 62471
Photobiological safety standard for lamps and LEDs; classifies sources into an exempt group and risk groups 1–3.
Kappa angle
The angle between the optical axis of the eye and the visual axis through the fovea, about 4–6°, different per person.
KSS
Karolinska Sleepiness Scale, a nine-level self-rating from extremely alert (1) to fighting sleep (9).
Microsleep
A short sleep episode with eyes closed and no perception; Euro NCAP tests eye closures of 1–2 s.
NIR
Near infrared, roughly 750–1400 nm; DMS cameras use 850 or 940 nm.
OMS
Occupant monitoring system: occupancy, classification, posture, seat-belt use and child presence for all seats.
Out-of-position (OOP)
An occupant posture in which an airbag could cause harm, such as the head close to the dashboard or feet on it.
PCCR
Pupil centre corneal reflection: gaze estimation from the vector between glint and pupil centre.
PERCLOS
Percentage of time in a window with the eyes at least 80 % closed; the classic drowsiness measure.
PnP
Perspective-n-point: estimating an object's pose from known 3D points and their 2D image positions; used for head pose.
RGB-IR
A colour filter array with infrared pixels in place of some colour pixels, for one camera that gives colour by day and IR at night.
Time of flight (ToF)
A depth camera that measures per-pixel distance from the phase or delay of modulated infrared light.
Transition demand
In R157, the system's request that the driver take over control.
VATS
Visual attention time sharing; Euro NCAP's short-distraction pattern, 10 s off the road within 30 s.
VCSEL
Vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser; a narrow-band infrared source that allows narrow lens filters; assessed under laser safety rules.
