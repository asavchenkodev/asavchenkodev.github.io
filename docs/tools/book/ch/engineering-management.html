Leadership and career

Engineering Management: Hiring, Teams and Performance

What changes when you manage engineers, and again when you manage several teams through their managers. The chapter follows one manager through about eighteen months at Arden, a fictional company of 600 people that sells a camera-based safety system for truck and van fleets and has started to license its perception stack to a truck maker. You join as the engineering manager of a perception team of six and end with three teams, 23 people and a second site in Munich. Along the way: the first 90 days, hiring, building and splitting teams, one-to-ones, performance reviews and underperformance, managing managers, headcount, delivery across teams, shipping machine-learning models, talking to customers, team health and reorganisations.

1The manager's job

An engineering manager is accountable for what a group of engineers delivers and for the health of that group over time. Almost everything that feels strange in the first year of management follows from one shift: you stop being measured by your own work and start being measured by the work of other people, which you influence only indirectly.

1.1Your output is the output of your team

Andy Grove put the definition most precisely in High Output Management (1983): a manager's output is the output of the organisation under them and of the neighbouring organisations they influence. The definition sounds obvious, but it changes what counts as a good day. As an engineer, a good day ended with a merged change or a solved bug. As a manager, a good day may end with nothing you made yourself: you spent an hour in a one-to-one that kept a senior engineer from leaving, half an hour rewriting an unclear priority so that two people stopped working against each other, and an hour on an interview that will decide who joins the team for the next five years. Each of those activities changes the output of six people for months; a merged change of your own changes it for a day.

Grove's second idea is managerial leverage: every activity has an effect on output, and the manager's job is to choose activities with high leverage. A decision that affects many people (a team's priorities), an activity whose effect lasts long (a hire, a clear expectation), and a small intervention at the right moment (a word with someone before a meeting rather than a fight in it) all have high leverage. Low-leverage work is often the most comfortable: writing code on the critical path yourself, answering every question personally, attending every meeting "to stay informed".

This also explains why the job feels unmeasurable at first. Feedback on engineering work arrives in minutes (the build passes or fails). Feedback on management arrives in quarters: whether the hire was good, whether the team kept its people, whether the plan held. New managers often compensate by holding on to engineering work because it still gives them the feeling of progress. A useful test is to ask, at the end of each week, what the team can now do that it could not do last week, and how much of that came from things only you could have done.

Why it matters

A manager who stays the strongest individual contributor on the team caps the team at what one person can review and decide. The team waits for them, the other engineers stop growing into the hard problems, and the manager works long hours while the team's output stays flat. The move from "doing" to "multiplying" is the core of the job, not a side effect of it.

1.2Tech lead, engineering manager, manager of managers

The senior-engineering chapter describes the career tracks from the individual contributor's side (Senior engineering 2.4): a staff engineer owns technical outcomes across a scope, an engineering manager owns people, hiring, growth, performance and team delivery, and many people move between the two tracks more than once. From the manager's side the roles look like this.

A tech lead is an engineer who owns a team's technical direction: the design, the quality bar, the sequencing of technical work. They have no formal authority over people; their tools are designs, reviews and their own code. A tech lead manager (TLM) is a hybrid that some companies use for small or new teams: one person is both the technical lead and the people manager of three to five engineers. It works while the team is small and the technical problem is well understood by the lead, and it breaks as the team grows because both halves of the job expand at once. An engineering manager (EM) manages one team, typically five to eight engineers, and works with a tech lead or staff engineer who owns the technical direction. A manager of managers (often titled senior manager, head of, or director) manages two to five managers and through them 20 to 60 people.

The usual span of control for a first-line manager of engineers is five to eight direct reports. Fewer than four rarely justifies a full-time manager and tends to produce micromanagement; more than eight to ten leaves too little time per person for one-to-ones, feedback and growth, and the manager turns into a dispatcher. These numbers are conventions rather than laws (very senior, autonomous teams can run with twelve; a team of new graduates needs a smaller span), but they are a good default for planning.

Pitfall

Promoting the best engineer to manager because "it is the next step". Management is a different job, not a higher level of the same job; at a company with a proper dual ladder (Senior engineering 2.1) a staff engineer and an engineering manager can be at the same level and paid the same. Promote someone into management because they want the work (hiring, conversations, organising) and show early signs of it, and make it easy to step back without loss of face.

1.3The running example: Arden's perception group

Arden (fictional) has about 600 employees and its headquarters in Amsterdam. It sells a fleet safety product: a road-facing camera and a driver-facing camera in each truck or van, an on-board unit that runs perception models (vehicles, pedestrians, lanes, following distance, driver distraction), and a cloud service where fleet managers see events and coach drivers. A year ago a European truck maker asked Arden to supply its road-facing perception stack for a new driver-assistance feature in its own trucks. That contract is the reason the perception group will grow.

You join as the engineering manager of the perception team: six engineers (two senior, three mid-level, one junior), a tech lead among the seniors, and a backlog that mixes fleet product features, model improvements and the first requests from the truck maker. Over the chapter the team grows to three teams with 23 people: a models team, a data and ML platform team, and an integration team in Munich close to the customer. You report to the VP of Engineering; your peers are the managers of the cloud, the on-board software and the hardware teams.

The example is deliberately a product company rather than an automotive supplier: the management practice described here is mainstream practice at technology companies. Where an automotive context changes something (labour law in Germany, technical meetings with a carmaker) there is a short box marked "In automotive". The automotive project process itself, from the V-model to ASPICE and release gates, belongs to Project management.

1.4The first 90 days: listen, diagnose, then act

A new manager has two assets that wear off: the right to ask naive questions and the goodwill of people who are curious about them. The first 90 days are best spent turning those into an accurate picture of the team, because every later decision depends on it. The common failure is the opposite: a new manager arrives with a plan from their previous job, changes the process in week two, and spends the next six months recovering trust.

The first month is mostly listening. Hold a long one-to-one with every person on the team and ask the same handful of questions, so the answers can be compared: what is going well that we must not break; what is frustrating; what would you change if you were me; what do you want to be doing in a year; what should I know that nobody will tell me. Meet your peers and the product manager with similar questions, read the last quarter's plans, incident reports and design documents, and if possible sit in on a customer call. At Arden, two conversations in the first week already point at the same problem: releases of new models to the fleet take three weeks because evaluation is manual, and the senior engineers do most of it.

From about the third week the notes turn into a diagnosis: a short written "state of the team" that covers people (strengths, risks, who might leave), delivery (what ships, how predictably), technical health (debt, incidents, on-call load), and relationships (how the team is seen by its customers and peers). Write it down and test it: share parts with the tech lead, ask your manager whether it matches their view. A diagnosis that nobody has challenged is a guess.

Somewhere in the second month pick one small, visible improvement that people asked for and that you can deliver without a large change: at Arden, a script that automates the comparison of a new model with the current one on the frozen evaluation set, which cuts the release from three weeks to one. It shows that listening leads to action, and it buys credit for the larger changes that follow. By the end of the third month you agree a plan with your manager: priorities for the next two quarters, the hiring plan, and the one or two structural changes you intend to make.

In practice

Two things to avoid in the first weeks: rating people before you have seen them work (first impressions of quiet engineers are usually wrong), and making promises to individuals about promotions, projects or pay. Both are hard to take back. "I hear you, and I will come back to it once I understand the whole picture" is a complete answer in week two.

Interesting detail

Michael Watkins' The First 90 Days (2003) gives the framework most companies use for leadership transitions. One of its useful points is that the right strategy depends on the situation you inherit: a start-up, a turnaround, a realignment of a team that is drifting, or sustaining a team that works well. In a sustaining situation the biggest risk is breaking what works; in a turnaround the biggest risk is waiting too long.

2Hiring

Hiring is the decision with the longest reach that a manager makes on their own. A hire shapes the team for years, a mis-hire costs months of everyone's time, and both the quality of the decision and the fairness of the process depend mostly on how the process is designed, not on how good the interviewers are at judging people.

2.1Why hiring deserves a designed process

A typical engineering hire takes two to four months from opening the role to an accepted offer, and in countries with long notice periods another one to three months until the person starts. After that, a new engineer needs three to six months to become fully productive in an unfamiliar codebase and domain. Each hire therefore represents half a year of investment before any return, plus the time the team spends interviewing and onboarding. A wrong hire costs that investment and more: months of performance management, damage to the team's trust in the hiring process, and often a second search.

The other reason is that unstructured human judgement of candidates is poor. People form an impression in the first minutes of a conversation and then spend the rest of it confirming it; they rate people who resemble them more highly; they are swayed by confidence and fluent speech that say little about engineering ability. A designed process does not remove these effects, but it reduces them by deciding in advance what is measured, how, and by whom, and by keeping individual judgements independent until they are combined.

2.2Defining the role before writing the job ad

Most hiring problems start before the first candidate: the team "needs another senior ML engineer", the job ad is a list of fifteen technologies, and each interviewer looks for something different. The fix is a short written role definition, sometimes called a scorecard, agreed by the hiring manager and the interviewers before sourcing starts. It contains three things.

First, the mission and outcomes: what this person will have achieved after twelve months. At Arden, the first hire is "make the evaluation of perception models trustworthy and fast: a model can be compared with the current one on the frozen set and on the hard-case slices within a day, and nobody argues about the numbers". Outcomes like these tell you which experience matters, and they are what you will later use to set the hire's goals. Second, the competencies: the four to six abilities the role needs, each with a description of what good looks like at the target level (coding, ML fundamentals, perception data and evaluation, system design for production, ownership, collaboration). Third, the level: the band on the company's ladder, which fixes the expected scope and the pay range. Hiring "senior or staff, we'll see" is a common source of later trouble, because the same person is then interviewed against two different bars and offered a level that one half of the panel disagrees with.

The job ad is then written from the scorecard: what the person will do and achieve, what the team is like, the few requirements that are truly required, and the salary range where the law or the company allows it. Long lists of requirements discourage capable candidates who do not tick every box; a short list of real requirements widens the pool without lowering the bar.

2.3Sourcing and the funnel

Candidates come from four main channels: inbound applications, referrals from employees, outbound sourcing (a recruiter or the hiring manager contacting people directly), and agencies. Referrals usually convert best, because employees pre-filter and candidates arrive with an accurate picture of the job; outbound sourcing is the only way to reach people who are not looking, which for experienced perception engineers is most of them. Agencies charge a fee, often around a fifth of the first year's salary, and are worth it mainly for hard roles and new locations.

Thinking in a funnel makes two things visible. The first is cost: at Arden the team spends about 15 hours on technical screens, 30 hours in interview loops and 10 hours in debriefs for each hire, roughly 55 engineer hours, which at three hires a quarter is more than a full person-month of senior time each quarter. The second is where the process leaks. If many candidates pass the technical screen and then fail the loop, the screen is not measuring what the loop measures; if many candidates decline offers, the problem is the offer, the speed, or the candidate experience, not the pipeline. A manager who tracks the conversion of each stage can fix the right thing.

Numbers

Arden's funnel per hire: 120 applicants or sourced, 30 recruiter screens (25 %), 12 technical screens (40 %), 5 interview loops of four interviews (42 %), 2 offers (40 %), 1 acceptance (50 %). Engineer time: 12 screens × 1.25 h = 15 h, 5 loops × 4 interviews × 1.5 h including write-up = 30 h, 5 debriefs × 4 people × 0.5 h = 10 h. The conversion rates are illustrative; real ones vary widely by role, market and brand.

2.4Designing an interview loop

An interview loop is the set of interviews a candidate goes through before the decision, usually four to six of 45 to 60 minutes each. Designing it means deciding which competency each interview measures, with which kind of exercise, and against which rubric. Two rules make the design work. Each competency on the scorecard is measured as the primary focus of at least one interview, and preferably seen from a second angle in another; and no interview tries to measure more than two or three things, because an interviewer who scores six competencies in 45 minutes scores their overall impression six times.

The exercises should resemble the work. For a perception engineer, a good ML-depth interview starts from a realistic failure ("the pedestrian detector's recall dropped from 0.91 to 0.84 on the night slice after the last data refresh; how do you find out why?") and follows the candidate's reasoning into data, labels, metrics and model behaviour. A good system-design interview asks for the design of a part of the real system (an evaluation pipeline that compares two models on 200 hours of fleet video overnight) and probes the trade-offs. Take-home assignments give a realistic signal but cost the candidate many hours and favour people with free evenings; if you use one, keep it to two or three hours and pay for it or offer an alternative.

Every interview gets a written rubric: for each competency, what weak, adequate, strong and exceptional answers look like at the target level, with examples. Rubrics turn "I liked them" into "they found the label-noise explanation unprompted and proposed a way to test it, which is strong on our rubric". They also make interviewers consistent with each other and allow new interviewers to be trained by shadowing and reverse-shadowing (the trainee interviews while an experienced interviewer observes).

2.5Structured interviews, validity and bias

A structured interview asks every candidate for the same role the same core questions, scores the answers against a predefined rubric, and records the evidence for each score. An unstructured interview is a conversation that goes wherever the interviewer takes it. The difference in quality is large and well measured. Industrial psychologists summarise how well a selection method predicts later job performance by its validity, the correlation between the method's score and a measure of job performance. The most cited summary, by Schmidt and Hunter (1998), put structured interviews at 0.51 and unstructured ones at 0.38. A re-analysis by Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022) corrected statistical adjustments that had inflated earlier estimates and found structured interviews at the top of the list at 0.42, job knowledge tests at 0.40, work samples at 0.33, general cognitive ability tests at 0.31, and unstructured interviews at 0.19.

A validity of 0.42 is a correlation, not a hit rate: it means a single structured interview carries real but limited information about how the person will perform. This is why loops have several interviews, and why the way the judgements are combined matters as much as each judgement (Section 2.6).

Structure also reduces bias, because it narrows the room in which unrelated impressions can act. The common biases in hiring are well known: affinity bias (rating people who are like us more highly: same university, same hobbies, same way of talking), the halo effect (one strong impression colours every other score), confirmation bias (forming a view in the first minutes and then hearing only the evidence for it), and contrast effects (a candidate looks weaker after a very strong one). The countermeasures are mechanical rather than moral: the same questions for everyone, scores against a rubric, notes written during or immediately after the interview, and no discussion between interviewers before they have written their feedback.

Pitfall

"Culture fit" as a hiring criterion. In practice it usually means "someone like us", which is affinity bias with a name. If there are values the team really needs (owning problems end to end, writing things down, disagreeing openly), write them into the scorecard as behaviours, ask about them with structured questions, and score them like any other competency. Some companies call this "values fit" or "culture add" to make the point.

In automotive

In Germany the General Equal Treatment Act (Allgemeines Gleichbehandlungsgesetz, AGG) applies to job ads and the selection process. It prohibits discrimination on grounds of race or ethnic origin, sex, religion or belief, disability, age and sexual identity. A rejected applicant who shows indications of discrimination can claim compensation, up to three monthly salaries if they would not have been hired even without the discrimination, and must do so in writing within two months (§ 15 AGG). This is why German job ads say "(m/w/d)" after the title, why interview questions about family plans or age are avoided, and why written, rubric-based interview notes protect the company as well as the candidate.

2.6The debrief and the hire or no-hire decision

After the loop, each interviewer writes their feedback independently: a recommendation (strong no, no, yes, strong yes), the scores per competency and the evidence behind them. Only then does the panel meet for a debrief, run by the hiring manager or a trained bar raiser. The order matters. If people talk first, the first confident speaker anchors everyone else, and four interviewers produce one opinion. Written-first debriefs keep the four views independent, so that the panel's combined judgement contains more information than any single one.

The interactive figure models this. Each interview is a noisy measurement of a candidate's true job performance with a given validity. The panel averages several interviews; their errors may be correlated, for example because interviewers discussed the candidate before writing feedback, because they asked the same question, or because they all reacted to the same first impression. The company makes offers to a fixed share of the candidates who reach the loop.

With Arden's loop of four structured interviews (validity 0.42 each) and independent feedback, the panel's combined validity is 0.68, and if offers go to the top quarter of candidates, 59 % of the people hired are in the top quarter of true performance, against 25 % for a random pick and 44 % for a single structured interview. If the same four interviewers discuss the candidate before writing (modelled as an error correlation of 0.5), they are worth only 1.6 independent interviews, the panel validity falls to 0.51, and the share of strong hires falls to 48 %. Four unstructured interviews (validity 0.19) reach a panel validity of only 0.36: more interviews cannot compensate for interviews that measure little.

The model also shows the trade-off in the bar. Making offers to only the top 10 % raises the share of strong hires to 74 %, but turns away 70 % of the strong candidates and takes longer to fill the role. Every hiring process makes both kinds of error; the question is which one you can afford. Most companies prefer to reject some good candidates rather than hire a weak one, because a weak hire is expensive to correct, which is the origin of the rule "if in doubt, no hire". For a role the team needs urgently, the cost of a long search is also real, and the hiring manager has to weigh it honestly rather than lower the bar silently.

Who decides differs between companies. In many, the hiring manager decides after the debrief and owns the outcome. Some large companies use a hiring committee that reads the written packet without having met the candidate, which separates the decision from the interviewers' impressions and keeps the bar consistent across teams. Amazon's bar raiser is a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team with a veto, whose job is to make sure each hire raises the average of the team. All three approaches try to solve the same problem: the hiring manager is under pressure to fill the role and is therefore biased towards "yes".

2.7Closing: from offer to acceptance

Good candidates have other options, and the time between the final interview and the offer is when many of them are lost. Close quickly (an offer within a few days of the loop), make the offer personally (the hiring manager calls), and know what the candidate cares about before you make it: the problem, the team, growth, flexibility, money, stability. A candidate who wants to work on hard perception problems at scale responds to a conversation with the tech lead about the hard-case mining pipeline more than to a signing bonus.

Negotiation is normal and not a sign of bad faith. Keep the offer within the level's band, explain how pay is structured (base, bonus, equity), and avoid exploding offers that pressure people; candidates who accept under pressure are more likely to keep looking. If a candidate declines, ask why and record it; a pattern of declines for the same reason is information about the offer or the process.

2.8Onboarding: the hire is not done when the contract is signed

Onboarding decides how quickly a new engineer becomes productive and whether they stay. Its goals are concrete: by the end of the first week the new person has a working development environment, has shipped a small change to production (a fix, a test, a documentation improvement), and knows who to ask for what; by the end of the first month they understand the team's system well enough to take on a normal piece of work; by the end of the third month they own an area. An onboarding buddy (a peer, not the manager) answers the hundred small questions that people are embarrassed to ask a manager.

The manager's part is to write the 30-60-90 day goals before the person starts, derived from the outcomes on the scorecard; to hold weekly one-to-ones from the first day; and to ask, at the end of the first month, what was confusing, missing or wrong in the onboarding, and fix it for the next person. New hires see the team's undocumented assumptions more clearly than anyone else, and that window closes after a few months.

3Building and scaling teams

A team is not a set of people who share a manager; it is a group that owns something together, plans together and covers for each other. Building one means choosing who joins and in which order, deciding what the team owns, and recognising early when it has grown too large to work as one team.

3.1The first hires decide what kind of team it becomes

The first few hires of a new or small team have an outsized effect: they set the quality bar, the habits (how code is reviewed, how decisions are written down, how on-call works) and the network through which later candidates are found. Hire for the team you need in a year, not for the gap you feel this week. At Arden, the gap this week is "more hands on the truck maker's requests"; the team in a year needs someone who can own evaluation and data quality, because both the fleet product and the customer contract depend on trustworthy numbers. The first hire is therefore the evaluation-focused senior engineer from Section 2.2, not another model engineer.

Two simple tools help. A skills matrix lists the areas the team owns (detection models, tracking, lane perception, driver monitoring, data pipeline, evaluation, on-board integration) against the people, with a mark for "can work on it alone" and "can teach it". It shows the bus factor of each area (how many people could disappear before nobody understands it) and therefore where to hire and where to spread knowledge. The second is the seniority mix. A team of only seniors is expensive and often lacks people who enjoy well-defined work; a team with too many juniors overloads the seniors with mentoring. A common healthy shape for a product team is about a third senior and above, half mid-level and the rest junior, with at least two people who can make sound technical decisions alone.

In practice

When a team is small, a single strong hire who leaves can undo a year of building. Spread ownership deliberately from the start: every area has a primary and a secondary owner, the secondary does part of the on-call and reviews for that area, and the primary takes a real holiday at least once a year without the area falling over. A holiday that the team survives is the cheapest test of the bus factor.

3.2Team size, team types and cognitive load

Small teams work better for a structural reason. The number of communication paths between n people grows as n(n−1)/2: 15 for a team of six, 55 for eleven, 105 for fifteen. Each person can maintain only so many close working relationships, and as the number of paths grows the team either spends more time coordinating or stops coordinating and starts to fragment informally. Most practitioners settle on five to nine people per team (Amazon's "two-pizza team" is the same idea); Team Topologies recommends small, long-lived teams of no more than about nine people and groups of teams of no more than about 50, citing Dunbar's work on group sizes.

The senior-engineering chapter explains Conway's law and the four team types from Team Topologies (Senior engineering 11.1): stream-aligned teams own a flow of work for users end to end, platform teams provide internal services that stream-aligned teams consume, enabling teams help others adopt new capabilities, and complicated-subsystem teams own a part that needs rare specialist knowledge. From the manager's chair the most useful idea in the book is cognitive load: a team can own only as much as it can understand well enough to change safely. When a team owns too much, the symptoms are predictable: estimates get worse, incidents happen in areas "nobody really knows", on-call becomes dreaded, and new hires take longer and longer to become productive. The fix is to give the team less to own, either by splitting the team or by moving a part of its scope to a platform.

A team's boundary should therefore be drawn around something it can own end to end with a manageable load: a product area, a stage of a pipeline with a clear interface, or a customer. Boundaries drawn around technologies ("the Python team", "the CUDA team") or around phases of work ("the testing team") create hand-offs on every change and should be the exception.

3.3Scaling from one team to three

Arden's perception team grows over eighteen months as the truck maker's contract ramps up. The stepper shows the four stages and the decisions at each.

The starting point. One backlog, one standup, one on-call rotation. You know every change that ships and can still review designs yourself. The team can absorb two or three more people without changing shape.

Six months later the team has eleven people and the signs of overload appear: the standup takes 35 minutes and most people listen to updates that do not concern them, planning turns into a negotiation between the fleet features and the customer work, the on-call rotation covers areas nobody knows end to end, and you hold eleven weekly one-to-ones. Fifty-five communication paths are too many. This is the moment to split, ideally before the symptoms become a crisis.

The split follows the work, not the people. The perception models team stays stream-aligned to the product features (detection, tracking, lanes, driver monitoring). The data and ML platform team owns the data pipeline, labelling operations, training infrastructure and the evaluation system, and serves the models team through a written interface (what it provides, how fast, how to ask for changes). Each team has a tech lead; you manage both for now, which is possible because each team is small. Most engineers choose their team; the manager decides only where preferences and needs clash.

The truck maker asks for engineers near its development centre, and the integration work (running the stack on the customer's hardware, joint testing, answering their engineers' questions) is enough for a team. A third team starts in Munich. You can no longer manage 20 engineers directly, so each team gets a manager: the models team's tech lead, who wanted to try management, becomes its manager; a manager with platform experience is hired externally for the data and ML platform team; and the first hire in Munich is the integration team's manager. You become a manager of managers (Chapter 6).

Two rules make splits go better. Split along a seam that already exists in the work, where the teams will need to talk to each other less than they do now; a split that creates a hand-off on every change makes things worse. And split before the team is in crisis, while people still have the energy to set up new rituals and interfaces; a split during a missed deadline feels like blame.

Pitfall

Splitting a team by moving the "weaker" people into the new team, or by keeping all the senior people in one team. The new team then has no one who can make independent decisions, its manager or lead spends all their time rescuing it, and everyone reads the split as a judgement. Each team needs at least one or two people who can own its area alone from the first day.

3.4Opening a new site

New sites are opened for good and bad reasons. Good reasons are access to talent that will not move, proximity to a customer or a partner (Arden's case), and coverage of time zones for operations. A bad reason is cost alone: a site chosen only because salaries are lower usually receives the work nobody else wants, struggles to hire good people for it, and confirms the prejudice that it is a second-class location.

A new site works when it owns something real from the start: a product area, a customer, or a complete component, with decisions made locally. A site that is an "extended bench" for teams elsewhere spends its time waiting for decisions in another time zone. It also needs critical mass: one or two people alone in a new office are isolated, so plan to reach five to eight people within the first year, start with a senior anchor who knows the company's culture (often a transfer from headquarters for a year or two), and budget for travel in both directions. The Munich integration team starts with its manager and a senior engineer who moves from Amsterdam for eighteen months, and reaches six people within a year.

In automotive

Building a team in Germany brings two institutions that managers from US or UK companies often meet for the first time. The first is the works council (Betriebsrat), an elected body of employees that can be formed in any establishment with at least five permanent employees (§ 1 BetrVG, the Works Constitution Act); employees, not management, decide to set one up. It has rights of co-determination on, among other things, working-time rules, overtime and the introduction of technical systems that are suitable for monitoring employees' behaviour or performance (§ 87(1) no. 6): in practice this covers time tracking, many productivity dashboards and even some developer tools, which therefore need a works agreement before they are introduced. In companies with more than 20 employees eligible to vote, hiring and transfers need its consent (§ 99), and it must be heard before every dismissal; a dismissal without that hearing is void (§ 102). The second is dismissal protection (Kündigungsschutzgesetz): after six months of employment, in establishments with more than ten employees, a dismissal needs a reason related to the person, their conduct or the business. Neither is an obstacle to running a good team, but both reward managers who document expectations and involve HR and the works council early rather than at the end.

4One-to-ones, goals and growth

One-to-ones are the manager's main instrument: the place where trust is built, problems surface early, feedback is given, and careers are discussed. They work only if they belong to the engineer rather than to the manager's status needs.

4.1Running one-to-ones that are worth the time

A one-to-one is a regular private conversation between a manager and each direct report, typically 30 minutes every week or 45 to 60 minutes every two weeks. Its purpose is everything that does not fit in a group setting: what the person is worried about, what is getting in their way, how they feel about their work and the team, what they want to learn, and feedback in both directions. It is not a status meeting. If the first fifteen minutes of every one-to-one are a list of tickets, the conversation never reaches the topics that matter, and the engineer learns that the manager cares about tickets.

The engineer owns the agenda; the manager keeps a shared running document with notes, open items and a few standing prompts for when the engineer has nothing prepared: what was the best and the worst part of the last two weeks; what is slowing you down; is there anything you would like feedback on; what should we stop doing as a team. The manager brings their own items too (feedback, context about upcoming changes, a question about goals), but after the engineer's.

Two habits separate good one-to-ones from routine ones. The first is to never cancel them casually; moving one is fine, but a manager who cancels one-to-ones whenever something "more important" comes up tells people exactly where they rank. The second is to notice what is not said: an engineer who used to bring topics and now has "nothing this week" for a month is often disengaging, and that is the moment to ask, not the day they hand in their notice.

Talk track

To a new direct report in the first one-to-one: "This half hour is yours. I'll keep notes in a shared document so we can both see what we agreed. If you don't have anything, I'll ask a few questions, but I'd rather you set the agenda. And if there's something about how I work that gets in your way, this is the place to tell me; I'll ask about it every month or so, because I know it's awkward to bring up."

4.2Goals and growth plans from the manager's side

Engineers grow mainly through the work they do, not through training courses. The manager's main lever is therefore the matching of work to people: who gets which project, which problem, which customer meeting. A growth plan makes this deliberate. Once or twice a year, separately from the performance review, the manager and the engineer agree where the engineer wants to be in one to two years (the next level, a move towards management, deeper expertise in an area, a different domain), which two or three abilities stand between them and that goal, and which work in the next six months will exercise those abilities. The plan is short, written, and revisited in one-to-ones.

At Arden, one of the mid-level engineers wants to become senior. The gap, measured against the level guide, is scope and independence: they deliver well-defined tasks reliably but have not yet owned an ambiguous problem from start to finish. The plan gives them the evaluation of the driver-monitoring models on the truck maker's cabin cameras, a problem with no clear owner and a real customer, with the tech lead as a sounding board rather than a supervisor. The manager's job is to make sure the work stays visible and that the engineer gets the credit.

The senior-engineering chapter covers the difference between mentoring, coaching and sponsorship and how to delegate at the right level (Senior engineering 8.1 and 8.2). For a manager, the important addition is that sponsorship is part of the job, not a favour: putting someone forward for a visible project, mentioning their work in rooms they are not in, and making the case for their promotion.

4.3Allocating opportunities fairly

Because work assignments drive growth, they are also where unfairness accumulates quietly. The most visible projects tend to go to the people who ask loudest or who resemble the manager; the necessary but unglamorous work (onboarding, documentation, coordinating with other teams, organising the offsite), sometimes called glue work, tends to land on the same few people, often women, and is undervalued in reviews (Senior engineering 3.5).

The countermeasures are simple to state and require discipline: keep a list of who led which visible project in the last year and rotate deliberately; assign glue work explicitly, as a named responsibility that rotates and is recognised in reviews, instead of letting it fall to whoever volunteers; and before each staffing decision ask who else could do this and would grow from it.

4.4Retention and attrition signals

People leave managers, the saying goes, but more precisely they leave situations: no growth, unclear or shifting priorities, a bad relationship with the manager or a colleague, pay that has fallen behind the market, or a life change. Most of these are visible months in advance to a manager who is paying attention. Common signals are a drop in engagement in one-to-ones and meetings, fewer opinions in design discussions, a sudden interest in exactly how the promotion process works, more days off taken one at a time, and a change in how the person talks about the future ("if I'm still here next year").

The useful response is a stay conversation, held before anyone resigns: what keeps you here, what would make you leave, what would you change about your work. It is much easier to change someone's project or address a pay gap before they have an offer than to counter an offer, and counter-offers often only delay a departure because they fix the money but not the reason.

Managers distinguish regretted attrition (people the team would want to keep) from non-regretted attrition. Some non-regretted attrition is healthy. A team with no attrition at all over several years can be a sign that the bar is not being held; a team with high regretted attrition is losing its future, because the people who leave first are usually the ones with the best options.

Numbers

The cost of losing an engineer at Arden, worked out from this chapter's numbers: about three months to hire a replacement, up to three months of notice period in Germany, about six months to full productivity, and about 55 engineer hours of interviewing. A departure of a productive senior therefore costs on the order of six to nine months of that person's output, before counting the knowledge that leaves with them.

5Performance management as the manager

Performance management is the part of the job new managers dread most and the part that most affects whether a team trusts its manager. Done well it is mostly unremarkable: clear expectations, frequent feedback, a written review with no surprises, honest advocacy in calibration, and early, fair action when someone is not meeting the bar.

5.1Expectations come first

People cannot meet expectations they do not know. Most performance problems that reach a review started months earlier as a gap between what the manager expected and what the engineer thought was expected. The level guide of the company's career ladder describes expectations in general terms (Senior engineering 2.1); the manager translates them into the person's actual work. For a mid-level engineer at Arden that might read: owns features of the size of "add the cut-in event to the fleet product" from design to production with occasional guidance; writes designs that the tech lead approves without major rework; handles their on-call shifts without escalation for known failure types.

Write these down at the start of each review period and when someone's role changes. Then check, in one-to-ones, how the person is doing against them. Expectations that are clear on paper also make the later conversations much easier: "here is what we agreed, here is what I see" is a conversation about facts.

5.2No surprises: feedback through the year

The rule that matters most is that nothing in a written review should surprise the person. If an engineer reads in October that their designs were too shallow, and nobody told them in March, the review is not feedback but a verdict, and the engineer will rightly feel that the manager withheld information they needed. Feedback belongs close to the event, in one-to-ones and in the moment, using the techniques described in Senior engineering 8.3 (concrete situation, behaviour, impact; caring personally while being direct).

A practical habit is to keep a private, factual log for each direct report: dated notes of notable work, feedback given, feedback received from others, and agreed actions. It takes five minutes a week and turns the review from an exercise in memory (which over-weights the last six weeks, the recency effect) into a summary of evidence.

5.3Writing a review

A written review does three things: it states how the person performed against the expectations of their level, it gives the evidence for that judgement, and it says what they should focus on next. Good reviews are specific, balanced and about impact; weak ones are generic, list activities rather than outcomes, or describe personality rather than behaviour.

5.4Calibration from the manager's chair

The senior-engineering chapter explains why calibration exists and how it looks from the engineer's side (Senior engineering 3.2). In the room, the manager has two jobs that pull in different directions: to advocate for their people, and to help the group apply the same standard to everyone, including to their own team.

Prepare for each person a short summary: the proposed rating, the two or three strongest pieces of evidence with scope and impact, and the honest weakness. Managers who present every person as exceptional lose credibility quickly, and the next time their genuinely exceptional engineer is discussed, the room discounts it. Listen to challenges as information: if another manager says "that sounds like solid mid-level work, what made it senior?", either the evidence is missing from your summary or your bar is off. Afterwards, the manager owns the outcome in front of the engineer. "Calibration decided" is not an explanation; the manager explains the rating as their own and what would change it next time.

Pitfall

Forced distributions (a fixed share of the lowest rating in every team) turn calibration into a negotiation about who has to give someone up. They can make a strong small team rate a good engineer as low and a weak team hide a real problem. Many companies have moved away from strict curves to "guidelines" for the distribution at the level of a large group; where a curve is enforced, managers need to know it before writing reviews, not after.

5.5Promotions: building the case and saying "not yet"

Most companies promote people who are already working at the next level, rather than promoting them so that they can grow into it. The manager's work therefore starts long before the promotion cycle: identifying who is close, making sure they get work of the next level's scope, and collecting the evidence as it happens. The case itself is a written document, usually read by a committee that does not know the person, which compares concrete work against the next level's expectations, with the scope, the difficulty, the person's specific contribution and the impact. Senior engineering 3.4 walks through such a case from the engineer's side.

"Not yet" is one of the most important conversations a manager has. Done badly ("the committee didn't think you're ready") it demotivates; done well it is a plan: what the committee or you saw, which one or two gaps remain, which work in the next six months closes them, and when you will review progress. Never promise a promotion; promise to make the case when the evidence is there, and to tell the person honestly where it stands.

5.6Underperformance: diagnose, support, decide

When someone is not meeting the expectations of their level, the manager has to act, early and fairly. Waiting is the most common mistake: the team notices long before the manager acts, picks up the slack, and starts to wonder whether the bar applies to everyone. The longer a manager waits, the harder the conversation becomes, because the person can rightly ask why nobody said anything.

The first step is diagnosis. Senior engineering 8.4 describes an order of diagnosis that applies equally to a manager: expectations (does the person know what is expected?), skill (can they do it?), context (is something in the environment in the way: the project, the codebase, a conflict?), and only then motivation or personal circumstances. The first three are common, fixable, and not anyone's fault. A manager has more levers than a tech lead: they can change the project, the team, the expectations of the role, or offer time off or support for a personal situation.

Collect concrete examples from the last weeks (missed commitments, review comments, incidents), check them against the written expectations, and talk to the tech lead. Look for causes in the expectations, the skills and the context before assuming a motivation problem.

Tell the person directly, in private, that their performance is below the expectations of their level, with two or three specific examples and the impact. Ask for their view: often there is context the manager did not know. Agree on what good looks like over the next weeks and write it down. The conversation should be unambiguous; a gap described so gently that the person does not realise it is serious is the main reason later steps feel unfair.

For four to six weeks, the person works towards three concrete goals with weekly check-ins, and the manager removes obstacles: pairing with a senior engineer, a smaller and clearer project, training, or relief from a part of the role. Both write short weekly notes. Many situations resolve here, especially when the cause was unclear expectations or the wrong project.

At the end of the support period, the manager and the person review the goals together. If performance has reached the bar, the plan ends and normal feedback continues; it is important to say so explicitly. If not, the manager decides, with HR, whether to move to a formal plan, change the role, or discuss a mutual separation.

A performance improvement plan (PIP) is a written document with specific, measurable goals, the support provided, a fixed duration (commonly 30 to 90 days) and the consequence if the goals are not met. It is reviewed weekly and documented. A PIP should be a real chance to succeed, not a formality before a dismissal; if the manager has already decided, it is more honest to discuss a separation directly. The outcome is either that the person meets the goals, or an exit handled with respect.

Pitfall

Using a performance plan to manage out someone the manager simply does not get along with, or as the first conversation about a problem. Both are visible to the rest of the team, and both destroy the trust that makes the process work for the next person. The test is simple: could the engineer have described the gap, in their own words, before the plan started?

5.7Exits

Some people leave because they are not meeting the bar after a fair process; others because of a reorganisation or a reduction in force (a layoff) that is about the business rather than their performance; others because the role has changed and no longer fits them. In every case the manager's job is the same: decide with HR and legal on the facts, tell the person yourself, in private, clearly and kindly, explain what happens next (last day, pay, references, how the team will be told), and protect their dignity in front of the team. The remaining team will judge the company by how the departing person was treated.

Tell the team promptly and briefly, without details of the reasons for an individual departure, and plan the hand-over of the person's work. After a layoff, expect the remaining people to be shaken: hold one-to-ones with everyone in the following days, explain the reasons and the plan as honestly as you can, and answer the question everyone is asking privately ("am I next?") as truthfully as you are allowed to.

In automotive

In Germany a PIP has no special legal status; what counts in a dispute is whether a dismissal is socially justified under the dismissal protection law. A dismissal for conduct usually requires a prior written warning (Abmahnung) for the same kind of conduct; a dismissal for poor performance requires evidence that performance is well below that of comparable employees over a longer period, which is hard to establish. The works council must be heard before every dismissal (§ 102 BetrVG). In practice most separations are therefore negotiated as a mutual termination agreement (Aufhebungsvertrag), often with a severance payment. A manager's best contribution is the same as anywhere: written expectations, documented feedback and support, and HR involved early.

6Managing managers and several teams

When you manage managers, you no longer manage the work or even most of the people directly. You manage the system: who leads each team, how teams are shaped, how decisions are made and how consistent the standards are, and you learn what is happening mostly through other people's eyes.

6.1What changes when your reports are managers

Three things change. Your information becomes second-hand: what you know about a team's work, morale and problems comes mostly from its manager, compressed and filtered by their judgement of what matters. Your actions become indirect: you change things by coaching managers, setting standards and changing structures, and if you act directly on a team's work you undermine its manager. And your time horizon lengthens: the decisions that are yours (who manages a team, how many people each team gets, how the group is organised) play out over quarters and years.

The most common failure of new managers of managers is to keep managing the teams themselves: joining their standups, giving direct instructions to engineers, answering questions that their managers should answer. It feels helpful and it produces managers who cannot manage, because every decision they make can be overridden. The opposite failure is abdication: assuming that because a team has a manager, everything in it is fine. The job is between the two: give managers real ownership of their teams, and know enough about each team to notice when that ownership is not working.

6.2Hiring and growing managers

Whether to promote a manager from inside or hire one from outside is a trade-off. An internal promotion brings deep knowledge of the system and the people and the trust of the team, and it is a strong signal that growth is possible; its risk is a first-time manager who needs a year of support, and a team whose former peer is now their manager. An external hire brings management experience and fresh perspective; its risk is someone who does not understand the domain or the culture, and a team that feels passed over. At Arden the models team gets its tech lead as manager (they wanted the move, the team respects them, and you have time to coach them) while the platform team gets an experienced external manager, because the team has no candidate and needs someone who has run a platform before.

First-time managers need explicit support: a written description of what the job is and is not, a weekly one-to-one in which you talk mostly about their people and their decisions rather than about the team's status, a peer group of other new managers, and permission to make mistakes and talk about them. Watch for the classic early problems: holding on to the technical work, avoiding difficult conversations, and trying to be liked by everyone. Agree with them, in advance, how they can step back to an engineering role without loss of face if management turns out not to be for them.

6.3Getting ground truth: skip-levels and artefacts

Because the information that reaches you through managers is filtered, you need ways to sample the teams directly without bypassing their managers. The standard instrument is the skip-level: a conversation with the reports of your reports, one-to-one or in small groups, every few months. Its purpose is to hear how the team sees its work, its manager and the organisation, to catch problems that are not reaching you, and to let people know who you are. It is not a place to make decisions about their work, and the managers should know when skip-levels happen and what themes came up (without attributing them to individuals).

Artefacts are a second channel: design documents, postmortems, review comments, demo recordings and the questions people ask in shared channels show how a team thinks and how it treats each other, often more honestly than a status report. Metrics are a third, with the usual caution (Senior engineering 9): trends in on-call load, delivery predictability, model-release lead time and attrition say where to look, not what is wrong.

Talk track

Opening a skip-level: "I do these with every team every few months. I'm not here to check on your manager or to make decisions about your work; I want to hear how things look from where you sit, what's getting in your way that might be mine to fix, and anything you think I don't know. I'll share themes with your manager, never who said what."

6.4Consistency across teams, and what to leave to them

Several teams under one manager need some common standards and should be free in most other things. The things that must be consistent are the ones where inconsistency is unfair or dangerous: the hiring bar and the interview process, the meaning of levels and ratings, on-call expectations and compensation, incident handling, security and safety practices, and the interfaces between teams. Things that can differ are the ones that each team can choose best for itself: how it plans its sprints, its rituals, how it splits work, its internal tools.

A useful rule is to standardise outcomes and interfaces rather than methods: "every model release has an evaluation report in the agreed format, signed off by the platform team's evaluation owner" rather than "every team uses the same sprint length". The manager of managers sets the few common standards, explains why, and runs a regular forum (a weekly staff meeting with their managers) where cross-team problems are raised and solved.

6.5Headcount and budget

A manager of managers owns a budget, most of which is people. Headcount planning usually happens once a year with quarterly adjustments: the manager proposes how many people the group needs, in which roles and on which teams, with the reasoning, and the leadership team allocates headcount across the company. The proposal is a business case, not a wish list: what the group will deliver with its current people, what additional people would make possible, and what will not happen if the headcount is not granted.

The cost of an engineer to the company, the fully loaded cost, is well above the salary: employer social contributions, benefits, equipment, office space, software licences, training and travel typically add 25 to 50 % in Europe. Machine-learning teams have an additional large budget item that is easy to forget in a headcount plan: compute for training and evaluation, data storage, and labelling, which at Arden is of the same order as the cost of the people in the data and ML platform team.

The second thing to understand about headcount is that hiring does not add capacity immediately; for some months it subtracts it. Existing engineers interview candidates and then onboard and mentor the new people, while the new people ramp up from zero. The interactive figure models a hiring push at Arden: the team of six makes offers for six months, each hire costs 55 engineer hours of interviewing, new hires start after their notice period, ramp to full productivity linearly, and need mentoring that falls as they ramp.

With 1.5 hires a month for six months and a one-month notice period, the team of six dips to about 5.1 engineer-equivalents in the first month, reaches 9.2 by month six and 15 by month twelve; the extra output catches up with the output lost during the dip around month six. With a three-month notice period, typical in Germany, the same plan delivers only 6.8 engineer-equivalents in month six and catches up around month eight. Hiring twice as fast deepens the dip (4.3 at its lowest) and doubles the eventual gain. The lesson for planning is the old one from Fred Brooks's The Mythical Man-Month (1975): hiring is an investment for next year, not a remedy for a deadline in three months. Adding people to a late project makes it later, because the dip arrives before the help does.

7Running delivery across teams

A manager is accountable for what the team delivers, but not for doing the planning, estimation and tracking personally. The job is to make sure the team works on the right things, that commitments are realistic and kept or renegotiated early, and that the system of forums, priorities and dependencies lets engineers spend most of their time on focused work.

7.1Planning and prioritisation

Most product companies plan in two horizons: a yearly direction (what the group wants to have achieved, how much headcount it has) and quarterly plans that commit to specific outcomes. Some use objectives and key results (OKRs) for the quarterly level: a few qualitative objectives, each with two to four measurable key results, set partly top-down and partly bottom-up. Whatever the format, the manager's contribution is to make the plan an honest trade-off rather than a list of everything everyone wants.

A practical device is to split capacity explicitly before choosing work. Arden's perception teams plan roughly 60 % for product and customer commitments, 20 % for engineering health (technical debt, tooling, the evaluation pipeline), 10 % for exploration, and keep 10 % unplanned for interrupts, incidents and customer escalations. The numbers differ by team and season; what matters is that the split is chosen deliberately and visibly, so that engineering health does not lose every individual trade-off against a feature and then collapse all at once. Estimation techniques, schedules and risk registers are described in Project management 8.2 and the following sections.

7.2The operating cadence

An operating cadence is the set of recurring forums through which a group plans, reviews and adjusts its work. Each forum answers one question at one time horizon, and a good cadence has as few forums as possible but no gaps: this week's blockers, the last two weeks' delivery, the month's metrics, the quarter's plan.

The weekly staff meeting with the managers handles cross-team problems, people topics, hiring and decisions that need several teams; it is not a round of status updates, which belong in a written weekly summary. The sprint reviews and demos show working software and models to the people who use them. The monthly review looks at the few metrics the group has agreed matter (Section 8.4), the trends and any surprises. Quarterly planning starts about three weeks before the quarter ends, so that dependencies between teams can be negotiated before commitments are made.

Pitfall

Meetings accumulate. Every problem produces a new recurring meeting, and none is ever cancelled. Once a quarter, list every recurring meeting the group holds, the question it answers and who needs to be there, and cancel those whose question is answered elsewhere. A manager's calendar full of meetings is a sign that decisions are not delegated.

7.3Dependencies between teams

Dependencies are the main source of slipping plans in a multi-team group, because each team plans its own work well and nobody plans the hand-offs. The tactics for a single cross-team project (a shared dependency map, early asks, named owners, regular sync) are in Senior engineering 11.2. The manager of managers has an additional lever: structure. If two teams depend on each other for most of their work, the boundary between them is in the wrong place, and the fix is to move it rather than to add coordination. If one team is on everyone's critical path, it either needs more capacity, a self-service interface that removes it from the path, or a narrower scope.

At Arden the first quarter after the split shows the problem clearly: every model release waits for the platform team to run the evaluation, and the platform team is busy building the new labelling pipeline. The managers agree to make evaluation self-service: the platform team builds a tool the models team runs itself, with the platform team owning the tool and the evaluation set rather than each run.

7.4Protecting focus and saying no

Engineers need long blocks of uninterrupted time to do difficult work, and every team is under constant pressure from requests: a customer question, a bug from the fleet product, a favour for another team, a meeting. The manager's job is to absorb and route that pressure so that most of the team can work. Common techniques: a rotating "interrupt" or "shield" role that takes all incoming requests for a week while the others focus; meeting-free blocks; a single intake channel for requests with a visible queue; and limits on work in progress, because a team that starts everything finishes nothing (the queueing effect is shown in Senior engineering 14.2).

Saying no is part of protecting focus. A manager who accepts every request makes promises the team cannot keep and teaches stakeholders that the loudest request wins. A better answer is usually a conditional yes that makes the trade-off visible: "We can do the extra night-time evaluation for the customer this month if we move the tracking improvement to next month; which do you prefer?" (Senior engineering 5.3).

7.5When delivery slips

Plans slip. What separates good managers from others is how early they see it, how honestly they say it, and how well they present the options. The four levers are always the same: reduce scope, move the date, add people (which helps only if the delay is long enough to absorb the dip from Section 6.5, and the work can be split), or accept risk, for example by cutting testing, which is almost never acceptable for a perception system that goes into vehicles. Present the options with their consequences and a recommendation, as early as the slip becomes likely rather than certain; a two-week warning a month ahead is a planning problem, the same news a day before the deadline is a trust problem. The senior-engineering chapter walks through a slipping launch (Senior engineering 5.4), and project management covers the formal side of change and escalation (Project management 10.4).

8Managing machine-learning teams that ship

Machine-learning work does not behave like feature work. Outcomes are uncertain, progress is measured in metrics rather than features, much of the effort goes into data and infrastructure, and a model that works in a notebook is far from a model that works in a vehicle. Managing it well means planning for uncertainty, making the path from prototype to product explicit, and staffing the unglamorous parts.

8.1Research work and product work have different shapes

A feature has a known destination and an uncertain cost. A research question ("can we detect driver drowsiness from the cabin camera reliably enough to warn?") has an uncertain destination: after four weeks the answer may be "yes", "not with this camera", or "we don't know yet". Treating research like features (estimating it in story points, committing a date for a result) produces either padded estimates or missed commitments; treating features like research (open-ended exploration) produces nothing that ships.

The manager's tools for research are timeboxes and questions. A research task is framed as a question with a decision attached ("by the end of four weeks we decide whether to build drowsiness detection into the next release, stop it, or invest another four weeks"), and the timebox is fixed while the scope adapts. What must be delivered at the end is an answer with evidence, not a model. Keep research and product work visibly separate in the plan, and protect the research timebox from being consumed by urgent product work, which otherwise always wins.

8.2Gates from prototype to product

The most expensive mistake in managing ML teams is letting a promising prototype drift into production without anyone deciding that it should. The prototype accumulates users, the team spends months making it robust, and only then does someone discover that it cannot meet the latency budget on the target hardware or that the improvement on the benchmark does not appear in the field. Explicit gates prevent this by asking, at each stage, for the evidence that justifies the next, more expensive stage.

At Arden the gates look like this. Exploration is timeboxed and ends with a short written result. The feasibility gate asks whether the new approach beats the current model on the frozen evaluation set by a margin agreed in advance, not chosen after seeing the numbers. The prototype gate asks whether it fits the latency and memory budget on the target hardware with the full pre- and post-processing, whether training is reproducible, and whether any slice of the evaluation set (night, rain, tunnels, unusual vehicles) got worse. The shadow gate asks whether the disagreements between the new and the old model on fleet data have been reviewed and whether the field effect has been estimated. The rollout gate checks guardrail metrics at each step. The mechanics of shadow mode, drift monitoring and staged rollout are in Neural network training 14.2; the data engine that feeds hard cases back into training in Deep learning for vision 15.1.

Why it matters

Gates make stopping cheap and normal. A team that may only report success hides failures until they are expensive; a team whose manager treats "we stopped it at the feasibility gate, here is what we learned" as a good result tries more ideas and wastes less time on bad ones. For a supplier to a truck maker, the gates also line up with the customer's release process and the safety argument (Functional safety 12).

8.3Staffing the whole system, not only the models

The visible part of a perception group is the model work; most of what makes models good in production is elsewhere. A well-known paper from Google, "Hidden technical debt in machine learning systems" (Sculley et al., 2015), made the point with a figure in which the ML code is a small box surrounded by much larger boxes for data collection, verification, feature extraction, configuration, serving infrastructure and monitoring. A manager has to staff all of it, or the model engineers end up doing it badly in their spare time.

8.4The metrics a perception manager reports

A perception manager reports upwards and to customers on a small set of metrics that answer three questions: how good is the system, how fast are we improving it, and what does it cost. Model quality is reported on the frozen evaluation set per slice, not only as one average (overall recall can rise while night recall falls), together with the number of regressions caught before release. Field quality is reported with the product's own measures: at Arden, false collision warnings per 1,000 driving hours and the share of real events detected, from reviewed fleet samples. Speed of improvement is measured by the time from a field failure being found to a fixed model in the fleet, and the number of evaluated experiments per week. Cost is compute, labelling and storage per month, and per model release.

The usual caution from the metrics section of the senior-engineering chapter applies (Senior engineering 9.1): these are measures of the system, not targets for individuals. A team rewarded for its benchmark number will overfit the benchmark; a team rewarded for its number of experiments will run small ones. Pair each metric with one that catches its abuse (benchmark quality with field quality; experiment count with the share of experiments that change a decision).

8.5Planning under research uncertainty

Because individual ML bets fail often, a perception group's plan works best as a portfolio: a large share of the capacity on improvements with a high probability of a modest gain (more data for a known weak slice, a better augmentation), a smaller share on bets with a lower probability and a larger payoff (a new architecture, a new sensor modality), and explicit kill criteria for each. A manager who commits to a customer only what the high-probability work can deliver, and treats the bets as upside, keeps commitments while still making room for the ideas that change the product.

When a customer or the leadership asks "when will the model be good enough?", the honest answer has the shape of a forecast with a confidence, not a date: "on the current trend, night recall reaches the target in two to four months; we will know which end by the next release, and if it is not on track we have a fallback of restricting the feature at night". That kind of answer is easier to give if the team measures its rate of improvement routinely.

9Representing the team outside

A manager is the team's interface to the rest of the company and often to customers and partners. The team's reputation, its priorities and how much help it gets depend on how well that interface works.

9.1Managing up

Managing up means making your manager effective at helping you and your team. Your manager needs three things from you: no surprises (bad news early, from you, not from someone else), decisions framed so they can be made quickly (the question, the options, your recommendation, what you need from them), and a clear view of the few things that matter most right now. The techniques for communicating up concisely are in Senior engineering 4.4; for a manager, the content shifts from technical decisions to people, delivery and resources.

It also means understanding what your manager is measured on and under pressure from, and connecting your team's work to it. At Arden, the VP of Engineering is judged on the truck maker's contract milestones and on the fleet product's reliability; a perception manager who reports progress in those terms, and raises risks to them early, gets support for the investments (evaluation tooling, data quality) that make both possible.

Talk track

A weekly note to your manager, five lines: "Customer milestone B on track; the latency risk is retired, measured 41 ms on target. Risk: night recall is two points below the target; options in the attached page, I recommend restricting the feature at night for the first release. Decision needed by Friday: second headcount for Munich. People: one senior engineer considering an internal move to the cloud team, we're talking. Nothing else needs you."

9.2Technical meetings with customers and partners

Engineers in technical meetings with a customer are the company's most credible voice: customers trust them more than sales, because they have nothing to sell. That trust is also the risk. An engineer who agrees to a change in the meeting because it is technically easy has made a commitment the company may not be able to keep, and an engineer who answers a question about a known weakness too frankly can create a contractual issue.

The manager prepares the team for such meetings: what the meeting is for, what the customer is likely to ask, who answers which topics, which questions the team should not answer on the spot ("we'll come back to you in writing"), and who writes the minutes. After the meeting, the commitments made are confirmed in writing and entered into the team's plan. The goal is not to make engineers cautious but to separate technical discussion, which should be open, from commitments on scope, dates and liability, which go through the project lead and the contract.

In automotive

Technical meetings with a carmaker have some particular habits. The carmaker's engineers are often specialists in exactly the area being discussed and expect the supplier to bring data: measurement reports, evaluation results per scenario, and analysis of specific field cases. The supplier's project lead usually leads the meeting and owns commitments (Project management 10.2); changes to the agreed scope go through the formal change request process, however small they look in the room; and the minutes, often written by the carmaker, have weight in later disputes, so the supplier checks and corrects them promptly. Meetings frequently mix languages; agree beforehand who speaks for the supplier on which topic.

9.3Collaboration across companies

When engineers from two companies work on the same system (Arden's integration team and the truck maker's driver-assistance team), the usual tools of cross-team work apply, with three additions. Intellectual property and confidentiality define what can be shared: model weights, training data and source code are often covered by different clauses of the contract, and engineers need a simple written guide of what they may send. Interfaces need to be written down and versioned more strictly than inside a company, because a misunderstanding costs a change request rather than a conversation. And the relationship needs a regular forum at two levels: an engineering sync for the working teams and a steering meeting for the managers, so that technical teams can solve problems directly without escalating every disagreement to the commercial level.

10Team health and culture

A team's output over years depends on whether people can disagree openly, recover from mistakes, sustain their effort and trust that changes are made for good reasons. These things are shaped more by the manager's daily behaviour than by any initiative.

10.1Psychological safety

Psychological safety, a term from Amy Edmondson's research on hospital and work teams (1999), is a shared belief in a team that it is safe to take interpersonal risks: to ask a question that might sound naive, to admit a mistake, to disagree with a senior person, to report a problem. Google's Project Aristotle, a study of 180 teams published in 2015, found it the most important of five factors that distinguished effective teams, ahead of dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact.

It is not the same as being nice or avoiding conflict. A psychologically safe team argues openly about designs and holds high standards, precisely because people are not afraid that disagreement or failure will be held against them personally. Managers build it mainly through their own responses: thanking people for reporting problems, especially their own mistakes; asking for dissent explicitly ("what is wrong with this plan?") and responding to it with curiosity; admitting their own errors; and running postmortems that look for causes in the system rather than for someone to blame (Senior engineering 10.4). A single public reprimand for a mistake undoes months of this.

10.2Conflict

Conflict about tasks (which design, which priority) is normal and healthy in a good team; conflict between people (mistrust, resentment, personal criticism) is corrosive and spreads. The two often mix: a disagreement about a design becomes personal when one side feels repeatedly overruled or disrespected. The manager's job is to keep task conflict productive (a clear decision process, so that disagreements end in a decision; decision records so that settled questions stay settled) and to deal with relationship conflict early.

When two engineers are in a conflict that they cannot resolve, a reliable sequence is: talk to each separately and listen, without taking sides, to understand their view and what they need; look for the structural cause (unclear ownership of an area is the most common one; competition for the same opportunity is another); bring them together with a specific, shared goal for the conversation; agree on concrete changes in behaviour or in structure; and follow up. If one person's behaviour is the problem (disrespect, personal attacks), it is a performance issue and handled as one, regardless of how strong they are technically. Teams watch closely whether "brilliant jerks" are tolerated.

10.3Burnout and sustainable pace

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 classification describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon (not a medical condition) resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three dimensions: exhaustion, increased mental distance from or cynicism about one's job, and reduced professional efficacy. It develops over months, and engineers who care most about their work are often most at risk.

The manager's levers are workload and control: keeping the sustained load realistic (crunch periods are sometimes necessary, but they must be short, rare and followed by recovery), sharing on-call fairly and keeping its load low by fixing the causes of pages, protecting holidays, and giving people control over how they do their work. Watch the signals: someone who always answered quickly and now does not, a rise in cynical comments, more mistakes in someone usually careful. And model the behaviour: a manager who sends messages at midnight teaches the team that midnight is a working hour, whatever they say.

10.4Remote and multi-site teams

Distributed teams work well when the work is designed for them and badly when an office-based way of working is simply stretched across sites. The scarce resource is overlap: the hours when people at all sites are working at the same time. At Arden in winter, Amsterdam and Munich share a time zone, the labelling vendor in Pune overlaps with them for four and a half hours, and a partner team in San Jose has no overlap at all with a nine-to-six day in Amsterdam unless someone shifts their hours.

Several practices follow. Default to written communication (design documents, decision records, written updates), so that people at other sites are not dependent on conversations they missed. Use overlap hours for discussions and decisions rather than status. Rotate the inconvenient meeting times so that the same site does not always take the early or late call. Make sure each site owns whole pieces of work (Section 3.4) rather than handing work across time zones every day. And watch for the "headquarters effect": people at the main site get more visibility, more interesting projects and faster promotions, simply because they are seen. A manager has to compensate deliberately, by travelling to the other sites, by making sure their work is presented by them, and by checking promotion and project data by site.

10.5Reorganisations and change

Reorganisations are sometimes necessary: when the structure no longer fits the work (Arden's split in Section 3.3), when the company's strategy changes, or when teams have grown or shrunk. They are also costly: every reorganisation interrupts relationships, ownership and focus for a period, and frequent ones teach people that the structure is temporary and not worth investing in. A good reorganisation solves a specific, explainable problem; a bad one solves the problem of a new leader wanting to make their mark.

When a reorganisation is necessary, the sequence matters. Decide the design with a small group, based on the work and the interfaces, before discussing names; then decide who goes where, talking to the most affected people individually before any announcement; announce to everyone at once, with the reasons, what changes and what does not; then hold one-to-ones and small-group conversations to answer questions; and finally, over the following weeks, fix the details that the design missed. People mainly want to know three things: what happens to me, why, and whether this is the last change for a while. Answer those first.

Pitfall

Leaking a reorganisation in pieces. If the plan circulates as rumours for weeks before the announcement, people fill the gaps with the worst interpretations and the best people start talking to recruiters. Keep the group that knows small, the time between decision and announcement short, and the announcement complete.

11Engineering-manager interviews

Interviews for engineering-manager roles test whether a candidate has done the job described in this chapter, and how they think about it. They mix behavioural questions about real situations with discussions of people, delivery, hiring and organisation, and usually include a technical round to check that the candidate can lead technical people credibly.

11.1What the loop tests

A typical engineering-manager loop has five to six interviews: people management (growing people, performance, conflict), execution and delivery (planning, prioritisation, cross-team work, handling slips), hiring and team building, technical depth (a system-design discussion or a deep dive into a past technical decision, at the level of judgement rather than coding speed), a cross-functional interview with a product manager or another partner, and a conversation with the hiring manager about motivation and fit with the role. For managers of ML teams, the technical round often asks how the candidate would evaluate, ship and monitor models, which Chapter 8 covers.

The interviewers look for evidence of the things this chapter describes: whether the candidate thinks of their output as the team's output, whether they act early on problems, whether they can describe concrete situations with specific actions and results, and whether they learned from what went wrong. The STAR format and the idea of a prepared story bank are described from the candidate's side in Senior engineering 15; for manager roles, the stories need to show decisions about people and organisation, not only technical leadership.

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

What is a manager's output, in Grove's definition, and what follows from it?Basic

The output of the organisation under the manager plus the neighbouring organisations they influence. It follows that a good day can contain no work of one's own, that the manager should choose activities by their leverage (how many people they affect, for how long, at what moment), and that staying the team's strongest individual contributor caps the team at what one person can review and decide.

How do a tech lead, a tech lead manager, an engineering manager and a manager of managers differ?Basic

A tech lead owns technical direction without formal authority over people. A tech lead manager does both jobs for a small team of three to five, which breaks as the team grows. An engineering manager owns people, hiring, growth, performance and delivery for one team of about five to eight and works with a tech lead. A manager of managers owns two to five managers and the structure, standards and budget of a group of 20 to 60.

What would you do in your first 90 days as a new manager of an existing team?Basic

Listen first: long one-to-ones with everyone using the same questions, conversations with peers and product, reading plans, incidents and designs. From about week three, write a diagnosis of people, delivery, technical health and relationships, and test it with the tech lead and my manager. Deliver one small, visible improvement people asked for. By day 90 agree a plan with priorities, hiring and the one or two structural changes. Avoid early judgements of people and promises to individuals.

What goes into a role definition (scorecard) before you start hiring?Basic

The mission and the outcomes expected after twelve months, the four to six competencies with what good looks like at the level, and the level itself. It aligns the interviewers, drives the job ad and the loop design, and later becomes the new hire's goals. Hiring for "senior or staff, we'll see" interviews the same person against two bars.

What makes an interview structured, and why does it matter?Basic

Every candidate gets the same core questions, answers are scored against a predefined rubric, and the evidence is written down. Structured interviews predict job performance markedly better than unstructured conversations (validity about 0.42 against 0.19 in Sackett et al. 2022) and leave less room for affinity, halo and confirmation bias.

Why should interviewers write their feedback before the debrief?Intermediate

Because discussion first makes the judgements correlated: the first confident speaker anchors everyone, and four interviewers carry little more information than one or two. In the chapter's model, four structured interviews with independent feedback reach a panel validity of 0.68; with an error correlation of 0.5 they act like 1.6 interviews and reach 0.51, and the share of strong hires drops from 59 % to 48 %.

How do you design an interview loop for a senior perception engineer?Intermediate

Start from the scorecard's competencies and draw a matrix of competencies against interviews: each competency is the primary focus of one interview and is seen from a second angle in another, and no interview carries more than three. Use exercises that resemble the work (a real recall drop to diagnose, an evaluation pipeline to design), give every interview a rubric, and train interviewers by shadowing.

What is the trade-off when you raise the hiring bar?Intermediate

A higher bar raises the share of hires who are strong but rejects more strong candidates and lengthens the search. In the model, offers to the top 10 % instead of 25 % raise strong hires from 59 % to 74 % but reject 70 % of strong candidates instead of 41 %. Most companies accept false negatives to avoid expensive false positives, but the hiring manager must weigh the cost of an unfilled role openly rather than lowering the bar quietly.

What does good onboarding achieve in the first week, month and quarter?Basic

Week one: a working environment, a small change in production, and knowing who to ask. Month one: enough understanding of the system for a normal piece of work. Quarter one: ownership of an area. The manager writes the 30-60-90 day goals before the start, assigns a peer buddy, holds weekly one-to-ones and asks the new person what was missing, then fixes it.

When and how do you split a team?Intermediate

When it outgrows about eight to ten people or owns more than it can understand: long standups, planning as internal negotiation, on-call over unknown areas, too many one-to-ones. Split along a seam that already exists in the work so that the new teams need to talk less than before, give each team someone who can make decisions alone, let people state preferences, and split before the crisis rather than during it.

What is cognitive load in the Team Topologies sense, and how does a manager use it?Intermediate

The amount a team has to understand to own its scope safely. When it is too high, estimates degrade, incidents happen in areas nobody knows and onboarding slows. The manager uses it as a design input: give the team less to own by splitting it or by moving scope into a platform with a self-service interface, and draw boundaries around products, pipeline stages or customers rather than technologies.

What is a one-to-one for, and what are the signs it is not working?Basic

It is the engineer's private time for concerns, obstacles, growth and two-way feedback. Signs it is not working: it is mostly a ticket status, the manager cancels it often, the engineer never brings topics, or problems first surface in a resignation. A shared running document and a few standing questions help when the engineer has nothing prepared.

How do you write a review paragraph that is useful?Intermediate

State the outcome and impact, the person's specific contribution and the scope, with numbers where possible ("raised night recall from 0.84 to 0.90 by fixing the labelling guideline"), rather than activities or personality ("worked on", "great team player"). Write evidence first, then place it against the level guide; describe development needs as observable behaviour with a concrete next step.

What are your two jobs in a calibration meeting?Intermediate

To advocate for my people with concise evidence, and to help apply one standard across all teams, including mine. Presenting everyone as exceptional costs credibility; challenges from other managers are information about missing evidence or a miscalibrated bar. Afterwards I own the rating as mine in front of the engineer.

Walk through how you handle an engineer who is not meeting expectations.Intermediate

Diagnose in the order expectations, skill, context, motivation. Have a clear private conversation with specific examples and ask for their view. Agree written goals and a four to six week support plan with weekly check-ins and real help. At the decision point, either close the plan explicitly or move, with HR, to a formal improvement plan with measurable goals and a fixed end, followed by success or a respectful exit. Act at the first signs, not after months.

What is a skip-level and what are its rules?Basic

A conversation between a manager of managers and the reports of their reports, individually or in small groups, every few months. It samples how the team sees its work, its manager and the organisation. Rules: managers know it happens, it makes no decisions about the team's work, and themes are shared with the manager without attributing them to individuals.

What should be consistent across teams, and what should be left to them?Intermediate

Consistent: the hiring bar and process, the meaning of levels and ratings, on-call expectations, incident handling, security and safety practice, and interfaces between teams. Local: sprint mechanics, rituals, internal tools, how work is split. Standardise outcomes and interfaces, not methods.

Why does hiring reduce a team's capacity at first?Intermediate

Existing engineers spend time interviewing (about 55 hours per hire at Arden) and then mentoring, while new hires start after their notice period at near-zero productivity and ramp over months. In the chapter's model, 1.5 hires a month for six months take a team of six to about 5.1 engineer-equivalents first and catch up with the lost output around month six, or month eight with a three-month notice period. Hiring helps next year, not this quarter.

What does fully loaded cost include, and what extra budget do ML teams need?Basic

Salary plus employer social contributions, benefits, equipment, office, licences, training and travel, typically 25 to 50 % on top in Europe. ML teams also need compute for training and evaluation, storage and labelling, which can be of the same order as a platform team's salaries.

What is an operating cadence and what belongs in each forum?Intermediate

The recurring forums through which a group plans and adjusts. Weekly: a staff meeting for cross-team problems, people, hiring and decisions (status goes into a written summary), plus one-to-ones. Every two weeks: sprint reviews and demos. Monthly: metrics review and skip-levels. Quarterly: kick-off, review and planning starting about three weeks before quarter end. Each forum answers one question at one horizon; prune the rest.

What gates would you put between an ML prototype and a release?Advanced

A timeboxed exploration with a written result; feasibility: beats the baseline on the frozen evaluation set by a margin agreed in advance; prototype: fits the latency and memory budget on target hardware with the full pipeline, reproducible training, no slice worse; shadow: disagreements with the current model reviewed and the field effect estimated; staged rollout with guardrail metrics at each step. Any gate can send the work back or stop it, and stopping is a valid result.

Which metrics does a perception manager report, and how do you keep them honest?Advanced

Quality on the frozen evaluation set per slice and regressions caught; field quality in product terms (for example false warnings per 1,000 driving hours, share of real events detected); speed of improvement (time from field failure to fixed model in the fleet, evaluated experiments per week); cost (compute, labelling, storage). Keep them as system measures, not individual targets, and pair each with one that catches its abuse, such as benchmark with field quality.

How do you prepare a team for a technical meeting with a customer?Intermediate

Agree the purpose, the likely questions, who answers what, which questions get a written follow-up instead of an answer on the spot, and who writes the minutes. Keep technical discussion open but route commitments on scope, dates and liability through the project lead and the change process; confirm commitments in writing afterwards and put them into the plan.

What is psychological safety, and how does a manager build it?Basic

A shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks: ask, admit mistakes, disagree, report problems (Edmondson 1999). It is not avoidance of conflict. A manager builds it through their reactions: thanking people for bad news, asking for dissent, admitting their own errors and running blameless postmortems; one public reprimand undoes months.

How do you make a multi-site team work?Intermediate

Give each site whole ownership, default to writing, use overlap hours for discussion and decisions, rotate inconvenient meeting times, travel, have each site present its own work, and check projects and promotions by site to counter the headquarters effect. Reach critical mass at a new site quickly with a senior anchor.

Q.2Why and what if

Why do companies prefer to reject some good candidates rather than hire a weak one?Why

Because the costs are asymmetric. A rejected good candidate costs a longer search; a weak hire costs months of their salary, the team's time in reviews and support, a performance process, damage to trust in the hiring bar and often a second search. The asymmetry is weaker when a role is critical and the search is long, which is why the hiring manager must weigh it explicitly.

Why do more interviews not fix a weak process?Why

Because averaging helps only with independent noise. Interviews of low validity add little each, and correlated interviews (same questions, shared impressions, discussion before writing) add almost nothing. In the model four unstructured interviews reach a panel validity of 0.36, below a single pair of independent structured ones. Fix what each interview measures and keep judgements independent first.

Why should a new manager avoid changing the process in the first weeks?Why

Because they do not yet know why things are the way they are, what works, and who is affected. Early changes based on a previous job often break something that worked and cost trust that the larger later changes need. A small, requested fix after listening shows action without that risk.

Why is a forced rating distribution a problem?Why

It assumes every team has the same spread of performance. A small, strong team must rate a good engineer low, and a weak team can hide behind its quota. It turns calibration into negotiation about who gives someone up, and it discourages managers from hiring strong people into strong teams.

Why do many perception groups build a data and ML platform team early?Why

Because data, labelling, evaluation and training infrastructure usually become the bottleneck before modelling ideas do, and model engineers do that work badly in their spare time. A platform team with a self-service interface makes evaluation trustworthy and fast for everyone, and gives labelling and compute a real owner.

What if your best engineer wants to become a manager but you doubt they will be good at it?What if

Find out by doing, at low risk: give them parts of the job (running interviews and debriefs, onboarding a new hire, leading a small project with others, mentoring) and talk about how it felt. If they enjoy it and show the signs, support them with clear expectations, coaching and a peer group. Agree in advance that stepping back to engineering is a normal outcome without loss of face or pay.

What if the leadership asks you to deliver a large customer commitment in three months by hiring five more people?What if

Explain with numbers that hiring lowers capacity for the first months: interviewing, notice periods and ramp-up mean the five people contribute little before the date, and the existing team loses time to interviews and mentoring. Offer real options: reduce or phase the scope, move the date, borrow experienced people from a neighbouring team who know the system, or accept a defined risk. Hire anyway if the work continues next year, but not as the plan for this date.

What if a technically brilliant engineer is disrespectful to colleagues?What if

Treat it as a performance issue like any other: specific examples, the impact on the team, a clear expectation, follow-up, and consequences if it does not change. The team is watching whether the bar applies to strong engineers; tolerating the behaviour costs more in other people's output and attrition than the individual's brilliance adds.

What if a manager reporting to you is avoiding a difficult performance conversation?What if

Coach rather than take over: help them prepare the examples and the opening, role-play it, agree when it will happen, and debrief afterwards. Taking the conversation yourself undermines them and teaches the team to go around them. Make it explicit that acting on performance is part of their job and that you will ask about it in your one-to-ones.

What if a promising ML prototype fails the latency budget on the target hardware?What if

That is what the prototype gate is for. Options: a smaller or quantised variant and a re-measured trade-off, moving part of the work elsewhere in the pipeline, a different hardware budget negotiated with the product and the customer, or stopping with a written lesson. The wrong answer is to keep developing it in the hope that the budget will move later.

What if your team's metric looks great but the customer is unhappy?What if

The metric measures something other than what the customer experiences, or it has become a target and been optimised directly. Look at field data and specific cases the customer raises, compare them with the evaluation slices, and add or change slices and field measures so that the reported number tracks what matters. Tell the customer what you found and what you are changing.

What if you open a site in Germany and want to introduce a new productivity dashboard?What if

If there is a works council, a system suitable for monitoring employees' behaviour or performance needs its co-determination (§ 87(1) no. 6 BetrVG), usually through a works agreement, and that applies to many dashboards and some developer tools. Involve HR and the works council early, explain the purpose, aggregate at team level and avoid individual performance views. The same restraint is good practice anywhere, because individual productivity numbers are easily misused.

GGlossary

Managerial leverage
Grove's measure of how much an activity of a manager changes the output of their organisation; high for decisions that affect many people, last long, or come at the right moment.
Tech lead manager (TLM)
A hybrid role in which one person is both the technical lead and the people manager of a small team.
Span of control
The number of direct reports a manager has; five to eight is the usual range for a first-line engineering manager.
Scorecard (role definition)
A written description of a role's mission, outcomes after a year, competencies and level, agreed before hiring starts.
Interview loop
The set of interviews a candidate goes through before the hiring decision, usually four to six.
Structured interview
An interview with the same core questions for every candidate, scored against a predefined rubric with written evidence.
Rubric
A written description of weak, adequate, strong and exceptional answers for each competency an interview measures.
Predictive validity
The correlation between the score of a selection method and later job performance.
Affinity bias
The tendency to rate people who resemble us more highly.
Halo effect
One strong impression colouring the judgement of unrelated qualities.
Debrief
The meeting after an interview loop where the panel combines its written feedback into a hire or no-hire decision.
Bar raiser
A trained interviewer from outside the hiring team, with a veto, whose task is to keep the hiring bar consistent.
Hiring committee
A group that makes hiring decisions from written interview packets without having met the candidate.
Onboarding buddy
A peer assigned to answer a new hire's everyday questions during their first months.
Bus factor
The number of people who could leave before nobody understands an area; one is a risk.
Cognitive load (team)
How much a team has to understand to own its scope safely; a Team Topologies design constraint.
Skills matrix
A table of a team's areas against its people showing who can work on and teach each area.
One-to-one
A regular private conversation between a manager and a direct report, owned by the report.
Stay conversation
A conversation about what keeps someone in their job and what would make them leave, held before any resignation.
Regretted attrition
Departures of people the team would have wanted to keep.
Glue work
Necessary, often invisible work that keeps a team functioning (onboarding, coordination, documentation), often undervalued in reviews.
Calibration
A meeting in which managers compare proposed ratings across teams so that the same rating means the same thing.
Recency effect
Over-weighting recent events when judging a longer period, a common distortion in reviews.
Performance improvement plan (PIP)
A written, time-limited plan with measurable goals, support and stated consequences for an employee below the expected level.
Skip-level
A conversation between a manager and the reports of their direct reports.
Headcount plan
The planned number of people per team and role over a period, with its business case.
Fully loaded cost
The total cost of an employee to the company: salary plus contributions, benefits, equipment, space and other overheads.
Operating cadence
The set of recurring forums through which a group plans, reviews and adjusts its work.
OKR
Objectives and key results: a few qualitative goals for a period, each with measurable results.
Timebox
A fixed amount of time for a piece of work whose scope adapts, used for research questions.
Stage gate
A decision point between stages of a project where defined evidence is required to continue.
Psychological safety
A shared belief in a team that it is safe to take interpersonal risks such as asking, admitting mistakes and disagreeing.
Burnout
In ICD-11, an occupational phenomenon from unmanaged chronic workplace stress: exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism, reduced efficacy.
Works council (Betriebsrat)
An elected body representing employees of an establishment in Germany, with information, consultation and co-determination rights under the Works Constitution Act.
Termination agreement (Aufhebungsvertrag)
A mutual agreement in Germany to end employment, often with severance, used instead of a contested dismissal.
