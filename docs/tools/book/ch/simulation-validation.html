Simulation, ROS and virtual validation

How driving software is tested before, beside and after the real car: ROS 2 and DDS as the plumbing of prototypes and simulators; what a driving simulator is made of; how scenarios are written down with the ASAM standards; sensor, vehicle and traffic models; synthetic data and the sim-to-real gap; software-, hardware- and vehicle-in-the-loop setups; replay and determinism; and how thousands of scenario runs become a pass/fail verdict in CI. One scenario runs through the whole chapter: a child steps out from behind a parked van in front of a car whose emergency braking (AEB) is the software under test.

1The big picture: why driving software is tested in simulation

A car cannot be driven enough kilometres to prove that its automated functions are safe, and the dangerous situations are exactly the ones nobody wants to stage on a real road. Simulation exists to make rare, dangerous and repeatable situations cheap. Everything else in this chapter is about making those simulated situations trustworthy.

1.1Why kilometres alone cannot prove safety

The classic argument comes from a RAND study (Kalra and Paddock, 2016). Human drivers in the US caused about 1.09 fatalities per 100 million miles. To show with 95% confidence that an automated vehicle is at least that good, assuming it never has a fatal crash during the test, it would have to drive about 275 million miles. The number comes from the "rule of three": if zero events are observed in n trials, the 95% upper bound on the event rate is roughly 3/n, so n = 3 / 1.09×10−8 ≈ 275 million. A fleet of 100 cars driving around the clock at 40 km/h would need more than ten years, and any software change would, strictly, restart the count.

Emergency braking is a smaller problem than full automation, but it has the same shape. The situations that matter, such as a child running out from behind a parked van, are rare in normal driving, impossible to repeat exactly, and unethical to provoke with a real child. A test track with a dummy pedestrian on a motorised platform can repeat a few dozen variants per day. A simulator can run tens of thousands per night, at any speed, in any weather, with a recorded ground truth of where every object really was.

So the industry splits the evidence. Simulation explores the space of situations widely and finds the weak spots; test-track runs confirm the simulation on a small number of points; real-world driving and open-loop replay of recorded drives find the situations nobody thought of and count false activations. Regulators have adopted the same split: the UNECE "multi-pillar" approach (often called NATM) and the EU regulation for automated driving systems (2022/1426) expect audits, virtual testing, track testing and real-world testing together, and they expect the virtual toolchain itself to be shown credible.

Why it matters

Simulation is not a cheaper version of road testing; it answers a different question. Road testing answers "what happens in the situations we happen to meet". Simulation answers "what happens in the situations we choose", including the ones we must never meet for real. The value of a simulator is therefore only as high as the quality of the chosen situations and the realism of the models.

1.2The running example: a child behind a parked van

The scenario followed through this chapter is a classic of pedestrian AEB testing (Euro NCAP has a version of it with a child target walking out from behind parked cars). A car drives at 40 km/h along an urban street. A 5.2 m van is parked in the parking lane on the right. A child stands just in front of the van, invisible to the car's front camera because the van is in the way, and steps out into the road when the car is 23 m away. If nothing brakes, the car hits the child in the middle of its bumper.

Figure 1.1 shows the whole chain that turns this sentence into a verdict. A scenario file in ASAM OpenSCENARIO says where the actors start and what triggers the child to walk; an OpenDRIVE file describes the road. The scenario engine inside the simulator executes that file. The simulator world holds the state of every actor and steps physics forward a few milliseconds at a time. Sensor models turn the world into what the car's sensors would deliver: a rendered camera image in which the van hides the child, radar detections, lidar points. The software under test (SUT) sees only those sensor outputs, exactly as in a car: perception finds the child, a tracker confirms it and estimates its velocity, the AEB function decides to brake. The brake command goes into a vehicle model, the car slows down, and the next sensor frame is rendered from the new position. That feedback is what "closed loop" means. Outside the loop an evaluator compares what happened with what should have happened and reports a verdict to CI.

Each box can be replaced by something more real, and that is how the X-in-the-loop levels arise. Replace the software under test by the real ECU and the sensor models by injected video, and you have hardware-in-the-loop (HIL). Replace the vehicle model by a real car on a test track with virtual pedestrians projected into its perception, and you have vehicle-in-the-loop (VIL). The structure stays; what changes is which parts are models and how fast the loop can run.

2ROS 2: the plumbing of prototypes and simulators

Almost every simulator, research stack and robotics prototype speaks ROS 2. It is not an operating system and rarely runs in a series-production ECU, but it is the common language between simulators, recorders, visualisation tools and the software under test. Knowing its graph model, its middleware and especially its quality-of-service rules saves days of "why does my node receive nothing".

2.1Why ROS exists and where it is used

The Robot Operating System started in 2007 at Willow Garage to stop every robotics lab from rewriting the same drivers, message formats, visualisation and logging tools. ROS 1 was a set of conventions plus a central master process that told nodes where to find each other. It worked well in labs and badly in products: the master was a single point of failure, there was no real-time story, no security and no control over delivery guarantees. ROS 2 (first release 2017) kept the programming model and replaced the transport by an industrial middleware standard, DDS, which brought decentralised discovery, configurable delivery guarantees and a path to security.

ROS 2 ships as distributions released every May. Even years bring a long-term-support (LTS) release with five years of support: Humble Hawksbill (2022), Jazzy Jalisco (2024) and Lyrical Luth (2026). Most automotive users sit on an LTS release, because their simulator bridge, drivers and their own code must all agree on one distribution.

In automotive work ROS 2 appears in four places: research and pre-development stacks (the open-source Autoware stack is built on it); the interface of simulators (CARLA, Gazebo, Isaac Sim and most commercial tools have ROS 2 bridges); data recording and replay (rosbag2); and tooling such as visualisation (RViz, Foxglove). In series ECUs the same roles are filled by AUTOSAR Adaptive, vendor middleware or plain DDS; a few vendors sell safety-certified ROS 2 derivatives (Apex.AI's SDK is the best known). Bridging ROS 2 to AUTOSAR is covered in AUTOSAR 12.3.

2.2The graph: nodes, topics, services, actions and parameters

A ROS 2 system is a graph. A node is a unit of computation with a name, such as /detector. Nodes talk in four ways, and choosing the right one is most of ROS 2 design.

A topic is a named, typed stream of messages with any number of publishers and subscribers who do not know about each other. Sensor data, detections, tracks and brake commands are topics: they flow continuously and the producer should not wait for anyone. A service is a request/response call, like a function call across processes: "load this scenario", "reset the simulation". It is fine for short operations and wrong for anything that takes time, because the caller blocks or polls. An action is for long-running goals: a client sends a goal ("run scenario 17 with seed 4"), the server streams feedback ("sim time 6.2 s") and finally returns a result ("pass"), and the client can cancel in between. Under the hood an action is a few services and topics bundled together. Parameters are per-node typed settings (use_sim_time, a detection threshold) that can be read and changed at runtime through services every node offers.

Messages are defined in small interface files (.msg, .srv, .action) and generated into C++ and Python types. Standard packages define the common ones: sensor_msgs/Image, sensor_msgs/PointCloud2, geometry_msgs/PoseStamped, nav_msgs/Odometry. Every message with a std_msgs/Header carries a time stamp and a coordinate frame name, and the convention that the stamp is the time of measurement, not of publication, is what makes replay and fusion possible later.

Nodes are started and wired by launch files (Python, XML or YAML), which set parameters, remap topic names and group nodes into processes. A useful detail for simulation is composition: several nodes loaded as components into one process can pass messages as pointers (intra-process communication), avoiding serialisation for large images.

Pitfall

Using a service for something that takes seconds, such as "drive to this point" or "run this scenario". The caller has no progress, no cancel and no timeout semantics, and a crashed server leaves it hanging. Long operations are actions; continuous data is topics; services are for short, idempotent requests.

2.3Under the hood: client libraries, the RMW layer and DDS

Figure 2.2 shows the layers. Your code uses a client library, rclcpp or rclpy. Both sit on rcl, a C library with the logic every language needs (names, parameters, time). Below it is the RMW interface (ROS middleware), a thin C API with functions such as "create publisher" and "take message". Each middleware implements it: rmw_fastrtps_cpp (eProsima Fast DDS, the default in recent distributions), rmw_cyclonedds_cpp (Eclipse Cyclone DDS), rmw_connextdds (RTI Connext) and, since 2025, the tier-1 rmw_zenoh_cpp based on Zenoh instead of DDS. The environment variable RMW_IMPLEMENTATION picks one per process, and two processes with different DDS vendors can usually talk, because DDS defines a common wire protocol, RTPS.

Discovery is decentralised. When a process starts, its DDS participant announces itself by UDP multicast (Simple Participant Discovery Protocol, SPDP) on a port derived from the domain id (7400 for domain 0); participants then exchange the lists of their readers and writers with topic names, types and QoS (Simple Endpoint Discovery Protocol, SEDP). A reader and a writer with the same topic, compatible type and compatible QoS are matched, and data flows directly between them. ROS_DOMAIN_ID separates systems on one network; on Linux, ids 0 to 101 are safe because higher ids collide with the ephemeral port range.

The executor is the part of the client library that waits for events and calls your callbacks. A single-threaded executor runs one callback at a time, which makes the node easy to reason about and deterministic for a given input order. A multi-threaded executor runs callbacks in parallel, and callback groups decide which may overlap: callbacks in a mutually exclusive group never run together; callbacks in a reentrant group may. Many surprises in timing ("my timer is late whenever images arrive") are executor configuration, not DDS.

Interesting detail

Discovery traffic grows roughly with the square of the number of participants, because every participant tells every other one about all its endpoints. A simulation with 200 nodes in 200 processes on one machine can spend seconds in discovery and noticeable CPU afterwards. Composing nodes into fewer processes, using a discovery server (Fast DDS) or switching to Zenoh, whose router avoids the all-to-all exchange, are the usual fixes.

2.4Quality of service: the contract between publisher and subscriber

DDS lets each writer and reader state what it offers and what it needs, through QoS policies. ROS 2 exposes the most important ones. Reliability: reliable retransmits lost samples, best effort does not. History and depth: keep last N keeps only the N newest samples per instance, keep all keeps everything until resource limits. Durability: volatile delivers only samples written after the reader joined; transient local lets the writer keep its last samples and hand them to late joiners. Deadline states the maximum expected period between samples, lifespan how long a sample stays valid, liveliness and its lease duration how a writer proves it is still alive.

Matching follows a "requested versus offered" rule: a reader may not ask for more than the writer offers. A reliable reader does not match a best-effort writer; a transient-local reader does not match a volatile writer. The reverse combinations are fine. Nothing fails loudly: the endpoints simply never match, and an "incompatible QoS" event is raised that many nodes never listen to. Two lessons follow. First, the classic failure: a camera driver publishes images with the "sensor data" profile (best effort, keep last 5), and a tool subscribes with the default profile (reliable, keep last 10). They never match, the tool shows no data, and only ros2 topic info -v reveals the mismatch. Second, defaults hide in more places than the ROS profile: in raw Cyclone DDS a transient-local writer hands late joiners only its last sample unless the durability-service history is raised; ROS 2's RMW layer sets that history from the depth for you, which is why the same code behaves differently when written against raw DDS.

from rclpy.qos import QoSProfile, ReliabilityPolicy, HistoryPolicy, DurabilityPolicy

# Camera frames: newest matters, a lost frame should not delay the next one.
SENSOR = QoSProfile(reliability=ReliabilityPolicy.BEST_EFFORT, history=HistoryPolicy.KEEP_LAST, depth=5)
# Brake command: every sample must arrive, in order, and late joiners should not get stale commands.
COMMAND = QoSProfile(reliability=ReliabilityPolicy.RELIABLE, history=HistoryPolicy.KEEP_LAST, depth=1,
                     durability=DurabilityPolicy.VOLATILE)
# Static configuration (like /tf_static or a calibration): late joiners must still get it.
LATCHED = QoSProfile(reliability=ReliabilityPolicy.RELIABLE, history=HistoryPolicy.KEEP_LAST, depth=1,
                     durability=DurabilityPolicy.TRANSIENT_LOCAL)
In practice

A short rule set covers most cases. Sensor streams: best effort, keep last a few, volatile. Commands and events: reliable, small depth. Configuration that must reach nodes started later (the static transform tree /tf_static, a map, calibration): reliable plus transient local. Subscribers in tools should request best effort, because a best-effort reader matches both kinds of writer.

Talk track

"ROS 2 sits on DDS, so every publisher and subscriber carries a QoS contract. They only connect if the reader asks for no more than the writer offers. Most 'no data' bugs are a reliable subscriber on a best-effort sensor topic, or a late joiner expecting data from a volatile writer."

2.5Time, transforms and bags

Simulated time is the feature that makes ROS 2 usable with simulators. Every node has a clock that can be system time or ROS time. If the parameter use_sim_time is true, the node's ROS time follows the /clock topic, which the simulator bridge publishes, and timers fire in simulated time. When the simulator runs at a quarter of real time because rendering eight cameras is slow, the software under test still sees a 30 Hz camera and a 100 ms timeout lasts 100 simulated milliseconds. Forgetting use_sim_time on one node is a classic bug: its time stamps are wall-clock, the message filters in the tracker drop its messages as "too old", and nothing crashes.

tf2 keeps the tree of coordinate frames (map, odom, base_link, camera_front) with time-stamped transforms. Static transforms (sensor mounting) are published once on /tf_static with transient-local durability, which is exactly the late-joiner case of Table 2.1; moving transforms stream on /tf. A node asks "where was the child, in base_link, at the time this image was taken", and tf2 interpolates.

rosbag2 records topics with their time stamps and replays them. Since the Iron release its default storage is MCAP, an open, indexed container format that also serves other ecosystems; before that it was SQLite. For this chapter, the closed-loop run of Chapter 10 was written as a real rosbag2 with the rosbags library: 3.59 s of simulated time, 496 messages on four topics (/clock and /ego/odom at 50 Hz in the log, /perception/child, /aeb/brake_request), 167 KB in one .mcap file plus a metadata.yaml. The commands that matter in daily work are short:

ros2 bag record -s mcap -o run_40kmh /clock /tf /tf_static /perception/objects /tracks /aeb/brake_request
ros2 bag info run_40kmh                    # topics, types, counts, duration
ros2 bag play run_40kmh --clock            # replay and publish /clock from the bag's time line
ros2 topic info -v /tracks                 # publishers, subscribers and their QoS: the first check for "no data"
Pitfall

Replaying a bag without --clock into nodes with use_sim_time true, or with --clock into nodes with it false. In the first case the nodes' time never moves; in the second, message stamps are hours in the past compared with the nodes' wall clock. Both look like "the tracker ignores everything".

2.6Performance, determinism and ROS 2 in products

Three mechanisms keep large data cheap. Intra-process communication passes a pointer when publisher and subscriber share a process. Loaned messages let the middleware hand the publisher a buffer in shared memory, so a 12 MB image is written once and read in place (Fast DDS data sharing, Cyclone DDS with iceoryx). Type adaptation lets a node publish its own image type and convert only when a message leaves the process. Without these, every subscriber of a camera topic costs a serialisation and a copy.

ROS 2 is not deterministic by default. Callbacks run when messages arrive, messages on different topics arrive in no guaranteed order, and a multi-threaded executor adds scheduling freedom. For simulation this matters a great deal (Chapter 9): the same scenario can give different results if the order of "image arrives" and "timer fires" changes. Deterministic setups use simulated time, single-threaded executors or explicit synchronisation, and a simulator that waits for the software before advancing.

For production, ROS 2 brings a large ecosystem but no safety certification, a heap-allocating API and dynamic discovery. Teams either use it only off-board (tools, simulation, data), wrap the certified parts behind an AUTOSAR or vendor middleware (AUTOSAR 12.3), or buy a certified derivative. The practical lesson for a camera ECU team is to keep the algorithm code independent of ROS (plain C++ libraries with thin ROS nodes around them), so the same code runs in the simulator with ROS and in the ECU without it.

Why it matters

The thin-wrapper rule is what makes software-in-the-loop honest. If the perception library itself includes ROS headers, the simulated build and the ECU build are different code, and a pass in simulation says little about the ECU.

3Driving simulators: what they are made of and which to choose

A driving simulator is a game engine plus everything a game does not need: exact time control, ground truth for every object, sensor models instead of a single pretty camera, scenario execution and interfaces to the software under test. The tools differ in fidelity, licence and interfaces much more than in structure.

3.1The parts of a simulator

Figure 3.1 shows the common structure. A time master owns simulated time. In each step it lets the scenario engine check its triggers and start actions (the child starts walking), updates the world state (poses of all actors, traffic lights, weather), lets physics and agents move everything (vehicle dynamics, collision detection, traffic and pedestrian behaviour), and then asks the sensor simulation to produce the measurements of this instant: rendered images on the GPU, ray-cast lidar points, radar detections. Bridges carry sensor data to the software under test and its commands back. A recorder keeps the ground truth of every actor in every step, which is the raw material of every verdict.

The design decisions that separate tools are: how good the rendering and sensor models are (Section 5); whether the simulator can run in synchronous (lockstep) mode, where it waits for the client, or only free-running; which scenario formats it executes natively; which interfaces it offers (ROS 2, ASAM OSI, FMI, a proprietary API); how it scales to thousands of parallel runs in the cloud; and whether its vendor will support a tool qualification argument for safety work.

3.2CARLA: the open-source reference

CARLA (since 2017, MIT-licensed code, CC-BY assets) is built on Unreal Engine: the 0.9 series on UE4 and, since version 0.10 (December 2024), on UE5. It runs as a server that owns the world and renders sensors; clients connect over TCP through a Python or C++ API, spawn actors, attach sensors and read data. A Traffic Manager drives background vehicles, ScenarioRunner executes scenarios written in Python or in OpenSCENARIO 1.x, a ROS bridge exposes everything as ROS topics, and a co-simulation bridge to SUMO adds realistic traffic. Its strengths are a large community, reasonable camera realism and free access; its weaknesses are radar realism, the effort of building new maps and the lack of a qualification story.

The most important setting is synchronous mode with a fixed time step. Without it, the server runs as fast as it can and the client sees whatever frame is current when it asks, so results depend on the speed of the machine. How the rendered camera relates to a real one, and how CARLA and Blender differ, is in Rasterization versus path tracing 7.

import carla

client = carla.Client("localhost", 2000)
world = client.get_world()
settings = world.get_settings()
settings.synchronous_mode = True          # the server waits for world.tick()
settings.fixed_delta_seconds = 0.05       # every tick advances exactly 50 ms of simulated time
world.apply_settings(settings)
client.get_trafficmanager(8000).set_synchronous_mode(True)   # background traffic must follow the same clock

for step in range(300):                   # 15 s of simulated time
    world.tick()                          # physics, agents and all sensors advance one step
    # ... read sensor queues for this frame, run or wait for the software under test, apply its control ...
Pitfall

Physics sub-stepping. With a 50 ms rendering step, CARLA splits physics into sub-steps (by default at most 10 ms each). If fixed_delta_seconds is larger than the sub-step limit allows, vehicle physics becomes unstable or the setting is rejected. A simulation that "works at 20 Hz but cars jump at 10 Hz" is usually this.

3.3Gazebo: robotics first

Gazebo comes from robotics. The original "Gazebo Classic" reached end of life in January 2025; its successor (first released as Ignition) is a set of libraries with releases such as Harmonic (2023, paired with ROS 2 Jazzy) and Jetty (2025). Worlds and robots are described in SDFormat; physics engines are pluggable (DART by default, Bullet, a simple "TPE" for kinematics); sensors include cameras, depth cameras, GPU lidar, IMU and contact sensors; and ros_gz bridges topics to ROS 2. Gazebo is excellent for robots, mobile platforms and anything with joints and contact, and weak for urban traffic, road networks and photorealistic camera data. In automotive work it appears in parking robots, logistics vehicles, agricultural machines and research on low-speed automation.

3.4NVIDIA Omniverse and Isaac Sim

NVIDIA's simulation tools are built on Omniverse, a platform whose scene description is OpenUSD (Universal Scene Description, originally from Pixar) and whose renderer uses RTX ray tracing. Isaac Sim is the robotics simulator on this platform, with physics (PhysX), ROS 2 bridges and RTX-based camera, lidar and radar models; Isaac Lab adds reinforcement learning. Omniverse Replicator is a framework for generating labelled synthetic data with domain randomisation (Section 7). For automotive, NVIDIA's DRIVE Sim was the AV-specific simulator; since 2025 NVIDIA has also positioned Omniverse "blueprints" for AV simulation and its Cosmos generative world models, which create or vary video data from recorded drives. The common thread is physically based ray-traced sensors and tight GPU integration; the price is hardware cost and dependence on one vendor's stack.

4Writing scenarios down: the ASAM standards

A scenario that lives in one tool's GUI cannot be reviewed, versioned, shared with a customer or run in another simulator. The ASAM standards give roads, scenarios, labels and simulation interfaces a common, text-based form. This chapter follows the running scenario from a sentence to a file that a simulator executes.

4.1Functional, logical and concrete scenarios

The German PEGASUS project (2016 to 2019) gave the vocabulary that most of the industry now uses, and ISO 34501 to 34505 build on it (Standards and regulations 10.2). A functional scenario is a sentence for people: "a child steps out from behind a parked car into the path of the ego vehicle in an urban street". A logical scenario turns it into parameters with ranges and, ideally, distributions: ego speed 10 to 60 km/h, child speed 3 to 10 km/h, van length, where on the bumper the impact would be, day or night. A concrete scenario fixes every parameter to one value and can be run: 40 km/h, 5 km/h, 5.2 m, middle of the bumper, day, dry, seed 1.

The distinction matters because the three levels answer different questions. Functional scenarios are what safety analysis and requirements talk about; logical scenarios are what a test plan covers; concrete scenarios are what a simulator runs and what a verdict refers to. "We tested the child-behind-van scenario" means nothing until someone says which parameter ranges, how they were sampled and what fraction passed.

4.2OpenDRIVE: the road as a reference line with lanes

ASAM OpenDRIVE (current version 1.8, 2023) describes the logical road network in XML. Its central idea is the reference line: a curve in the plane built from segments of five kinds (straight line, arc, spiral/clothoid whose curvature changes linearly, and two cubic polynomial forms). Every point on the road is then given in road coordinates: s is the distance along the reference line, t the lateral offset from it. Lanes are stacked to the left (positive ids) and right (negative ids) of the reference line as widths that may vary with s; lane 0 is the reference line itself. Junctions connect roads, and signals, objects and road marks hang off the same coordinates.

This representation is why scenarios can say "lane −1, s = 20" instead of x/y coordinates, and why the same scenario works on a curved road: positions follow the road. The generated file for this chapter is 46 lines: one <road> with a <planView> holding a single <line> of length 300 and a lane section with lanes 1, 0, −1 and −2, each 3.5 m wide. The 3D look of the road (meshes, textures, kerbs) is a separate asset; OpenDRIVE is the logic that the scenario engine, the traffic simulation and ground-truth labelling share. ASAM OpenCRG adds high-resolution road surface elevation, for vehicle dynamics on rough roads.

Pitfall

An OpenDRIVE map and the 3D scene that do not agree. If the lane marking in the rendered image is 10 cm away from the OpenDRIVE lane border, a lane-keeping test is judged against a ground truth that the camera never saw. Build both from one source or check them against each other.

4.3OpenSCENARIO XML: the storyboard

ASAM OpenSCENARIO XML (version 1.3, 2024) describes the dynamic content. A file declares parameters, references the road network and entity catalogues, declares the entities (vehicles, pedestrians, objects) and then a storyboard. The storyboard has an Init section that places the actors and sets initial speeds, and one or more Stories. A story contains Acts; an act contains ManeuverGroups, which name the actors; a maneuver group contains Maneuvers, and a maneuver contains Events. An event has a start trigger (conditions combined with AND inside a condition group and OR between groups) and one or more actions: speed changes, lane changes, following a trajectory, teleporting, changing the weather. A stop trigger ends the run.

The file for this chapter was generated with the open-source scenariogeneration Python library and is 196 lines long. The heart of it is the event that makes the child walk. Its trigger is the clever part: the distance at which the child starts is not a fixed number but an expression, ${$EgoSpeed * $LateralGap / $PedSpeed}. Interesting detail

Scenario-based testing across tools needs a check of the actors' ground-truth trajectories, not only of the file.

The file generated for this chapter sweeps ego speed from 10 to 60 km/h in 5 km/h steps and child speed from 0.5 to 2.5 m/s in 0.5 m/s steps, which gives 11 × 5 = 55 concrete scenarios.

Floating-point ranges in m/s. The first version of this file had upperLimit="16.67" (60 km/h). 4.5The rest of the family: DSL, OpenLABEL, OpenODD, OSI and FMI

OpenSCENARIO DSL (2.0 in 2022, 2.1 in 2024) is a different language, not a new XML version. It grew out of Foretellix's M-SDL and reads like code: actors, scenarios composed of other scenarios, constraints on parameters ("speed in [10..60] km/h"), and coverage definitions that say which combinations must have been seen. It describes logical scenarios and the intent of a test, and leaves the tool to generate concrete runs. Tool support is narrower than for the XML format.

OpenLABEL (1.0, 2021) is a JSON format for annotations: objects, their 2D/3D shapes over time, actions and relations, in sensor data and in scenarios. The same format can tag recorded drives with scenario descriptions ("child crossing, occluded"), which is how a scenario database is linked to real data. OpenODD aims at a machine-readable description of an operational design domain, building on the ISO 34503 taxonomy. OpenMATERIAL 3D standardises the material properties that physics-based sensor models need (how a surface reflects light, lidar and radar).

Two interface standards connect the pieces. ASAM OSI (Open Simulation Interface) defines protobuf messages for what flows between simulator and models: GroundTruth (every object, lane and sign), SensorView (the ground truth seen from one sensor's mounting position), SensorData (detections or objects a sensor model outputs) and traffic messages. FMI (Functional Mock-up Interface, from the Modelica Association; version 3.0, 2022) packages a model as an FMU, a zip file with an XML description and compiled binaries, which any compliant tool can load and step. Packaging an OSI sensor model as an FMU (the OSMP convention) lets a radar supplier deliver its sensor model to an OEM without delivering source code, and lets the OEM run it in several simulators.

5Sensor models: what the software under test actually sees

The software under test never sees the simulated world; it sees sensor outputs. A sensor model decides which situations a simulation can test at all. A perfect model of the world with a naive sensor model tests the planner, not the perception.

5.1Three levels of fidelity, three injection points

An ideal sensor passes the ground truth of every object in its field of view to the software, without noise or misses. It is fast and exactly explainable, and it tests only what comes after perception: tracking, fusion, the AEB decision. A phenomenological (or statistical) model starts from the same ground truth but reproduces what the real sensor does to it: a detection probability that falls with range and occlusion, position noise that grows with range, latency, false alarms and ghost objects, fields of view and blind zones. Its output is an object list or a detection list, injected where the real sensor's object list would enter the software. A physical model produces raw data: a rendered image with the lens, the noise and the image signal processor applied, a ray-cast point cloud, a radar spectrum or detection list from wave propagation. Only this level tests the perception network itself.

The level is chosen per question. To test whether the AEB decision brakes early enough when a pedestrian is detected 220 ms after it becomes visible, a phenomenological camera model with the measured detection delay is enough and runs thousands of times faster than rendering. To test whether the detector finds a child in a dark coat in front of a dark van at dusk, nothing short of a physical camera model will do, and even then the result must be checked against real images (Chapter 7).

In practice

The phenomenological models are usually fitted from real data: drive or track tests give detection probability versus range and occlusion, error distributions versus range (Automotive sensors 7.4) and latency histograms. The model is only as good as that fit, and it is valid only inside the conditions the data covered.

5.2Camera models: lens, sensor, ISP and timing

A physical camera model has four stages after rendering. The lens maps rays to the image plane with distortion: Brown-Conrady polynomials for normal lenses, equidistant or Kannala-Brandt models for fisheye lenses (mathematics in Mathematics 5; simulating fisheye lenses in a rasteriser in Rasterization versus path tracing 8). Vignetting, flare and the point-spread function (blur) belong here too. The sensor turns light into electrons and electrons into numbers: photon shot noise grows with the square root of the signal, read noise is a fixed floor, dark current grows with temperature, and pixel-to-pixel gain and offset differences add fixed patterns (Camera pipeline: noise). With 4 electrons of read noise, the experiment's simple model gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 2.0 (6 dB) at 10 electrons, 9.3 (19 dB) at 100, 31 (30 dB) at 1,000 and 100 (40 dB) at 10,000 electrons: the dark parts of a dusk scene are where the noise model decides what a detector sees.

The ISP (demosaicing, HDR merge, tone mapping, denoising, sharpening, gamma) shapes the image the network was trained on. Either the simulator models the ISP and injects processed images, or it injects raw sensor data and lets the real ISP run, which is the cleaner choice on a HIL rack. Timing effects are the ones most often forgotten: a rolling shutter reads rows one after another over tens of milliseconds, so a crossing child is sheared; motion blur depends on exposure time; HDR sensors merge exposures taken at slightly different times, which gives ghost edges on moving objects; and LED traffic lights and headlights flicker at their PWM frequency, so a short exposure can catch them dark. A renderer gives none of this for free; each effect has to be modelled explicitly.

Pitfall

Training or testing on rendered images that are too clean. Rendered images have no noise, perfect focus, no compression and no flicker; a detector that performs well on them can drop sharply on real images. Section 7.3 shows a toy version of this effect with numbers.

5.3Lidar models: ray casting and its imperfections

Lidar is the easiest sensor to simulate geometrically: for each laser beam (each elevation channel and azimuth step) cast a ray into the scene and return the first hit. The beam pattern, the scan timing and the range limits come from the data sheet; how many points land on a child at a given range follows directly from the angular resolution (Automotive sensors 4.5). In the running example the ray caster gets occlusion right automatically: no ray reaches the child through the van.

The realism is in what pure geometry misses. Real beams have a footprint that grows with range, so a beam hitting the van's edge partly returns from the van and partly from the child behind it, giving mixed points between the two. Return intensity depends on material reflectivity and angle, and dark or specular surfaces return nothing, so points drop out. Rain, fog and spray scatter the beam, producing early returns and fewer points (Automotive sensors 8.2). Retro-reflective signs cause blooming. A spinning or scanning lidar takes about 100 ms per sweep, so a moving scene is distorted unless motion is compensated. A good lidar model adds these effects statistically on top of ray casting, with parameters fitted to measurements.

5.4Radar models: the hardest sensor to simulate

Radar is hard because its wavelength (about 4 mm at 77 GHz) is not small compared with the structures it sees. A car does not reflect from its surface like a mirrored box; it reflects from a few strong scattering centres (wheel arches, licence plate, corners), and their strength fluctuates strongly with aspect angle. A pedestrian's radar cross-section is small and fluctuating, and swinging arms and legs add micro-Doppler. Most important, radar waves bounce: off the road, off guard rails, off other cars.

Figure 5.2 shows what this means for the running example. The camera cannot see the child until it leaves the van's shadow. A radar, however, can sometimes receive a weak return from the child's legs through the gap under the van's floor, reflected off the road; research prototypes have exploited exactly this to detect occluded pedestrians earlier. A radar model that ignores multipath will never show this benefit, and one that models it crudely will show ghosts that the real radar does not have. Three modelling approaches exist. Object-level statistical models give detection probability and noise as functions of range, angle and RCS, with false alarms added; they are fast and fitted from data. Ray-tracing models shoot many rays (shooting and bouncing rays), follow reflections with material properties and build the received signal; they capture multipath but are expensive and need material data (OpenMATERIAL 3D exists for this reason). Data-driven models learn the mapping from scene to radar output from recorded drives. The radar equation, RCS values and what a radar delivers are in Automotive sensors 3.7.

Numbers

Received power in the radar equation falls with the fourth power of range: doubling the distance costs 12 dB. Typical radar cross-sections are around −10 to 0 dBsm for a pedestrian and 10 to 20 dBsm for a car, so a car at 200 m returns roughly as much power as a pedestrian at well under 100 m. Any statistical radar model must at least reproduce this range-and-RCS dependence.

5.5Validating a sensor model

A sensor model is itself a piece of software that needs evidence. The usual method is to rebuild a real test in simulation (same track, same targets, same trajectories measured with differential GPS) and compare distributions, not single frames: detection probability versus range, position and velocity errors, number of points or detections on a target, false-alarm rates, and for cameras the detector's own output on real versus rendered images. The comparison defines the domain in which the model is credible. Regulations for automated driving now ask for exactly this kind of toolchain credibility assessment, and the safety argument (Functional safety 8.6) can only use simulation results inside the validated domain.

"We pick the sensor-model fidelity by what we test: ideal or statistical object lists for planning and AEB logic, physics-based rendering only for perception. Every model is fitted and validated against real measurements, and its validity domain is part of the result."

6Vehicle and traffic simulation

The ego vehicle must react to the software's commands the way a real car would, and the other road users must behave plausibly. Neither needs to be perfect; both need to be good enough for the question being asked, and they have to run in step with everything else.

6.1Vehicle dynamics: as simple as the test allows

Vehicle models span a wide range. A point mass with a speed and an acceleration limit is enough for many traffic participants. The kinematic bicycle model (Planning and control 9.1) is adequate for low lateral acceleration. A dynamic single-track model with a tyre model captures understeer and limit handling (Planning and control 9.2). Commercial multibody models with dozens of degrees of freedom, suspension, powertrain and brake hydraulics (CarMaker, ASM, VI-grade) are used when the function interacts with the chassis: stability control, emergency steering, braking on split friction.

For an AEB test the longitudinal chain is what matters, and the brake actuator dominates it. The model in this chapter has a dead time of 150 ms between the brake request and the start of deceleration (pump and hydraulics), then a jerk limit of 30 m/s³ up to the friction limit μ·g (8.8 m/s² on a dry road). With it, the car needs 1.55 s from brake request to standstill from 40 km/h on a dry road, and 2.49 s on a road with μ = 0.5. Leaving out the dead time would make every AEB in the simulation look about 1.7 m better at 40 km/h than it is.

6.2Traffic simulation: SUMO and agent behaviour

Background traffic gives the software under test realistic surroundings: cars that cut in, queue, accelerate away from traffic lights. Eclipse SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility, started at DLR in 2001) is the standard open-source microscopic traffic simulator: every vehicle is simulated individually on a road network, following a car-following model (how a driver keeps distance to the vehicle ahead) and a lane-change model. The default car-following model, Krauss, drives as fast as is safe and adds random "driver imperfection" (parameter sigma); the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM, Treiber et al. 2000) accelerates and brakes smoothly towards a desired time gap. External programs control SUMO through TraCI (a TCP protocol) or libsumo (the same API in-process).

The experiment ran SUMO 1.28 on a 2 km two-lane road with a 50 km/h limit and 1,800 vehicles per hour for 600 s of simulated time in 50 ms steps (12,000 steps), stepped through TraCI as a co-simulation master would. With Krauss the mean speed was 48.2 km/h and the 5th percentile of time headways was 1.01 s; with IDM, 45.3 km/h and 1.22 s, a measurably more cautious traffic stream from one changed line. Two runs with seed 1 produced the identical state hash; seed 2 gave a different one (mean speed 47.1 km/h). Querying every vehicle over TCP in every step limited the run to 7 to 8 times real time on one core; this per-call overhead is why co-simulations batch their queries or use libsumo.

Pedestrians and cyclists are usually scripted by the scenario (like the child here) or driven by simple rule-based agents. The trend is towards learned agent models trained on large recorded datasets, which react to the ego vehicle plausibly; the risk is that such agents are hard to control precisely, which is exactly what a test needs.

Pitfall

Reactive agents can make a scenario non-critical. If background traffic brakes for the child too, or a smart pedestrian model waits for the car, the "child behind van" test passes because the child never steps out. Critical actors are scripted; reactive agents fill the background.

6.3Co-simulation: making several simulators share one clock

A realistic setup often combines tools: CARLA for the world and sensors, SUMO for traffic, a vehicle-dynamics FMU, the software under test. Each has its own natural step: vehicle dynamics at 1 kHz, the rendering world at 20 Hz, traffic at the same 50 ms, a camera at 30 Hz. Co-simulation runs them as separate solvers that exchange data only at agreed synchronisation points, under a master algorithm that advances everybody to the next point and passes the outputs across. FMI's co-simulation mode standardises this handshake; CARLA and SUMO's bridge does the same with TraCI.

Two details decide whether co-simulation is right. The exchange step adds latency: a SUMO car that brakes at 51 ms is seen by CARLA only at 100 ms, so the coupling step must be small compared with the dynamics that matter. And sensor frames rarely align with the coupling grid: a 30 Hz camera frame at 33.3 ms must be rendered with the world interpolated to 33.3 ms, or the time stamp must say when the world state really was, otherwise the tracker computes velocities from wrong time differences.

Talk track

"Our simulators run in lockstep under one master: the world, traffic, vehicle dynamics and the software under test all advance to the same synchronisation points and exchange data only there. That keeps time consistent and runs repeatable, at the cost of waiting for the slowest participant."

7Synthetic data, domain randomisation and the sim-to-real gap

Simulators are used not only to test networks but to train them. Synthetic data comes with perfect labels for free and can contain any rare situation, but a network trained on it learns the simulator's world. The art is to make the network learn the task instead.

7.1Why synthetic data

Labelling real data is slow and expensive: a 3D box costs seconds to minutes of human time, a pixel-accurate segmentation of one image tens of minutes, and some labels (exact depth for every pixel, optical flow, the occluded part of a pedestrian) cannot be drawn by a human at all. A simulator knows all of these exactly. It can also produce what is rare or dangerous in reality, such as children stepping out from behind vans at dusk in rain, in thousands of variations, and data for a new camera position before the first prototype car exists. Real data stays essential, because it defines the distribution the network must work on; synthetic data fills the gaps (Deep learning vision 15.1 describes the data engine; Training 9.4 the long tail).

7.2The domain gap and its four sources

The sim-to-real gap is the drop in performance when something developed in simulation meets reality. It has four sources. Appearance: textures, lighting, materials and reflections look different, and rendered images are too clean (Section 5.2). Content: the simulated world has fewer kinds of objects, less clutter and different statistics of where things are (every simulated child wears one of ten outfits). Sensor: the camera model misses noise, ISP, compression and timing effects. Behaviour: simulated agents move too regularly, never hesitate and never do anything strange. A network can exploit any of these: it may learn that "pedestrians have perfectly sharp edges" and then miss blurred real ones.

7.3Domain randomisation: making reality one more variation

Two strategies attack the gap from opposite sides. Photorealism tries to make the simulation so close to reality that the gap disappears; it is expensive and never complete. Domain randomisation (Tobin et al., 2017; applied to car detection by NVIDIA's Tremblay et al., 2018) randomises everything the network should not rely on: textures, lighting, camera exposure, noise, blur, object colours, distractors. If the training data varies more than reality does, reality looks like just another variation. Structured domain randomisation keeps the scene plausible (cars on roads, pedestrians on pavements) while randomising appearance.

The experiment for this chapter shows the effect on a deliberately small problem. A tiny renderer draws 16×16 pixel patches that either contain a child silhouette or only background clutter. A camera model applies exposure gain, blur, shot and read noise, gamma and 8-bit quantisation. A linear detector on gradient features (a miniature HOG detector) is trained on different data and always tested on "real" patches, whose camera is darker, softer and noisier and whose backgrounds are busier than the clean simulator's.

Three results carry over to real systems. The detector trained on clean simulation is excellent on simulation (91.6%) and poor on reality (64.8%): good simulation results say nothing until checked on real data. Randomising the camera and backgrounds costs a little on clean simulation (88.1%) and gains ten points on reality (75.1%), reaching the ceiling that 6,000 real examples give in this toy (75.1%); 300 real examples alone reach 71.2%. And randomised synthetic data plus a small real set (75.0%) is the usual practical recipe, because in real problems the randomisation never covers every aspect of reality and the real samples anchor what it misses.

Pitfall

Randomising the wrong things. Randomising textures does nothing against a sensor gap caused by rolling shutter; randomising noise does nothing against a content gap where the simulator has no prams, wheelchairs or children in costumes. Find out which of the four gaps dominates (by training on synthetic data and analysing the real-data errors) before deciding what to randomise.

7.4Digital twins and neural reconstruction

"Digital twin" is used for three different things, and it helps to ask which one is meant. A twin of the environment is a specific test track, junction or city district reconstructed to centimetre accuracy, so that real drives and simulated drives can be compared directly. A twin of the sensor is a sensor model validated against the real sensor (Section 5.5). A twin of the ECU or vehicle is a virtual ECU that runs the production software, from the application code alone up to the full software stack on an instruction-set simulator of the target processor, and a vehicle model that matches the real vehicle.

Building environment twins by hand is slow, which is why neural reconstruction has spread quickly since 2023. Methods such as neural radiance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting reconstruct a drivable scene from the camera and lidar data of a recorded drive; the scene can then be re-rendered from slightly different viewpoints, with objects removed, moved or inserted. This turns a real drive into a closed-loop test: if the new software brakes earlier, the reconstructed scene can render what the camera would have seen from the new position. Generative world models go further and synthesise or vary whole video sequences from a text or a layout. Both reduce the appearance gap dramatically for situations close to the recordings, and both get unreliable as the viewpoint moves far from the original drive or for objects they never saw.

Why it matters

Neural reconstruction blurs the line between replay and simulation (Chapter 9): the content comes from reality, the closed loop from simulation. When someone presents such results, ask how far from the recorded trajectory the ego moved, because that distance limits how much the rendered images can be trusted.

7.5Measuring the gap

The decisive measure is task performance: train on synthetic data, test on real data, and compare with a model trained on real data. Cheaper proxies exist (image-statistics distances such as FID between rendered and real images, comparing the distributions of detector confidence or of feature activations), but a small FID does not guarantee that the detector behaves the same. For testing rather than training, the equivalent question is whether the software under test makes the same errors in simulation as in reality on the same scenario; it is answered by running matched real and simulated tests and comparing their error distributions. The result decides how much weight simulated test results may carry in the safety argument.

Talk track

"Synthetic data gives us perfect labels and rare situations. We close the gap with randomisation plus a real-data anchor, and we never accept a simulation-only metric: the final number is always on real data."

8Software-, hardware- and vehicle-in-the-loop

X-in-the-loop names which parts of the system are real and which are simulated. Each step to the right in Figure 8.1 makes the test more realistic, more expensive and slower; a sound validation plan uses each level for what only it can find.

8.1The ladder: MIL, SIL, PIL, HIL, VIL

In model-in-the-loop (MIL) an algorithm runs as a model or prototype (Simulink, Python) against simulated inputs. In software-in-the-loop (SIL) the production source code, compiled for a PC, runs against the simulator. In processor-in-the-loop (PIL) the code is compiled with the target compiler and runs on the target processor (an evaluation board or an instruction-set simulator), which exposes compiler, numeric and timing differences. In hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) the real ECU with its production software and I/O is connected to a real-time simulator that pretends to be the rest of the car. In vehicle-in-the-loop (VIL) a real car with real sensors drives on a test track while virtual objects are injected into its perception or fused object list, so a virtual child can step out in front of a real braking car. Driver-in-the-loop simulators add a human driver for HMI and takeover studies.

The planning side of this ladder (what each level typically finds, availability and cost per test hour) is in Project management 6.3. Here the interest is how the setups are built.

8.2A SIL setup that can run ten thousand times a night

A modern SIL runs in containers on CI runners or cloud nodes (Figure 8.2). The simulator container gets a GPU (Build and containers 10.1) and runs in synchronous mode. A bridge translates between the simulator API and ROS 2 or OSI, publishes simulated time and makes the simulator wait until the software under test has processed each step. The software under test is the same source code as the ECU build, compiled for x86, in its own containers; the parts of the ECU environment that do not exist on a PC (vehicle bus, diagnostics, time synchronisation, health monitoring) are stubbed. A recorder writes ground truth and SUT topics to MCAP, and an evaluator turns the log into metrics and a JUnit report. Every container image is pinned by digest, so a run can be repeated a year later.

SIL is where breadth comes from: thousands of concrete scenarios per night, each in seconds to minutes, in parallel. What it cannot show is everything that depends on the real target: execution times on the ECU's CPU and accelerator, the behaviour of the target compiler and libraries (a different float rounding, a different inference engine), real sensor electronics and real I/O. Its timing results are in simulated time and say nothing about whether the ECU meets its 33 ms frame budget.

Pitfall

Different inference back ends in SIL and on the ECU. The SIL often runs the network in a PC framework, the ECU in a vendor inference engine with INT8 quantisation. The two can disagree on borderline detections, which are exactly the ones a critical scenario produces. Either run the target engine in SIL (on an emulator or a matching GPU) or quantify the difference on a reference data set and include it in the sensor model.

8.3A camera-ECU HIL rack

A HIL rack for a camera ECU (Figure 8.3) must convince the ECU that it is in a car, in real time, because the ECU will not wait. A real-time computer runs vehicle dynamics at around 1 kHz and simulates the rest of the vehicle network (rest-bus simulation): the CAN and Ethernet messages carrying speed, yaw rate and gear arrive with the right timing and content, and the ECU's outputs, such as the brake request, are read back into the vehicle model. A render PC produces each camera image for the current simulated pose. A video injection unit emulates the camera module: it behaves like the image sensor and serialiser on the GMSL or FPD-Link link, answers the ECU's register accesses on the back channel, follows the ECU's frame-sync signal, and sends the rendered frames, either as raw sensor data (so the ECU's own ISP runs) or as processed images. Programmable power supplies reproduce cranking dips and under-voltage, a fault injection unit opens lines, drops frames or corrupts CRCs, and an automation PC sequences tests and collects results.

The hard part is timing. Rendering must keep up with the camera rate for every camera (33 ms at 30 fps, times the number of cameras), the injection chain adds its own latency that must be constant and known, and the frame the ECU receives must belong to the same simulated instant as the vehicle speed on the bus. A render that occasionally takes 40 ms produces a dropped or repeated frame, which the ECU may treat as a sensor fault. Alternatives to direct injection are over-the-air stimulation (a screen in front of the real camera, limited by the screen's dynamic range and refresh) for cameras, and radar target simulators that delay and Doppler-shift the radar's own signal in an anechoic chamber.

Numbers

A HIL rack runs one scenario in real time, so a 15 s scenario takes 15 s plus set-up; a rack does perhaps a few thousand scenario runs per day, compared with tens of thousands per night for a cloud SIL. Racks cost hundreds of thousands of euros each, which is why HIL capacity is a planning topic (Project management 6.3).

8.4Vehicle-in-the-loop and the test track

VIL closes the remaining gap: the real vehicle dynamics, real brakes and the real sensors' view of the real world, with only the critical actor virtual. The car drives on an empty track; its precise position (differential GNSS and inertial measurement) is fed to a simulator that places the virtual child; the child is injected into the object list, or rendered into the camera images with the real background. Because nothing physical is in the way, a failed test costs nothing but a repeat. The limitation is the injection itself: injecting at object level bypasses perception, and blending rendered children into real images is an augmented-reality problem with its own gap. Test-track runs with real soft targets (a child dummy on a low platform) remain the final anchor, and they are also what consumer test protocols specify.

8.5Choosing the level

The level follows from the question. Does the AEB logic brake in time across all parameter combinations? SIL with statistical sensor models, thousands of runs. Does the detector find the child in rendered dusk images? SIL with physical camera models and real-data comparison. Does the ECU meet its timing with eight cameras, react correctly to a lost camera link and keep working at 9 V supply? HIL. Does the complete car stop for a child at 40 km/h on a wet surface? Track, with VIL for the cases too dangerous or too expensive to set up physically. A failure found at one level becomes a regression test at the cheapest level that can reproduce it.

Talk track

"SIL gives us breadth, HIL gives us the real ECU and real-time behaviour, the track gives us reality. Every failure found on the right moves to the left as a regression test, so the expensive levels keep finding new things."

Not in this chapter: bench levels in production integration and diagnostics on the HIL (Production integration 10.3).

9Replay, re-simulation and determinism

Recorded drives are the most realistic test input there is, and simulated runs are the most controllable. Both are useful only if a run can be repeated: a test that passes on Monday and fails on Tuesday with the same software proves nothing in either direction.

9.1Open-loop replay and closed-loop simulation

In open-loop replay (also called re-simulation or reprocessing) recorded sensor data and vehicle bus data are fed into a new version of the software, and its outputs are compared with labels or with the old version's outputs. The inputs are real, so there is no sim-to-real gap; but the world does not react. If the new AEB decides to brake 300 ms earlier, the recorded car still drives on, because it was driven by a human months ago. Replay is therefore excellent for perception metrics and for counting false activations: running a new AEB over hundreds of thousands of kilometres of normal driving and counting how often it would have braked for nothing is the main evidence that it is not dangerous in the other direction (Functional safety 8.6). It cannot show whether a correct activation would have avoided the collision.

Closed-loop simulation answers exactly that question, at the price of models everywhere. The two are complementary, and a pedestrian AEB needs both: closed loop for "does it stop for the child", replay for "does it brake for shadows, manhole covers and pedestrians on the pavement". Neural reconstruction of recorded drives (Section 7.4) is the attempt to get both at once: a recorded scene that can be re-rendered when the ego moves differently.

9.2What a recording must contain

Replay works only if the recording is complete enough to recreate the software's inputs exactly. That means every sensor stream with capture time stamps (when the light hit the sensor), not only arrival times; the vehicle bus with its own time stamps on a common time base (PTP-synchronised in the car); the calibration of every sensor at the time of recording; the software and configuration versions of the recording car; and, for perception, raw data before any lossy step that the new software might do differently. Recording formats include rosbag2/MCAP in ROS-based stacks, ADTF DAT files from the ADTF framework common in German automotive, and ASAM MDF4 for measurement data.

Numbers

One 8.3-megapixel camera delivering raw 12-bit images at 30 fps produces 12.4 MB per frame, 373 MB/s (about 3 Gbit/s) and 1.3 TB per hour. A data-collection car with several such cameras, radars and lidars records tens of terabytes per day, which is why recordings are compressed (losslessly for raw sensor data meant for replay), selected by triggers and moved to the data centre by swapping disks rather than over a network.

Replay itself has a timing problem: the software expects data at the rate it was recorded. A replay tool can play in real time (needed for HIL replay into a real ECU), faster (when the software keeps up) or in lockstep with the software, so that a slow PC does not drop frames. The ros2 bag play --clock command of Section 2.5 makes the bag the time master; the same idea applies to every replay system.

9.3Determinism: same inputs, same verdict

A simulation run is deterministic if the same scenario, parameters, seed and software versions always give the same result. Without it, nothing else in this chapter works: a regression cannot be bisected, a failure cannot be debugged, a fix cannot be confirmed and a pass rate cannot be trusted. Figure 9.2 shows the main mechanism. In free-running mode the simulator produces frames at its own pace; when the software is still busy with one frame, the next is dropped or queued, and the exact pattern depends on the load of the machine. In lockstep mode the simulator waits until the software has processed each step before advancing simulated time; the wall clock may run slower or faster than real time, but the software sees the same sequence of inputs on every run.

The experiment quantifies this with the closed-loop model of Chapter 10 on a borderline case (child at 8 km/h, ego at 45 km/h, 100 ms perception latency), 1,000 runs each:

Three conclusions follow. Lockstep with a fixed seed makes the run perfectly repeatable, which is what regression tests need. And free-running timing turned a "pass" into a coin toss: the same software and the same scenario failed in 47% of runs. A free-running setup would report this scenario as flaky; in truth the software has almost no margin, and that is the real finding.

Why it matters

Determinism and realism pull in opposite directions. The real car is free-running: frames arrive with jitter, threads compete, the ECU is sometimes busy. Lockstep hides that. The practical answer is to use both deliberately: lockstep with fixed seeds for regression and evidence, and randomised timing and seeds as a separate test that measures how sensitive a function is to timing, with the variation itself as the result.

9.4Where nondeterminism hides

Even in lockstep, many things can make two runs differ, and each needs its own fix. Wall-clock time in the software (a timeout measured with the system clock, a time stamp taken from std::chrono::system_clock) is the most common; everything must use the simulated clock. Threads and multi-threaded executors process callbacks in a different order on every run; deterministic setups use single-threaded executors or explicit sequencing. Message order across topics is not guaranteed by DDS; when a node's output depends on whether the image or the vehicle-speed message came first, it must synchronise on time stamps. Random numbers without a logged seed: in the simulator (traffic, sensor noise), in the software and in libraries. Floating-point summation order: a parallel reduction on a GPU that uses atomic additions sums in a different order each time, and the last bits differ; some deep-learning operations are nondeterministic for this reason unless deterministic algorithms are forced. Build differences: an inference engine that selects its kernels by timing measurements at build time can produce a slightly different engine on every build, so the engine file, not just the model, must be pinned. Physics with variable sub-steps, and rendering with temporal effects (anti-aliasing that accumulates over frames) also carry state between runs.

In practice

Test determinism directly: run a fixed set of scenarios twice in every nightly build and compare hashes of the recorded SUT outputs, as the SUMO experiment did with its state hash (seed 1 gave the same hash twice; seed 2 a different one). A changed hash with unchanged inputs is a determinism bug and is treated like any other failing test.

10Scenario-based validation and CI: following the child to a verdict

This chapter puts the pieces together. The OpenSCENARIO file from Chapter 4 runs in a simulator with a camera model, the software under test decides, the vehicle model brakes, and the evaluator returns a verdict. Then the single run becomes a swept logical scenario, and the sweep becomes a CI job.

10.1From requirement to pass criteria

A verdict needs a criterion that comes from a requirement, not from what the software happens to do. For pedestrian AEB the typical requirements combine three things: avoid the collision up to a certain speed; above it, reduce the impact speed by a required amount; and do not brake without a reason more often than a tolerated rate. The evaluator computes metrics from the ground-truth log: whether a collision occurred and at what speed, the minimum distance, the time to collision when braking started, the deceleration and jerk applied. Criticality metrics such as time to collision (TTC), post-encroachment time (PET, the time between one road user leaving a conflict point and the other arriving) and required deceleration also describe how dangerous the scenario was, which matters when comparing runs. The false-activation side cannot be tested with this scenario at all; it comes from replay of normal driving (Section 9.1).

Pitfall

Pass criteria defined after looking at results. If the team first runs the sweep and then sets "avoid collisions up to 45 km/h" because that is where the software currently fails, the test documents the software instead of testing it. Criteria come from the requirement, which comes from the safety analysis and the customer.

10.2One run, step by step

Figure 10.1 runs the concrete scenario with a small closed-loop model written for this chapter. It contains everything from Figure 1.1 in miniature: the scenario trigger with the OpenSCENARIO expression, a camera model that casts seven rays from the camera to points across the child and counts how many miss the van, a detection probability of 95% per frame once at least 40% of the child is visible, a tracker that confirms after three consecutive detections and estimates lateral velocity from the last five, a perception latency, an AEB function that requests full braking when the child's predicted path crosses the car's path and the time to reach it drops below 1.4 s, and the brake actuator of Section 6.1. Every number in this section comes from that model; move the sliders to see how the event chain stretches.

At the default point (40 km/h, child at 5 km/h, 100 ms latency, dry road) the child starts walking when the car is 23.2 m away. It is hidden for 220 ms, until it has moved far enough out of the van's shadow; the tracker confirms it 100 ms later; the track reaches the AEB function after the 100 ms latency; and the AEB waits a further 265 ms, until the time to reach the child falls below its 1.4 s threshold. The car stops 5.33 m before the child: a comfortable pass. The margin is the 265 ms the AEB spent waiting. Make the child run at 8 km/h and that margin disappears: the trigger distance shrinks to 14.5 m, the child is visible after 120 ms, and by the time the track arrives the time to collision is already below the threshold, so braking starts at once and the car stops 0.70 m before the child. At 50 km/h the same child is hit at 17.6 km/h; at 40 km/h with 300 ms latency instead of 100 ms, at 18.7 km/h. On a wet road with μ = 0.5 the 5 km/h case still passes, but with 0.61 m to spare, because stopping takes 2.49 s instead of 1.55 s.

Interesting detail

Reducing the confirmation to a single frame does not help as much as expected. The tracker needs several measurements to estimate the child's lateral velocity, and without a velocity the AEB cannot predict that a child at the edge of the lane will enter the car's path. Detection, confirmation and velocity estimation together set the delay, which is why perception latency budgets are written for the whole chain, not for the detector.

10.3Sweeping the logical scenario

One concrete pass says little. Figure 10.2 sweeps two parameters of the logical scenario, ego speed (10 to 60 km/h) and perception latency (0 to 400 ms), and runs the closed loop for each of the 99 combinations, in your browser, each time you move a control.

The map tells a story that no single run can. With the child at 5 km/h on a dry road, all 99 combinations pass, and the smallest gap is 1.9 m; a test report could say "the AEB passes the child-behind-van scenario from 10 to 60 km/h with up to 400 ms latency". With the child at 8 km/h, which is a child running rather than walking, 42 of 99 fail, with impact speeds up to 47 km/h, and the boundary runs diagonally: every 50 ms of latency costs a few km/h of avoidable speed. Confirming after six frames instead of three raises the failures to 53; on a wet road (μ = 0.6) even the walking child produces 24 failures. The pass/fail result is a property of the chosen parameter ranges as much as of the software, which is why the logical scenario, its ranges and their justification belong in the test specification.

Talk track

"We don't report 'scenario passed'. We report the logical scenario, the parameter ranges and why, how we sampled them, and where the pass/fail boundary lies. The boundary is the interesting part: it tells us how much margin we have and which parameter eats it."

10.4Beyond grids: search, sampling and coverage

A grid wastes most runs far from the boundary and explodes with the number of parameters. Three families of methods do better. Adaptive sampling places new runs where neighbouring results disagree, refining the boundary of Figure 10.2 with a fraction of the runs. Falsification treats testing as optimisation: search the parameter space for the run with the smallest margin (or the highest impact speed), using Bayesian optimisation, evolutionary search or gradient-free methods, often with requirements written in signal temporal logic so that the margin is a number, not a yes/no. Importance sampling answers the statistical question: if parameters follow real-world distributions, what is the failure rate? It samples rare, critical combinations more often and re-weights the results, which turns billions of equivalent kilometres into thousands of runs.

Coverage is the counterpart: which parts of the operational design domain and which scenario types have been exercised, in which combinations? OpenSCENARIO DSL's coverage definitions, scenario tags in OpenLABEL and an ODD description are the tools; the result is a coverage report next to the pass rates, so that "98% pass" cannot hide "nothing tested at night".

10.5The CI pipeline for virtual tests

Virtual tests are only useful if they run automatically on every relevant change. Figure 10.3 shows a typical staging. Every merge request builds the containers and runs unit and component tests plus a SIL smoke suite of a few dozen scenarios that finishes in minutes. Every night the main branch runs the full scenario catalogue, tens of thousands of concrete scenarios in parallel on cloud GPUs, plus replay of recorded data. Release candidates go to HIL racks for timing and fault-injection tests, and finally to the track and the fleet. All results land in one store with everything needed to reproduce them.

A merge-request job can look like this (GitLab CI syntax; run_scenarios stands for an in-house runner that starts the simulator and SUT containers, executes a shard of the suite and writes JUnit XML):

10.6Turning runs into evidence

Each stored result needs the scenario file and its version, the parameter values, the seed, the simulator version, the sensor-model versions, the SUT version and the verdict with its metrics. With that, results can be traced to requirements (which runs support "avoid collisions with crossing children up to 40 km/h"), aggregated into pass rates per logical scenario and per ODD slice, and compared between software versions. Failures found at any level become new concrete scenarios in the catalogue, so the catalogue grows from experience.

Flaky virtual tests are handled like flaky unit tests (Software quality 4.6), with one difference: in simulation, flakiness is usually information. Either the setup is not deterministic (fix it, Section 9.4) or the scenario is close to the boundary and timing variation decides (Table 9.1), in which case retrying until green hides exactly the risk the test was built to find. Finally, the simulation results enter the safety case only together with the evidence for the toolchain's credibility: validated sensor models, a vehicle model checked against track data, and correlation between simulated and real results on a set of reference scenarios.

Talk track

"Every virtual test result is reproducible from its record: scenario, parameters, seed, simulator, models and software versions. That is what lets us bisect regressions, trace results to requirements and use simulation in the safety case."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

Why can't we validate ADAS and automated driving by driving test kilometres alone?Basic

Critical events are too rare. Showing a fatality rate no worse than human drivers (about one per 100 million miles) with 95% confidence needs roughly 275 million failure-free miles, and every software change restarts the count. Dangerous situations can also not be staged with real people. Simulation explores many chosen situations cheaply; track and road tests anchor it in reality.

Explain nodes, topics, services and actions in ROS 2, and when you use each.Basic

Nodes are units of computation. Topics are anonymous publish-subscribe streams for continuous data like images and tracks. Services are request-response calls for short operations like resetting a simulation. Actions are long-running goals with feedback, result and cancel, like running a scenario or driving to a pose. Parameters configure nodes at runtime.

What is DDS and how does ROS 2 use it?Basic

DDS is a data-centric publish-subscribe middleware standard with decentralised discovery, QoS policies and a common wire protocol (RTPS). ROS 2 uses it through the RMW interface, so the same code runs on Fast DDS, Cyclone DDS or Connext; Zenoh is now an alternative transport. Discovery finds matching readers and writers, which then exchange data directly.

My subscriber receives nothing although the topic exists. How do you debug it?Intermediate

First ros2 topic info -v to compare QoS: a reliable reader does not match a best-effort writer, and a transient-local reader does not match a volatile writer. Then check the domain id, the message type, use_sim_time (stamps out of range get filtered), network or multicast restrictions, and whether the publisher really publishes (ros2 topic hz).

Explain the QoS policies reliability, durability and history.Intermediate

Reliability decides whether lost samples are retransmitted. Durability decides whether late-joining readers get earlier samples (transient local) or only new ones (volatile). History with depth decides how many samples are queued per instance: keep last N drops old ones, keep all keeps everything up to resource limits. Matching follows requested-versus-offered.

What is simulated time in ROS 2 and why does it matter?Basic

With use_sim_time true, a node's ROS clock follows the /clock topic published by the simulator or a bag player. Timers, time stamps and timeouts then run in simulated time, so the software behaves the same whether the simulator runs faster or slower than real time. One node left on wall time breaks time stamps and filtering.

Compare CARLA, Gazebo and a commercial simulator.Intermediate

CARLA is open-source, Unreal-based, strong in urban driving and camera rendering, with Traffic Manager, ScenarioRunner, ROS and SUMO bridges. Gazebo is robotics-focused, strong in physics and robot sensors, weak in traffic and photorealism. Commercial tools bring validated vehicle models, physics-based sensors, HIL integration, cloud scale, support and qualification arguments. The choice follows the question being tested.

What are functional, logical and concrete scenarios?Basic

A functional scenario is a natural-language description. A logical scenario defines its parameters with ranges and distributions. A concrete scenario fixes all parameters to one value and can be executed. Verdicts belong to concrete scenarios; coverage and pass rates belong to logical ones.

What do OpenDRIVE and OpenSCENARIO describe, and how do they relate?Basic

OpenDRIVE describes the static road network: reference lines from lines, arcs, spirals and polynomials, lanes as widths in s-t coordinates, junctions and signals. OpenSCENARIO describes the dynamic content: entities, initial positions, a storyboard of events with triggers and actions, and stop conditions. The scenario references the road file and uses its lane coordinates for positions.

Walk me through the structure of an OpenSCENARIO storyboard.Intermediate

Init places actors and sets initial states. Stories contain Acts, Acts contain ManeuverGroups that name actors, ManeuverGroups contain Maneuvers, Maneuvers contain Events. Each Event has a start trigger made of condition groups (AND inside, OR between) and actions. A stop trigger on the storyboard ends the run, for example on timeout or collision.

What is ASAM OSI and why package sensor models as FMUs?Advanced

OSI defines protobuf messages for ground truth, sensor views and sensor outputs, so a sensor model can be connected to any compliant simulator. FMI packages the model as an FMU with a standard stepping interface and compiled binaries. Together a supplier can deliver a validated sensor model without source code, and an OEM can run it in several tools.

Describe the fidelity levels of sensor models.Intermediate

Ideal models pass ground truth in the field of view. Phenomenological models add measured detection probability, noise, latency, occlusion and false alarms at object or detection level. Physical models produce raw data by rendering or ray tracing. Lower levels test downstream logic quickly; only physical models test perception, and only if validated against real data.

Why is radar harder to simulate than lidar or camera?Advanced

Radar's millimetre wavelength makes reflections depend on scattering centres, material and aspect angle, with strong fluctuation. Multipath via road, guard rails and other vehicles creates ghosts and sometimes lets radar see under or around obstacles. Capturing that needs ray tracing with material data; statistical models miss it and naive ray tracing invents effects.

What is the sim-to-real gap and how do you reduce it?Intermediate

It is the performance drop when moving from simulated to real data, caused by appearance, content, sensor and behaviour differences. Remedies: more realistic rendering and sensor models, domain randomisation, mixing synthetic data with real data, fine-tuning on real data, neural reconstruction from real drives. Always measure the final performance on real data.

What is domain randomisation?Basic

Training with simulated data in which everything irrelevant to the task (textures, lighting, exposure, noise, blur, distractors) varies more than in reality, so that the network learns the task rather than the simulator's appearance and treats reality as one more variation.

Explain MIL, SIL, PIL, HIL and VIL.Basic

MIL: algorithm as a model against simulation. SIL: production source compiled for a PC. PIL: target-compiled code on the target processor. HIL: real ECU with production software, I/O connected to a real-time simulator. VIL: real vehicle and sensors on a track with virtual objects injected. Realism and cost rise, throughput falls.

How does a camera-ECU HIL rack feed images to the ECU?Advanced

A render PC creates each camera frame for the current simulated pose; a video injection unit emulates the camera module on the serialiser link (GMSL or FPD-Link), answers register accesses, follows the ECU's frame sync and delivers raw or processed frames. A real-time computer runs vehicle dynamics and rest-bus simulation so bus signals match the images in time.

What is the difference between open-loop replay and closed-loop simulation?Basic

Replay feeds recorded real data to the software; inputs are realistic but the world does not react to its decisions. Closed-loop simulation feeds the software's commands back into a simulated vehicle and world; it tests consequences but every input is a model. Replay suits perception metrics and false activations, closed loop suits avoidance.

How do you make simulation runs deterministic?Intermediate

Run in lockstep with a fixed step, use simulated time everywhere, log and fix all random seeds, process callbacks in a defined order (single-threaded executor or explicit synchronisation on time stamps), force deterministic GPU algorithms, pin container images and inference engines, and check determinism by hashing outputs of repeated runs.

How would you set up CI for virtual testing?Intermediate

Stage it: a minutes-long SIL smoke suite on every merge request, the full scenario catalogue and replay nightly in parallel on cloud GPUs, HIL for release candidates, track and fleet for releases. Pin all images by digest, encode parameters and seed in test names, emit JUnit, store every result with its versions, and link failures to logs.

Which metrics would you use to evaluate a pedestrian AEB scenario?Intermediate

Collision yes or no, impact speed and speed reduction, minimum distance, time to collision at brake onset, deceleration and jerk. Criticality metrics such as TTC and post-encroachment time describe how hard the scenario was. False activation rates come separately from replay of normal driving.

How do you choose which concrete scenarios to run when a full grid is too large?Advanced

Use adaptive sampling to refine the pass/fail boundary, falsification (optimisation for the smallest margin, often with temporal-logic requirements) to find worst cases, importance sampling with real-world parameter distributions to estimate failure rates, and coverage metrics over the ODD to make sure nothing important is untested.

What does a SIL test not tell you about the ECU?Intermediate

Real execution times on the target CPU and accelerators, the effect of the target compiler, libraries and inference engine (for example INT8 quantisation), behaviour of real sensor electronics, interfaces and power, and anything that depends on real-time interaction with hardware. Timing in SIL is simulated time.

What is co-simulation and what can go wrong?Advanced

Several simulators with their own solvers and step sizes exchange data only at synchronisation points under a master algorithm, as standardised by FMI co-simulation. The coupling step adds delay, so it must be small relative to the relevant dynamics, and sensor frames between sync points must be rendered or stamped at their true time.

What is a digital twin in simulation-based validation?Intermediate

It can mean a precisely reconstructed environment (test track or district), a validated model of a specific sensor, or a virtual ECU or vehicle running the production software. Clarify which one is meant; each needs its own evidence that it matches the real counterpart.

Q.2Why and what if

Why does the child's start trigger use an expression instead of a fixed distance?Why

With a fixed distance, the encounter is critical only at one ego speed: at low speed the child crosses before the car arrives, at high speed the car passes first. Starting the child when the ego is LateralGap/PedSpeed seconds away keeps the would-be impact point in the middle of the bumper at every speed, so every concrete scenario tests the function.

Why can a best-effort tool subscriber see both best-effort and reliable publishers, but a reliable subscriber cannot see a best-effort one?Why

Matching is requested-versus-offered. A reliable writer offers more than a best-effort reader requests, so they match. A best-effort writer cannot provide the retransmissions a reliable reader requests, so DDS refuses the match and raises an incompatible-QoS event.

What if one node in the SIL forgets use_sim_time?What if

Its timers and stamps run on wall time while everything else runs on simulated time. Synchronisers and transform lookups reject its messages as too old or in the future, timeouts fire at the wrong moments, and the run silently degrades instead of crashing. It is also nondeterministic, because wall time differs on every run.

Why did reducing the confirmation from three frames to one barely help in the sweep?Why

The AEB needs the child's lateral velocity to predict that it will enter the car's path, and a velocity needs several measurements. The single-frame track has no useful velocity, so the decision still waits for more frames. The whole perception chain sets the delay, not the confirmation rule alone.

What if the scenario passes in SIL but fails on the HIL rack?What if

Look for what differs: target execution time and dropped frames (free-running timing), a different inference engine or quantisation changing borderline detections, ISP processing of injected raw data, bus timing and signal content, or the injection chain's latency. The HIL result is the more realistic one; reproduce the cause in SIL as a regression test once found.

Why is a flaky virtual test often more important than a stable failure?Why

If the setup is deterministic, flakiness means timing or noise decides the outcome, so the function has almost no margin in that scenario, as in Table 9.1 where free-running timing made 47% of runs fail. Retrying until green hides exactly that risk. If the setup is not deterministic, the flakiness is a toolchain bug that undermines every other result.

What if the simulator's 3D scene and its OpenDRIVE map disagree by 20 cm?What if

The camera sees lane markings where the ground truth does not put them. Lane-keeping or lane-assignment tests are then judged against a wrong truth, and the error looks like a perception bug. Generate both from one source or check their consistency as part of the map pipeline.

Why can't open-loop replay show that the AEB avoids a collision?Why

The recorded vehicle was driven by a human or an older software version; when the new software brakes earlier, the recording still shows the car moving on. The consequence of the decision is not in the data, so only closed-loop simulation, reconstruction-based re-simulation or a physical test can show avoidance.

What if the sweep shows 0 failures?What if

Check the ranges before celebrating. In this chapter the same AEB passes all 99 runs with a walking child and fails 42 with a running one. Zero failures may mean robust software or a parameter space that leaves out the hard cases; the ranges must be justified from the ODD and accident data.

Why does domain randomisation lower accuracy on clean simulated data?Why

The model can no longer rely on the simulator's specific appearance and must spend capacity on invariance to the randomised factors. That costs a little on the clean domain (91.6% to 88.1% in the toy) but generalises to reality (64.8% to 75.1%), which is the domain that matters.

What if a radar sensor model never produces ghost targets?What if

Tests will overestimate the fusion's robustness, because real radars report multipath ghosts near guard rails, tunnels and parked cars, and the tracker or AEB must reject them. The model also cannot show genuine multipath benefits such as detections under a parked vehicle. Its validity domain excludes multipath-heavy scenes until fixed.

Why does every 50 ms of perception latency cost speed in the sweep?Why

When the child appears late, braking starts as soon as the track arrives, so every millisecond of latency is distance travelled at full speed: 50 ms at 45 km/h is 0.63 m, which must then be absorbed by braking. Near the boundary that distance decides between stopping short and an impact.
