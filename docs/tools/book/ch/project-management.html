Process and projects

Automotive project management and ASPICE

How an automotive software project is actually run: the product lifecycle and who answers for what, systems engineering and the V-model, requirements and traceability, Automotive SPICE with the current PAM 4.1 and VDA guidance, verification and test strategy as planning decisions, agile inside an ASPICE world, estimation, risk and change, release and configuration management, and the reporting and escalation work that holds it together. The running example is a Tier 1 supplier's camera ECU programme, from nomination to start of production in 30 months.

1The big picture: why automotive projects run the way they do

An automotive project builds a product that several companies develop together, that must be safe, that will be produced in hundreds of thousands and supported for fifteen years, and whose delivery date is fixed by a car that cannot wait. Almost every rule in this chapter follows from those facts.

1.1What makes an automotive project different

A web team that ships a bad release rolls it back in ten minutes. A camera ECU that ships with a bad release sits in a hundred thousand cars, some of them in countries without a dealer nearby, and the fix may need a workshop visit, a regulator's approval or a recall. That one difference, the cost of being wrong after delivery, explains most of the extra weight in automotive development. The process is heavier because it is cheaper to prove that the product is right before it leaves than to repair it afterwards.

Four other facts shape the work. First, the delivery date is not yours. The OEM's start of production (SOP) is fixed years ahead by a vehicle programme with hundreds of suppliers, a plant, a marketing launch and homologation slots; a supplier who is late does not move the car, it gets replaced or pays for the damage. Second, the product is built by a chain of companies: the OEM specifies and integrates the vehicle, the Tier 1 owns the ECU and its software, and Tier 2 suppliers provide the system-on-chip, the operating system, the image sensor or a perception library. Every interface between them is also a contract. Third, the software is safety-relevant and must be shown to be so, under ISO 26262, ISO 21448 (SOTIF) and, since UN R155 and R156, under cybersecurity and software-update regulations. Fourth, the product lives long: seven years of production and up to fifteen years of spare parts and service, long after the team that built it has moved on.

So the questions a project lead answers every week are not only "are we done?" but also "can we prove what we built, to whom, and with which evidence?", "which supplier is the weak link?" and "if this changes, what else changes?". The tools for those questions are what this chapter is about: the V-model, traceability, ASPICE, configuration management, release gates, risk registers and escalation paths. Each exists because a specific kind of failure happened often enough to become expensive.

Why it matters

When you move from engineer to technical project lead, the work changes from making the product right to making sure the product is demonstrably right on a date someone else chose. Lead roles are defined by exactly that switch: how you plan with uncertain inputs, how you keep evidence without drowning in it, and how you tell a customer bad news.

1.2The product lifecycle from acquisition to end of life

The lifecycle of an automotive component has six phases, and the responsibilities of OEM, Tier 1 and Tier 2 shift between them (Figure 1.1). In acquisition the OEM writes the requirement specification (the Lastenheft), runs the request for quotation and nominates a supplier; the supplier answers with a compliance matrix, a plan and a price. The sourcing chapter covers this phase in detail (RFI to SOP 2.1). After nomination, development runs through sample phases: A-samples are functional prototypes on the bench, B-samples are close to series design and go into the OEM's prototype vehicles, C-samples are built with series tooling and series processes, and the last phase proves the production process (PPAP) before SOP (RFI to SOP 12.1). Then come series production and maintenance, with field monitoring, fixes and over-the-air updates, and finally the time after end of production, when spare parts and occasional safety fixes must still be supplied.

Each phase ends in a decision that is hard to reverse: nomination commits the supplier to a price, B-sample commits the hardware architecture, C-sample commits tooling worth millions, SOP commits the factory. Project management in automotive is mostly the art of reaching each of those decisions with the right information, and of knowing which work must be finished before which decision. The industry formalises this with gates: the OEM's own product development process, APQP (Advanced Product Quality Planning) and in Germany the VDA's Maturity Level Assurance for new parts, which defines maturity levels ML0 to ML7 from innovation release to the hand-over into series (RFI to SOP 12.2).

Interesting detail

Software no longer follows the hardware's rhythm exactly. Hardware matures in a few large steps (A, B, C), because every step costs tooling. Software is released every few weeks into those samples and keeps changing after SOP through updates. A modern automotive plan therefore has two clocks: a slow hardware clock with sample phases and a fast software clock with releases. Chapter 7 shows how agile teams fit between them.

1.3OEM, Tier 1, Tier 2: who answers for what

The most important rule of the supply chain is that responsibility follows the contract, not the code. The OEM has a contract with the Tier 1, and the Tier 1 has contracts with its Tier 2 suppliers. If the Tier 2's SoC software development kit has a defect that delays the camera ECU, the OEM does not care whose code it was: the Tier 1 owns the delivery and must manage its supplier (RFI to SOP 1.1). This is why ASPICE has a process for supplier monitoring (ACQ.4) and why safety and security standards require interface agreements that say who does which part of the safety and security work.

The OEM, in turn, owns what only it can know: the vehicle, its electrical architecture, how the function behaves for the driver, homologation and the overall safety concept. A good split of responsibilities is written down early, in the development interface agreement for safety (DIA), the cybersecurity interface agreement (CIA), and a responsibility matrix (often called RASI or RACI: responsible, accountable or approving, supporting, informed or consulted) for every major work product. A missing line in that matrix is a typical root cause of late surprises: both sides assumed the other one would validate the warning behaviour in rain, and nobody did.

Pitfall

"The Tier 2 is late" is not a status a customer accepts. It is a risk you should have registered months earlier with a mitigation, such as an early evaluation kit, a fallback implementation, or a contractual milestone with the supplier that falls well before your own.

1.4Roles inside the supplier's project

Inside a Tier 1, a programme of this size has a small set of roles, each with a counterpart at the customer. The project lead (or programme manager) owns the overall plan, budget and customer relationship. The technical project lead owns the technical content: the system architecture, the integration plan, the technical risks; on smaller projects this is the same person as the project lead. Under them, sub-project leads run software, hardware, mechanics, and increasingly machine learning (data and training). A system architect owns the system requirements and architecture, a test manager the verification strategy and its environments.

A second group of roles exists because some judgements must not be made by the people who did the work. Quality assurance (SUP.1 in ASPICE) checks that work products and processes follow the agreed rules, and must be able to escalate independently of the project lead. The configuration manager controls baselines and releases. The safety manager and the cybersecurity manager own the safety and security plans and cases, again with a degree of independence that the standards prescribe (Functional safety 1.4, 10.3). Around the project sit the line functions that lend people to it, purchasing and supplier quality for the Tier 2s, and the customer-facing key account manager who owns the commercial side (RFI to SOP 15.1).

Talk track

"In a Tier 1 project I would separate three kinds of responsibility: who decides the plan and scope (project lead), who decides the technical solution (technical lead and architects), and who checks independently that we did what we said (quality, safety and security managers). The third group is not overhead; it is what lets the customer trust our evidence without re-doing it."

1.5The running example

The chapter follows one programme. A Tier 1 has been nominated to supply the camera ECU for an OEM's next vehicle platform: four surround-view cameras, a vision SoC, rear-view and top-view display, and a new function that warns the driver of pedestrians behind the car when reversing, based on a neural network. It is the successor of the surround-view system that the ASPICE chapter uses as its reference project (ASPICE 3.1), so the SWE examples there apply here too. The SoC vendor supplies the board support package and the neural-network accelerator SDK; an operating system vendor supplies the OS; a lens supplier provides the camera optics. Nomination is month 0 (M0), the first A-sample is due at M6, B-sample at M12, C-sample at M20 and SOP at M30. The OEM requires Automotive SPICE capability level 2 in the VDA scope, with an assessment around the B phase, and the pedestrian warning is safety-relevant.

The numbers in the chapter (thirty months, three to seven risks, ten-week planned durations) are typical, not taken from a specific project; where a number comes from a calculation, the script is in the chapter's experiments folder.

Not in this chapter: the commercial side of acquisition (quotes, costing, contracts) is in RFI to SOP 4–9; leadership and people skills belong to the planned chapter on senior engineering and technical leadership.

2Systems engineering and the V-model

A camera ECU is not a software project with some hardware attached. It is a system of optics, sensors, electronics, software, a trained network and a vehicle interface, and each part constrains the others. Systems engineering is the discipline that decides, before the parts are built, what each part must do so that the whole works.

2.1Why systems engineering exists

Suppose each team simply optimises its own part. The optics team picks a wide lens for coverage; the software team picks a network that needs sharp pixels at the image edge; the hardware team picks a SoC whose accelerator is fast enough for the network at the centre resolution. Each choice is reasonable, and together they do not work: the edges of a fisheye image are blurred and compressed, the network misses pedestrians at the side, and the SoC cannot afford the higher resolution that would fix it. Nobody made a mistake inside their own box. The mistake was that nobody owned the interaction.

Systems engineering owns it. It starts from what the stakeholders need (warn the driver of a pedestrian behind the car), turns that into system requirements that can be verified (detect a pedestrian of at least 0.9 m height within 3 m behind the bumper, warn within 300 ms), and then designs a system architecture that splits the work into elements (camera, ECU hardware, software, ML model) with requirements and budgets for each: how much latency each stage may use, how much power the ECU may draw, what image quality the lens must deliver at the edge. The budgets are the important part. They turn one hard system problem into several smaller problems that can be solved independently, and they show early when the sum does not fit.

Why it matters

Most expensive late problems in vehicle projects are system problems: timing that only fails with all features on, a thermal limit reached in the plastic housing, a camera that is fine alone and wrong in the vehicle. They are found late because they only exist once the parts meet. A project lead cannot solve them, but can insist that someone owns the budgets and that integration starts early enough to find the misfit.

2.2The V-model: specification levels and their mirror

The V-model draws this decomposition as a letter V (Figure 2.1). The left side goes down from stakeholder needs to system requirements, system architecture, software requirements, software architecture and finally detailed design and units. Each level refines the one above. The right side goes up again: units are verified, integrated into components and verified, integrated into the software and verified against the software requirements, integrated with the hardware into the system and verified against the system requirements, and at the top the finished product is validated against the stakeholders' needs. Each level on the right has a mirror on the left: the level it is checked against.

Automotive SPICE maps its engineering processes onto this picture: SYS.1 to SYS.5 for the system levels, SWE.1 to SWE.6 for software, HWE.1 to HWE.4 for hardware, MLE.1 to MLE.4 for machine learning, and VAL.1 for validation. The ASPICE chapter explains the software side in depth (ASPICE 2.1); for a project lead, the V matters for three reasons. It defines the work products that must exist (one specification per level on the left, one verification specification and report per level on the right). It defines the order of integration (you cannot verify the system before the software exists). And it defines what can be checked against what, which is the backbone of traceability in Chapter 3.

In practice

Write the verification criteria at the same time as the requirement, on the left side of the V, not when the test team gets to it. "Warn within 300 ms" becomes testable only with a definition: measured from which event, under which load, how many runs, what pass rate. Writing that down early forces the ambiguity out while it is still cheap.

2.3The V is a logic, not a calendar

The most common misreading of the V-model is to read it as a timeline: first all requirements, then all design, then all code, then all tests, one pass from left to right. Projects that run like that discover their system problems in the last quarter, which is exactly what the V was meant to avoid. The V describes how levels relate, not when work happens. In a real programme the V is run many times (tab 2 of Figure 2.1): once for each sample and software release, sometimes once per feature. The A-sample V is deep, because most of the design is new; the later Vs are shallow, because they carry changes and fixes, but each still runs its whole right side, because a change at the bottom can break a requirement at the top. That is why regression testing on every level is a planning item, not a detail.

The requirements baseline at the top of tab 2 moves too. After the A-sample the OEM learns from its first prototype and changes requirements; in the C phase the scope is frozen and only fixes are accepted. A plan that assumes requirements are complete at M3 is wrong; a plan that has no date after which requirements are frozen is worse. The project lead's job is to agree with the customer when each kind of change is still possible and at what price, which Chapter 8 returns to.

Pitfall

Agile teams sometimes argue that the V-model is waterfall and therefore obsolete. It is neither. The V says that a software requirement must be verified against its specification and traced to its parent; it says nothing about doing all requirements first. A team with two-week sprints can, and in automotive must, satisfy the V in every sprint.

2.4System requirements and architecture from the project lead's view

Two system-level processes deserve a project lead's attention. In system requirements analysis (SYS.2) the customer's requirements, often thousands of lines in a Lastenheft with a mix of real requirements, wishes and references to OEM standards, are turned into the supplier's own system requirements: structured, unambiguous, verifiable, with a status and a link to their source. In system architectural design (SYS.3) those requirements are allocated to elements and the interfaces between them are defined: which requirement the camera fulfils, which the SoC software, which the network, which the microcontroller that talks to the vehicle bus.

What the project lead watches here is not the content of each requirement but four signals. The first is the share of customer requirements that have an agreed status (accepted, accepted with deviation, rejected, clarification needed), because every open clarification is an unpriced risk. The second is whether every system requirement is allocated to an element, because an unallocated requirement is work nobody has planned. The third is whether the interface specifications with the OEM and with each Tier 2 are agreed and versioned, because interfaces are where companies meet and where most integration problems come from. The fourth is whether the technical budgets (latency, CPU and accelerator load, memory, power, thermal) have owners and are measured from the first build onwards.

Talk track

"I treat system architecture as the place where a project is planned technically. Every element in it becomes a work package with an owner, every interface becomes a contract with another team or supplier, and every budget becomes a measurement we track in each release. If the architecture is vague, the plan is vague, however detailed the Gantt chart looks."

3Requirements management and traceability

Requirements change, often and for good reasons. Requirements management exists so that each change is noticed, priced, decided and carried through to code and tests; traceability is the mechanism that makes "what else does this change affect?" answerable in minutes rather than weeks.

3.1Why requirements need managing

A requirement in an automotive project is not just a sentence. It is a commitment to a customer, the basis of a price, the input of a design decision and the reference of a test. When the OEM raises the pedestrian warning range from 3 m to 5 m, that one number touches the network's training data, the image resolution at the edge, the post-processing, the timing budget, six verification measures and possibly the price. If the change arrives as an email to one engineer, it may be implemented without being priced, or priced without being implemented, or implemented in software while the test still checks 3 m and passes for the wrong reason.

Requirements management prevents that by treating requirements as controlled items: each has an identifier, a version, an owner, a status and links to where it comes from and where it goes. Changes go through change control (Section 8.6), and every version is part of a baseline (Section 9.2). This sounds bureaucratic until you have been in a customer meeting where both sides hold a different version of the same specification.

3.2From Lastenheft to software requirement

Requirements in the running example exist on four levels. The OEM's stakeholder requirements are in its Lastenheft and its referenced standards; the supplier answers each in a compliance statement, which becomes a contractual document (RFI to SOP 5.2). The supplier's system requirements restate what the supplier commits to, in its own words and at a level it can verify. Software, hardware and ML requirements are derived from the system requirements and the architecture; ML requirements include data requirements, such as the conditions and the population that the training data must cover (ASPICE 11.7). Below that, the architecture and detailed design describe how the requirements are met.

Every requirement carries a few attributes that the project uses daily: a unique, never-reused identifier; a type (functional, performance, interface, safety, security, regulatory); a status in a simple workflow (draft, in review, agreed, implemented, verified, rejected); the release in which it is planned; the safety level (ASIL) if it has one; and a verification criterion. With these attributes, a requirements tool can answer project questions directly: how many requirements are planned for B2 and how many of them are verified, which safety requirements are still in draft, which customer requirements are still "clarification needed".

In practice

OEM and supplier usually exchange requirements in the ReqIF format (Requirements Interchange Format, an OMG standard), so that each side can keep its own tool while the identifiers and statuses round-trip. The first exchange is always painful: attribute mappings, rich text, tables and images. Do a trial exchange in the first month, not on the day of the first official delivery.

3.3Bidirectional traceability, step by step

Traceability means that every item is linked to the items it comes from and the items that realise and verify it. Bidirectional means that you can follow the links both ways, and that both directions answer different questions. Step through Figure 3.1 to see them on a small slice of the running example: one OEM requirement, its system and software requirements, the architecture elements and units that implement them, and the verification measures that check each one.

Two details make traceability work in practice. The first is granularity. Linking every line of code to a requirement is useless; linking a requirement to the architecture element and the unit that implement it, and to the test that verifies it, is enough to answer the questions above. ASPICE 4.0 asks for traceability and consistency together in one base practice per process (ASPICE 13.2): a link is only worth something if the linked items actually agree. The second detail is that the links must be maintained by the people who change the items, as part of the change, not by a separate team at the end. A trace matrix produced the week before an assessment is a known finding (ASPICE 12.5).

Why it matters

Traceability is also the safety argument's skeleton: ISO 26262 requires that every safety requirement is traced to its source and its verification (Functional safety 7.4). One set of links can serve the project (impact analysis), the assessor (ASPICE) and the safety case, if it is designed once for all three.

3.4Tools and daily practice

Requirements live in an application lifecycle management (ALM) or requirements tool: IBM DOORS Next, Siemens Polarion, PTC Codebeamer and Jama Connect are common in automotive, and many teams link them with their issue tracker and test management. The tool matters less than four habits. Baselines: at each release, the requirement set is frozen as a named version, so that "which requirements does B2 implement?" has one answer forever. Reviews: requirements change status only after a review with the right people (the architect, the tester who must verify it, for safety requirements the safety manager), and the review is recorded. Suspect links: they are cleared by someone who looked, not in bulk. Metrics in the status report: coverage and open items are tracked weekly, so they trend rather than surprise.

For machine learning, the "requirement" side includes data. The dataset that trained model v3.2 is identified by a version, and that version is part of the trace: if the warning range changes, the data requirement changes, and the test set must contain pedestrians at 5 m (NN training 2.4). In ASPICE this is SUP.11, machine learning data management.

Pitfall

Copying the OEM's Lastenheft one-to-one as "system requirements" looks efficient and creates a project that cannot be verified: half of the lines are not requirements (they are explanations, references or wishes), many are not testable as written, and none are in the supplier's architecture language. Restate, link to the source, and keep the compliance statement for the contractual view.

3.5Requirement quality and the most common failures

A good requirement is necessary, unambiguous, verifiable, feasible, consistent with the others and states what, not how. In practice the failures are always the same few. Vague words ("fast", "robust", "in all conditions") hide a number nobody wanted to commit to. Compound requirements ("detect and warn within 300 ms and log the event") cannot be verified as one item and fail as a whole. Missing conditions ("detect pedestrians" in daylight only? at what minimum contrast?) make the test either impossible or arbitrary. And implicit requirements, the ones everyone assumes, appear in the vehicle as defects: the warning must not trigger when the trailer hitch camera view is active, the system must start within two seconds of reverse gear.

For a project lead, requirement quality is a schedule issue. An unclear requirement is a decision postponed into the integration phase, when it is most expensive. A short checklist in the review (one statement, one number with unit and tolerance, conditions stated, verification method named) prevents most of these, and the same checklist is what the safety chapter recommends for safety requirements (Functional safety 3.4).

Talk track

"Bidirectional traceability is two questions: forward, is every requirement implemented and verified; backward, does everything we built have a reason. The day it pays for itself is the day a customer changes a requirement: the suspect links give us the impact set, which gives us the effort, the retest scope and the price, the same afternoon."

4Automotive SPICE: structure, versions and assessments

Automotive SPICE is how an OEM judges, before and during a project, whether a supplier's development process can be trusted to produce what it promises. The ASPICE chapter of this book goes deep into the software processes and Level 2; this chapter recaps the model briefly and looks at it from the project's side: what the full model contains, what changed up to PAM 4.1, and how to plan and survive an assessment.

4.1What ASPICE is, in one page

An OEM buys software it cannot inspect line by line, from suppliers it cannot supervise day by day. What it can check is how the supplier works: whether requirements are analysed, designs documented, tests planned and traced, changes controlled and the project steered. Automotive SPICE (ASPICE) is a process assessment model built for exactly that check. It was derived around 2005 from the generic ISO/IEC 15504 (SPICE) by a group of OEMs, and it is maintained today by the VDA's Quality Management Center (VDA QMC). An assessment rates the processes of one real project, not the company's process handbook; the result is a capability level per process.

The model has two dimensions (ASPICE 1.2). The process dimension says what must be achieved: each process has a purpose, outcomes and base practices, such as "specify software requirements" or "ensure consistency and establish bidirectional traceability". The capability dimension says how well the process is managed: Level 1 means the outcomes are achieved, Level 2 that the process is planned, monitored and adjusted and its work products controlled, Level 3 that the project uses an organisation-wide standard process, tailored. Each process attribute is rated N, P, L or F (not, partially, largely, fully achieved, at thresholds of 15, 50 and 85 percent), and a level is reached when its attributes are at least L and all lower ones F (ASPICE 1.4).

Why it matters

For the project, the target level is a contractual requirement like any other. "Level 2 in the VDA scope at the B-sample assessment" costs effort, needs people (quality assurance, configuration management) and has a date. It belongs in the plan and the budget from the quote onwards, not in a clean-up campaign before the assessor arrives.

4.2The process reference model and the VDA scope

Since version 4.0 the model contains 32 processes in three categories (Figure 4.1). The primary life cycle processes cover acquisition and supply (ACQ.4 supplier monitoring, SPL.2 product release), the engineering disciplines (SYS, SWE, HWE, MLE) and validation (VAL.1). The supporting processes are quality assurance (SUP.1), configuration management (SUP.8), problem resolution (SUP.9), change request management (SUP.10) and ML data management (SUP.11). The organizational processes are project management (MAN.3), risk management (MAN.5), measurement (MAN.6), process improvement (PIM.3) and reuse management (REU.2).

No OEM assesses all 32. The usual scope is the VDA scope, defined in the VDA guidelines, which since 4.0 works like a set of plug-ins: project management, quality assurance, configuration, problem and change management are always in, supplier monitoring is in when the project has suppliers, and the engineering groups are added according to what the product contains. A software-only supplier is assessed on SWE (and usually SYS); a supplier of a complete ECU with a neural network adds HWE, MLE and SUP.11. Processes outside the VDA scope, such as SPL.2, MAN.5, VAL.1 or REU.2, are assessed only when the customer asks for them. The running example is an ECU with hardware, software and an ML model, so its assessment covers all the coloured processes in the figure.

Interesting detail

The older "HIS scope", from the German OEM initiative that started the model, is still quoted in contracts written by people who learned ASPICE ten years ago. It roughly equals today's basic scope with SYS and SWE. If a contract names it for a 4.x assessment, clarify the intended scope with the customer in writing at the start of the project.

4.3Capability levels from the project's view

For a project lead, Level 1 is about the engineering: the work products exist and achieve the process outcomes. Level 2 is about steering, and much of it lands on the project lead's desk. PA 2.1, performance management, asks that each process has objectives and a strategy, is planned, has its resources and responsibilities defined, is monitored against the plan and adjusted when it deviates, and that the interfaces between the people involved are managed. PA 2.2, work product management, asks that the requirements for work products (content, structure, review criteria) are defined, that work products are identified, versioned and controlled, and that they are reviewed and adjusted (ASPICE 10.1, 10.2).

Level 3 moves the source of the process from the project to the organisation. PA 3.1 asks for a defined standard process with roles, competencies, infrastructure and a tailoring guideline; PA 3.2 asks that the project actually uses it, tailored according to the guideline, and that data from the project flows back to improve it. For a project lead, Level 3 is mostly good news: templates, checklists and a plan structure exist and do not have to be invented per project. It also means that tailoring decisions ("we do not do a separate detailed design for generated code") must be justified against the guideline and recorded.

Pitfall

Level 2 fails most often not on missing documents but on missing adjustment. A plan that was never updated although the project slipped three months shows planning without monitoring. Assessors ask "when did you last change the plan, and why?" A good answer names a date, a reason and the people informed.

Pitfall

Descriptions of the 4.1 edits in this section rely on published summaries, some of them written from review drafts; the official PAM 4.1 text from VDA QMC is the authority. When a customer quotes a rating rule, ask for the rule number in the guidelines edition named in the assessment plan.

4.5Planning an assessment as a small project

An external assessment is commissioned by the OEM and run by an intacs-certified lead assessor with one or more co-assessors. It has a predictable shape (ASPICE 12.1). About two to three months before, the assessment is planned: the OEM and the lead assessor agree the scope (processes and target level), the project and its parts that serve as evidence, the PAM and guidelines versions, the dates and the interviewees. The supplier then prepares: an internal pre-assessment or gap analysis, a list of evidence per process, and a "golden thread", one requirement followed from the customer's text through every level to its test result and release, which shows the assessors in half an hour that the chain exists (ASPICE 12.2). The on-site part takes a few days of interviews and evidence review, process by process. The assessors then rate and present preliminary findings, followed by a written report with strengths, weaknesses and ratings. If the target is missed, the supplier writes an improvement plan, and the OEM usually asks for a re-assessment of the weak processes some months later.

In the running example the plan has three dates (Figure 8.1): an internal pre-assessment at M9, after the first full A-cycle, to find the gaps while there is time; the OEM assessment at M15, on the B2 release; and a re-check at M21 if needed. The preparation is assigned like any other work package, with an owner per process (usually the person who runs it in the project) and a quality engineer who coordinates.

In practice

The best preparation is a project that produces its evidence as a side effect of its work: requirements reviewed in the tool, pull requests with reviews and CI results, release notes generated from the baseline, a risk list updated in the weekly meeting. Projects that "prepare evidence" for an assessment are recognisable, because every document carries a date from the last two weeks.

Talk track

"An assessment does not rate our documents, it rates whether our project actually works the way we say. So I plan it like a release: scope and date agreed early, a pre-assessment half a year before so we can still change how we work, and evidence that comes out of daily work rather than being produced for the assessor."

5.2Hardware and machine learning in the same plan

Hardware and machine learning follow different physics from software, and the plan has to respect both. Hardware moves in a few expensive steps: each sample phase needs a board design, parts with lead times of weeks or months, a build at a plant and a verification campaign. A hardware change after the C-sample design freeze means new tooling and new validation. Software that depends on a hardware feature therefore has a hard date: the feature must be in the B-sample design if it is to be tested in the vehicle.

Machine learning has a third rhythm. Its progress is driven by data: recording campaigns in the right seasons and conditions, labelling, training runs that take days, and evaluations that only mean something on a test set the model has never seen. Two consequences matter for planning. Data campaigns have long lead times (a winter campaign must be planned in summer), and model quality is not linear in effort: the last percent of recall at night may need a new data campaign rather than more training. The running example therefore puts ML data and training on the critical path (Figure 8.1) and freezes the model at M22, four months before the SOP release, so that the frozen model can be integrated, quantised, verified on target and validated in the fleet.

Why it matters

ASPICE 4.0 made ML development assessable (MLE.1–4, SUP.11), which also means it must be planned and evidenced like the rest: ML requirements with KPIs, a defined training process with reproducible runs, a model test against the requirements on independent data, and versioned datasets (ASPICE 11.7, NN training 2.2).

5.3Safety and cybersecurity as parallel tracks

Functional safety (ISO 26262), safety of the intended functionality (ISO 21448) and cybersecurity (ISO/SAE 21434) each add their own life cycle, plan, work products and independent checks on top of the engineering processes (Functional safety 1.1, 1.3). For the project lead they behave like parallel tracks with their own milestones: the safety plan and DIA early, the safety concept before the architecture is frozen, confirmation reviews and the functional safety assessment before release, the safety case as a release condition. The security track has its threat analysis (TARA), cybersecurity concept, penetration tests and the cybersecurity case, and under UN R155 the OEM needs evidence from its suppliers for its own type approval.

The practical rule is to integrate, not duplicate. A safety requirement is a requirement in the same tool, with an ASIL attribute, traced and verified like the others; a safety review is a review with additional participants; the safety case cites the same verification reports as the release. Projects that run safety as a separate document stream end up with two versions of the truth and a confirmation review that finds them.

6Verification, validation and test strategy

Testing is the largest single cost item in most automotive software projects and the one most often under-planned. The test strategy decides what is checked on which level, in which environment, how often and by whom; those are planning decisions with lead times, budgets and capacity limits, not details for the test team to sort out later.

6.1Verification versus validation

The short version is the classic one: verification asks "did we build the product right?", validation asks "did we build the right product?". Verification checks a work product against its specification: the software against the software requirements, the system against the system requirements. It can be done with tests, but also with reviews, analyses, simulations and inspections; ASPICE 4.0 therefore speaks of verification measures, each with a pass criterion. Validation checks the finished product against its intended use, in the intended environment, with the intended users: does the pedestrian warning actually help a driver reversing out of a supermarket car park in the rain, without so many false alarms that the driver switches it off?

The difference matters because a product can pass every verification and still fail validation. If the requirement said "detect pedestrians of at least 0.9 m" and children who crouch behind the car are smaller, the system is verified and wrong. Validation finds errors in the requirements themselves, which no amount of verification can. That is why ASPICE 4.0 added VAL.1, and why SOTIF (ISO 21448) asks for validation of the intended functionality in the real world, with scenario catalogues and field data.

Talk track

"Verification proves we met the specification; validation proves the specification was right. Both are needed because a requirement can be wrong. In a perception function, validation is where the long tail of real scenes shows up, so I plan fleet time and scenario coverage as early as test benches."

6.2Test levels: unit, integration, system, acceptance

The test levels follow the right side of the V. Unit verification (SWE.4) checks each unit against its detailed design, usually with unit tests in CI, static analysis and, for safety-relevant code, structural coverage targets that depend on the ASIL. Integration verification (SWE.5, SYS.4) checks that integrated parts work together through their interfaces: the detector and the warning manager, the SoC software and the microcontroller, the ECU and the vehicle network. System or software verification (SWE.6, SYS.5) checks the integrated product against its requirements, release by release. Acceptance is the customer's check: the OEM verifies the delivered ECU in its own system test and vehicles, and accepts or rejects the release.

Each level catches a different class of error, and an error caught on a lower level is cheaper: a unit test fails in minutes and points to one function; the same defect found in the vehicle costs a test drive, an analysis across companies and a new release. The often-quoted ratios (ten times more expensive per level) are folklore rather than measurement, but the direction is reliable, and it gives the most important rule of a test strategy: test each property on the lowest level where it can be tested, and use the expensive levels only for what cannot be seen lower down.

6.3X-in-the-loop as a planning decision

The test environments add a second axis to the levels (Figure 6.1). In model-in-the-loop (MIL) an algorithm runs as a model or prototype against simulated or recorded inputs. In software-in-the-loop (SIL) the production code runs on a PC or in the cloud, often replaying thousands of hours of recorded camera data overnight. In processor-in-the-loop (PIL) the code runs on the target processor or an evaluation board, which finds compiler, numeric and timing issues. In hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) the real ECU is connected to a real-time simulator that emulates the vehicle network, power supply, sensors and faults (Production integration 10.3). Finally there are prototype vehicles and the validation fleet.

For the project lead, the figure is a planning tool. Every environment has a lead time and a capacity, and both must be planned. A HIL rack needs the ECU's pin-out, the simulation models and the rest-bus configuration, and takes months to build; if it is not ready when the A-sample arrives, the first release is tested on a desk with a power supply. Capacity is arithmetic. A full regression of 1,200 HIL tests at three minutes each takes 60 hours, or three rack-days at 20 usable hours a day; with four hardware variants and two release candidates per release that is 24 rack-days, so finishing it in a ten-day release window needs 2.4 racks before any developer uses one. The same arithmetic for vehicles is harsher: a prototype vehicle is shared by many suppliers and available for a few days per month.

Why it matters

The shift towards SIL and cloud replay in recent years is economic, not fashionable: a SIL hour is cheap and parallel, so moving a test from HIL to SIL frees the scarce rack for what only the rack can test (I/O, diagnostics, timing on the real network). The technical setup of these environments belongs to the planned chapter on simulation and virtual validation; the decision of which test runs where belongs in the test strategy.

6.4The test strategy: what it decides

A test strategy is a short document, a few pages and a table, that answers the planning questions once for the whole project. Which properties are verified on which level and in which environment. What the entry and exit criteria of each level are (no unit is integrated without passing its unit tests and static analysis; no release goes to the customer with an open severity-1 defect). How regression works: what runs on every merge (build, unit tests, a SIL smoke test), every night (full SIL replay), for every release candidate (full HIL regression, release verification of all requirements in scope), and how the scope of a partial regression is chosen after a change (from the impact analysis in Section 3.3). Which coverage is required: 100 % of requirements with a verification measure, structural coverage per ASIL, scenario coverage for the ML function. Who verifies what: the supplier's verification versus the OEM's acceptance, written down so nothing falls between them. And which environments are needed when, with their owners.

In ASPICE 4.0 the verification strategies are no longer separate base practices of SWE.4 to SWE.6; they are expected as part of the process planning in GP 2.1.1 at Level 2 (ASPICE 13.2). The content has not changed: an assessor will still ask how regression is chosen and why a requirement is verified in SIL rather than on HIL.

Pitfall

"Everything is tested on HIL" sounds thorough and is a capacity plan that cannot work: the racks become the bottleneck of every release, testers queue for them, and the full regression is cut in the last week. A strategy that says what does not need HIL is the one that gets the important HIL tests done.

7Agile in an ASPICE world

Most automotive software teams now work in sprints, and most automotive customers still buy against fixed dates, fixed samples and an ASPICE level. Both are compatible, but only if the project is designed for it: agile cadences inside the hardware and release cadences, and evidence produced by the agile practices instead of beside them.

7.1Why agile arrived, and where it collides

Agile methods came to automotive for the same reason they came everywhere: requirements change, feedback from working software is worth more than a perfect specification, and big integration at the end of a long phase fails. Perception software is a good example. Nobody knows at M0 which scenes will be hard for the pedestrian detector; the team learns it from the first data, the first model and the first vehicle drives. A process that freezes the requirements at M3 and tests at M18 would learn far too late.

The collisions are real, though. The customer contract fixes scope, dates and price, while agile assumes the scope can flex. Hardware samples arrive in large steps that no sprint can accelerate. Safety standards and ASPICE expect requirements, architecture and verification evidence for each release, while a naive reading of the Agile Manifesto ("working software over comprehensive documentation") suggests skipping them. And many teams and companies are involved, so a team's sprint plan is useless if the Tier 2's SDK release is not in it. The answer that works is not "agile or V-model" but agile execution inside a planned frame: the frame fixes the release dates, the content commitments and the evidence; the teams decide inside it how to get there.

7.2Scrum and Kanban in brief

Scrum organises work in sprints of fixed length, usually two weeks in automotive teams. It has three accountabilities: the product owner, who orders the product backlog and decides what is built next; the developers, who decide how and commit to a sprint goal; and the Scrum Master, who keeps the process working and removes impediments. Each sprint has planning, a daily stand-up, a review where the increment is shown to stakeholders, and a retrospective where the team improves its way of working. The definition of done is the most important Scrum artefact for automotive: it says what "finished" means for every backlog item, and that is where the ASPICE evidence lives (Section 7.4).

Kanban has no sprints. Work flows through visible stages (analysis, implementation, review, verification), each with a work-in-progress limit, and the team optimises the flow. It suits teams whose work arrives continuously and unpredictably, such as integration teams, problem-resolution teams after SOP or a team that supports many projects. Little's law explains why the limits help: average lead time equals work in progress divided by throughput. A team that finishes ten items a week with thirty in progress has a lead time of three weeks; limiting work in progress to fifteen halves it, without anybody working faster.

Interesting detail

The product owner role is harder in automotive than in a web company, because the real "customer" is a chain: the OEM's function owner, the OEM's project lead, the supplier's own system architect and safety manager all have legitimate demands on the backlog. Successful projects give the product owner a clear mandate and a short line to the system architect, and keep the contractual scope visible in the backlog as a fixed part that is not reprioritised away.

7.3Nested cadences: sprints, increments, releases and samples

The practical way to combine agile and an automotive programme is a set of nested cadences (Figure 7.1). Two-week sprints produce working, tested software on the main branch. Every few sprints, an increment (a program increment in SAFe terms, about a quarter long) is planned jointly by all teams, including the Tier 2s' representatives where possible, so that dependencies are visible and agreed. Customer releases follow the OEM's delivery plan, here roughly one per quarter, each a controlled baseline with release note and verification report. And the hardware samples set the slowest rhythm: the release that goes into the B-sample vehicles must work on B-sample hardware.

Aligning increments with releases keeps planning honest: the increment planning answers "what will be in B2?" with the teams who will build it, and the result is the release content the project lead commits to the customer. Freezes protect the release without stopping development: from feature freeze, the release branch accepts only completion and fixes of agreed features; from code freeze, only fixes approved by the change control board. Main keeps moving, which is why the configuration management of Chapter 9 is a precondition for agile delivery, not a separate topic.

In practice

Put the Tier 2's deliveries into the increment plan as explicit backlog items with dates, owned by the person who manages that supplier. If the SoC vendor's SDK 3.2 is needed for the B2 feature set, the dependency should be visible on the planning board, with a fallback, before the increment starts.

7.5Scaling, and the failure modes

A programme with ten or more teams needs a way to coordinate them. The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) is the most common choice in automotive companies: teams form an agile release train, which plans together in a two-day program increment planning event every eight to twelve weeks, with system demos and an "innovation and planning" iteration at the end of each increment. Its strength is the visible, joint planning of dependencies; its weakness is weight, with many roles and ceremonies that small programmes do not need. Lighter alternatives such as LeSS, or simply a regular cross-team planning with a shared dependency board, work as well for a handful of teams.

The failure modes are well known. Water-Scrum-fall: sprints inside, but requirements fixed up front and testing deferred to the end, so the team has the ceremonies and none of the feedback. Documentation debt: requirements and architecture not updated during sprints, followed by a "documentation sprint" before the assessment, which produces exactly the late, unreviewed documents assessors look for. Velocity as a target: story points turned into a performance metric, which inflates estimates and destroys their use for planning. And ignoring the hardware clock: a backlog that assumes features can move freely between releases, when some of them depend on a sample that arrives once.

Pitfall

Announcing "we are agile now" to an OEM that has a fixed delivery plan and an ASPICE target sounds like "we will not commit to anything". Present it the other way round: fixed releases and content commitments to the customer, agile methods inside to meet them, with evidence by design.

Engineering workflow (branching, code review, CI practice) is the subject of the planned chapter on software quality and engineering workflow; this chapter covers only how it connects to planning and evidence.

8Planning: scope, estimation, schedule, risk and change

A plan in an automotive project is a set of commitments made under uncertainty to people in other companies. Its value is not that it is right, which it never is for long, but that it shows early when reality deviates, and what follows from the deviation.

8.1Scope and work breakdown

Planning starts with scope: what the project must deliver, to whom, by when, and what it explicitly does not deliver. In the running example the scope comes from the nomination, the Lastenheft with the agreed compliance statement, and the delivery plan of samples and software releases; the exclusions written in the quote (RFI to SOP 6.3) are part of it. The scope is then broken down into a work breakdown structure: deliverables first (A-sample ECU, software release B1, safety case, PPAP documentation), then the work packages that produce them, each small enough to estimate and to give to one owner.

The architecture is the best guide to a good breakdown (Section 2.4). Work packages that follow the elements and interfaces of the architecture have clear owners and clear handovers; work packages that follow departments ("software", "test") hide the integration work between them. ASPICE's MAN.3 asks for the same things in its own words: the scope of work, the life cycle model, the feasibility of the plan given the resources, and estimates of effort and resources.

8.2Estimation: three-point, reference class and velocity

Estimates are wrong; the useful question is how wrong, and in which direction. Engineering work is right-skewed: it can finish a little earlier than expected and much later, because surprises add time and rarely remove it. An estimate that gives only the most likely duration hides that skew. The three-point estimate asks for an optimistic, a most likely and a pessimistic value; PERT combines them as mean = (O + 4M + P) / 6 and standard deviation ≈ (P − O) / 6. For the pedestrian-detection post-processing, estimated at 6, 8 and 16 weeks, the mean is 9 weeks, a week above the most likely value, with a standard deviation of about 1.7 weeks.

The skew adds up along a chain of tasks. Four sequential work packages with most likely durations of 4, 6, 8 and 3 weeks sum to 21 weeks, but their means sum to 23.7 weeks, and an 80 % commitment is about 25.6 weeks. A plan built from the most likely values is therefore a plan that will be late more often than not, before anything has gone wrong. Two other techniques correct for the optimism of experts. Reference-class estimation asks how long similar work actually took in past projects (the last surround-view project needed eleven months from A-sample to B1, not the six that were planned) and starts from that. Velocity, in agile teams, measures how much work a team actually finishes per sprint and projects the backlog forward with it; it works well inside a stable team and badly across teams or for work unlike the past.

Numbers

Three-point estimate 6 / 8 / 16 weeks: PERT mean 9.0 weeks, standard deviation 1.7 weeks. Four tasks with most likely 4 + 6 + 8 + 3 = 21 weeks: sum of means 23.7 weeks, standard deviation 2.3 weeks, 80 % point about 25.6 weeks (computed in the chapter's experiments).

Pitfall

Adding a safety margin to every task makes the plan look safe and does not make it safe, because people tend to use the time they are given. Keep task estimates realistic and put the margin where it protects the commitment: as a visible buffer before the milestone, owned by the project lead.

8.4Dependencies and merge bias

Dependencies are where plans break. A task that waits for one input is late if that input is late; an integration milestone that waits for five inputs is late if any of them is late. This is merge bias: the probability that all inputs arrive on time is the product of the individual probabilities, so it falls quickly with the number of inputs, even if each input is reasonably safe. Figure 8.2 lets you see the effect. Each input was planned at its most likely duration of 10 weeks and can finish two weeks earlier or several weeks later; the integration can start only when the last one arrives.

With the default settings the numbers are sobering. One input planned at its most likely value arrives on time with only 25 % probability, because the most likely value of a right-skewed task is below its median (11.1 weeks). With five such inputs, the chance that all arrive by week 10 is 0.1 %. The integration start is 50 % likely by week 13.5 and 80 % likely by week 14.6, so an honest 80 % commitment needs about 4.6 weeks of buffer after the planned date. Even with symmetric inputs (worst case +2 weeks, so each input is a coin flip), five inputs all arrive on time only 3 % of the time.

The figure explains several habits of experienced project leads. They reduce the number of hard dependencies into a milestone (integrate the Tier 2's SDK early in a stub form instead of waiting for the final drop). They put the buffer in front of the merge point, not inside each task. They pull the inputs that have the longest tails (new suppliers, new technology, data campaigns) earliest. And they track the inputs to a milestone individually, because "integration starts in week 10" is only as true as its least reliable input.

Why it matters

The model assumes independent inputs. In reality delays are often correlated (the same overloaded expert is needed by three inputs, or one late SDK delays everything built on it), which makes the tail even longer. If someone presents a plan where five supplier deliveries converge on a sample date with no buffer, this is the argument for changing it.

8.5Risk management and the risk register

A risk is an uncertain event that, if it happens, affects the project's objectives. Risk management (MAN.5 in ASPICE) is the routine of finding risks early, judging them, deciding what to do about them and watching them, so that a problem arrives as a planned scenario rather than a surprise. The steps are simple: identify (in planning workshops, at each increment planning, from every status meeting's "what worries you?"), analyse (probability and impact, often on a scale of 1 to 5 each), treat (avoid, reduce, transfer or accept, with an owner and a date), and monitor (weekly for the top risks, with triggers that tell you a risk is turning into an issue).

R1 has a 60 % chance of costing 8 weeks and R2 a 40 % chance of costing 12; both have an exposure of 4.8 weeks, about the size of the buffer that Figure 8.2 suggested for a five-input milestone. That is a useful sanity check: if the summed exposure of the open risks on the critical path is larger than the buffer, the date is not credible.

Pitfall

Risk registers die in two ways: they list fifty generic risks ("requirements may change") that nobody can act on, or they are updated only before the steering committee. Keep the top risks specific (cause, event, effect), review them weekly in the project meeting, and close or demote risks that no longer matter. A risk that happens becomes an issue, with actions instead of probabilities.

8.6Change management

Change management exists because in a contract-based project every change has a price, and in a safety-related project every change has an impact that must be understood before it is made. A change request, from the customer or from inside, goes through a fixed path: it is recorded with its reason; analysed for its impact on requirements, design, tests, schedule, cost, safety and security (the traceability of Section 3.3 gives the technical impact set); decided by a change control board (CCB) in which the project lead, the architect, quality and, where relevant, the safety manager and the customer are represented; and, if accepted, planned into a release, implemented, verified and closed. SUP.10 in ASPICE describes the same path (ASPICE 11.5); the commercial side of customer changes is in RFI to SOP 11.

The project lead's skill is in timing and framing. Early in the project, changes are cheap and expected, and a light process is enough. After feature freeze, every change competes with stability, and the default answer should be "next release". Near SOP, only changes that fix defects or are required by law or safety get through. Saying this to the customer at the start, as part of the plan, makes the later "no" a shared rule instead of a confrontation.

Talk track

"We can do the 5 m range. The impact analysis shows a new data campaign, retraining, and six verification measures to redo; it fits into C1 if we decide by M10, otherwise C2. The cost is in the attached change offer. If it must be in C1 and the decision comes later, we need to drop something else from C1; here are two candidates."

9Release, configuration management and quality gates

A release is a promise that a specific, reproducible set of things was built, checked and approved, and can be rebuilt and supported for years. Configuration management makes the "specific, reproducible set" possible; quality gates make "checked and approved" mean something.

9.1Why configuration management exists

Two years after SOP, a workshop reports that the pedestrian warning sometimes triggers late on cars built in one month. To analyse it, the team needs to know exactly what is in those cars: which software version, built from which source, with which compiler and SDK, which model trained on which data, with which calibration parameters, verified with which tests. If any of those cannot be retrieved, the analysis becomes guesswork and the fix becomes a risk. Configuration management (SUP.8) exists so that the answer is always available: every item that makes up the product is identified, versioned, controlled, and grouped into baselines that can be reproduced.

The items under control, the configuration items, are much more than the source code (Figure 9.1, left). They include requirement and architecture baselines, the ML model with its dataset version and training configuration, the toolchain and build environment, parameters and coding data, test specifications and results, the release note with its known issues, and the safety and security work products. A useful test: for any delivered release, could a new engineer rebuild the binary bit for bit, and show which requirements and tests it corresponds to, without asking anyone who was there?

9.2Baselines, branches and variants

A baseline is a named, frozen set of versions of all configuration items, such as "B2.0". It is the unit that is released, assessed, delivered and later reproduced. Baselines are made from the version control system with tags and from the other tools with their own baseline mechanisms (a requirements baseline, a test-run record), tied together by the release record. A branching strategy decides how development and releases coexist. The usual pattern in automotive (Figure 9.1) has a main integration branch with continuous integration, a release branch per customer release that is created at feature freeze and only accepts approved fixes, and a long-lived maintenance branch after SOP. Fixes are made on main first and then cherry-picked into the release branches that need them, and the problem report records which branches were checked, so that a defect fixed in B3 is not found again in the SOP software.

Variants multiply this. A platform ECU often ships in several hardware variants, for several vehicle lines and markets, with features enabled by coding. Each delivered combination must be identifiable and verified, which is why variant management prefers one software build configured by parameters over many builds, and why the test strategy must say which combinations are tested fully and which by sampling. The detailed mechanics of release identity, memory layout and long-term support are in Production integration 4.1 and 14.3; reproducible builds and SBOMs in Build systems 6.6 and 11.5.

Pitfall

A release built on one engineer's machine "because the CI was down" breaks the chain: nobody can prove what toolchain built it. Make the controlled build environment the only way a release binary can be created, and make the release record reference the build, not a file on a share.

9.3The release process: what a release is and who signs it

Product release (SPL.2) answers four questions for every delivery: what is in it, what state is it in, who approved it, and how does it reach the customer. The content is defined in advance in the release plan (features, fixes, the requirements in scope) and agreed with the customer; summaries of PAM 4.1 report that this agreement with the intended customer is now explicit in SPL.2. The state is described honestly in the release note: which requirements are implemented and verified, which are partially implemented, the known open defects and their workarounds, and the restrictions of use (a B-sample release may be approved for test drives on closed tracks only). Approval is given by named roles, typically the project lead, quality, the test manager and, for safety-relevant content, the safety manager. Delivery follows the agreed channel, with checksums or signatures, and is recorded.

Release types differ by maturity. Internal integration builds are released many times a day to the team. Sample releases go to the customer for testing in benches and prototype vehicles, with restrictions. The SOP release is the one that goes into production; it carries the full verification, the safety and security cases, and the approvals needed for the OEM's homologation. After SOP, maintenance releases fix field problems and are delivered through the plant, the workshop or over the air, each with the same discipline (Production integration 14.3).

9.4Quality gates and the release-gate checklist

A quality gate is a decision point with defined criteria: the work may continue (or be delivered) only if the criteria are met, or if a named person accepts the deviation with a reason. Gates exist at the project level (the sample milestones, the maturity levels of the VDA's MLA, the OEM's own process) and at the release level. They work when the criteria are measurable and known in advance, when the evidence is produced continuously so the gate only confirms it, and when someone with authority actually says no sometimes. A gate that has never stopped anything is a meeting.

9.5Problem resolution and the flow of defects

Problem resolution (SUP.9) is the path every defect takes: recorded with enough information to reproduce it, analysed for cause and severity, assigned, fixed, verified, and closed, with the information which releases contain the problem and which contain the fix. The project lead watches it as a flow. The useful numbers are the inflow and outflow of defects per week, the age of open defects by severity, and the share of defects found by the customer rather than by the team. A rising inflow just before a release is normal (more testing happens); a falling outflow is not, and usually means the people who fix defects are busy with features.

Severity and priority are different. Severity describes the effect (a wrong warning in a safety-relevant situation is severe), priority the urgency of the fix given the release plan; a severe defect in a feature that ships in C2 may have a lower priority now than a minor defect that blocks the B2 test drives. Customer defects need a defined response time and a joint classification meeting, because both sides will rate them differently. And every defect that escaped to the customer deserves a short root cause analysis: not who made the mistake, but which check should have caught it and why it did not.

Talk track

"A release is a baseline plus evidence plus a signature. The baseline says exactly what we built, the evidence says what we checked, and the signature says who accepted the remaining risk. If any of the three is missing, it is a build, not a release."

10Stakeholders, suppliers and escalation

A project lead spends much of the week on communication: reporting status upwards and to the customer, coordinating suppliers, and escalating what cannot be solved at their level. Done well, this is what turns problems into decisions early enough to matter.

10.1Status reporting that people trust

A status report exists so that the people who can make decisions know, in a few minutes, whether they need to make one. Everything in it should serve that purpose. A good report for the running example fits on one page: the milestones with their current forecast (and the trend, Section 8.3), the top risks and issues with owner and next action, the decisions needed from the reader, and a small set of measures that show progress honestly: requirements verified for the next release against plan, open defects by severity and age, budget consumption, the key supplier deliveries. Traffic lights summarise, and they need definitions: red means the milestone will be missed unless someone above the project acts, amber means at risk but the project has a plan, green means on track.

The failure everyone knows is the watermelon report: green outside, red inside. It comes from fear of the reaction to red and from optimism about catching up, and it destroys the report's only value, because the first red appears when it is too late to act. Two habits protect against it: measures that come from the tools and not from opinions, and a reputation, earned by the project lead, for reporting amber early and calmly with a plan attached. MAN.6 in ASPICE asks for exactly this: measures derived from information needs and used for decisions.

In practice

Put "decisions needed" at the top of the report, before the status. Managers read the first lines; if what you need from them is on page three, you will not get it.

10.2Steering committees and customer meetings

A steering committee is where the people who own the money, the people and the customer relationship meet monthly to make the decisions the project cannot make itself: approve a change in scope or budget, add resources, accept a risk, decide a trade-off between the customer's demands and the supplier's capacity. Its agenda follows the report: decisions needed, milestones and trends, top risks, escalations. A good steering meeting ends with recorded decisions and owners; one that only receives status is a missed opportunity.

With the customer there are several regular forums: technical meetings between engineers (daily or weekly, about specific issues), the project meeting between the project leads (weekly or bi-weekly, about plan, deliveries, changes, defects), and the management review or quality review (monthly or per milestone). Each has its own minutes and action list. The rule that keeps them consistent is that commitments are only made in the forum that can keep them: an engineer agrees on the interpretation of a signal, a project lead on a release date, management on price.

10.3Coordinating suppliers

From the OEM's view, the Tier 1 is responsible for everything inside the ECU, including what its Tier 2s deliver. Supplier monitoring (ACQ.4) is the discipline that makes this responsibility manageable: agree on the joint processes and interfaces, exchange information regularly, review the supplier's technical progress against agreed milestones, track open points to closure, and act when the supplier deviates (ASPICE 11.6). In practice that means a supplier plan with delivery dates well ahead of your own needs, a weekly joint call with an action list, acceptance criteria for each delivery (an SDK drop is accepted only if it builds in your environment and passes your smoke tests), and access to the supplier's defect tracking for issues that affect you.

Safety and security add formal agreements. The DIA defines which safety activities the Tier 2 performs, which work products it delivers and how the two safety cases connect (Functional safety 1.4); the cybersecurity interface agreement does the same for ISO/SAE 21434. For components that the Tier 2 develops as a safety element out of context, such as an SoC with its safety manual, the Tier 1 must check that the assumptions in the safety manual hold in its design. For open-source and commercial off-the-shelf software, the obligations land fully on the Tier 1, because there is no supplier to sign a DIA.

Pitfall

Purchasing negotiates the contract and then the engineers manage the supplier day to day, with nobody holding both views. When the Tier 2 is late, the engineers do not know what the contract allows, and purchasing does not know what the delay costs. Name one owner per key supplier with both the technical and the contractual picture.

10.4Escalation

Escalation is moving a problem to the level that has the authority to solve it. It is not a complaint and not a failure; in a well-run project it is a routine tool with agreed rules. Figure 10.1 shows the usual ladder: engineers solve technical issues with their counterparts, project leads solve plan and delivery issues, the steering committee decides on scope, budget and resources, and the management of both companies settles what the steering committee cannot. Each level talks to its counterpart at the other company; going around a level (an engineer emailing the OEM's management) destroys trust on both sides.

Good escalations share a form. They state the problem in one sentence with its impact on a milestone or on cost, the options with their consequences, a recommendation, and the decision needed by a date. They are raised as soon as the criteria are met, not after a last attempt to fix it quietly, because each week of delay removes an option. And they are agreed in advance: a project handbook that says "a milestone at risk by more than two weeks, or an issue open for more than ten working days at project level, goes to the steering committee" makes escalation a rule rather than a personal decision, and nobody has to feel disloyal for following it.

Talk track

"I escalate by rule, not by mood: when a decision needs money, people or scope I do not control, or when an issue has been stuck longer than we agreed. I bring one sentence of problem, the impact on the milestone, two or three options and my recommendation. Escalating early is cheaper than explaining later why we did not."

10.5Talk tracks for the typical conversations

A few conversations come back in every project, and preparing them is worth the time. Telling the customer about a slip: lead with the fact and the new date, then the cause in one sentence, what you are doing about it, and what the customer can do to help; never let the customer find out from the delivery. Answering a new requirement in a meeting: thank, restate it to confirm the understanding, and promise an impact analysis by a date instead of a yes or no on the spot. Pushing back on a supplier: refer to the agreed milestone and acceptance criteria, state the impact on your delivery, and ask for their recovery plan with dates. Defending the process to a sceptical team: explain each piece of evidence by the failure it prevents, and remove the pieces that do not prevent anything. Explaining ASPICE to a non-automotive colleague: it is a model that lets a customer judge whether a supplier's development process can be trusted, by rating how one real project works.

The common thread is that each conversation turns a problem into a decision with options. Customers, managers and suppliers all accept bad news better than surprises, and decisions better than complaints. That is most of what stakeholder management means in practice; the people side of leading a team is the subject of the planned chapter on technical leadership.

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

What is Automotive SPICE and why do OEMs require it?Basic

A process assessment model maintained by VDA QMC, derived from ISO/IEC 15504. It rates how one real project's processes achieve their outcomes (process dimension) and how well they are managed (capability levels 0 to 5). OEMs require it because they cannot inspect suppliers' software line by line; a rated, managed process is their evidence that requirements, design, verification, configuration and changes are under control. The current version is PAM 4.1 (August 2026).

Explain the V-model. Is it a waterfall?Basic

The left side decomposes needs into system requirements, architecture, software requirements, design and units; the right side integrates and verifies each level against its mirror on the left, up to validation against the stakeholder needs. It is a logic of levels and their verification relationships, not a schedule. Real projects run it once per sample or release, and agile teams can satisfy it in every sprint.

What is bidirectional traceability and what do you use it for?Basic

Links from each item to its source and to what implements and verifies it, navigable both ways. Forward answers "is every requirement implemented and verified?", backward "does everything we built have a reason?". In daily work it gives the impact set of a change (suspect links), the coverage numbers for status reports and the backbone of the safety case.

What is the difference between verification and validation?Basic

Verification checks a work product against its specification ("built it right"), with tests, reviews, analyses or simulations. Validation checks the product against its intended use in the real environment ("built the right thing"). Validation can find wrong requirements, which verification cannot; ASPICE 4.0 added VAL.1 for it.

Which test levels and environments would you plan for a camera ECU, and why?Intermediate

Unit, integration, software and system verification, plus OEM acceptance and validation. Environments from cheap to expensive: MIL, SIL (with large-scale data replay), PIL on the target processor, HIL with the real ECU, prototype vehicles and a validation fleet. The rule is to test each property on the lowest level where it is visible, because cheap environments are early and scalable and expensive ones are scarce; HIL and vehicles test only what nothing cheaper can (I/O, diagnostics, network timing, real scenes).

What does capability level 2 require in practice?Intermediate

Level 1 fully achieved, plus PA 2.1 (process objectives and strategy, planning, resources and responsibilities, monitoring and adjustment, managed interfaces) and PA 2.2 (requirements for work products, identification and control, review and adjustment) at least largely achieved. In practice: plans that are updated when reality changes, defined roles, versioned and reviewed work products, and evidence that deviations led to actions.

What changed in ASPICE 4.0 and 4.1 compared with 3.1?Intermediate

4.0 added hardware (HWE), machine learning (MLE, SUP.11) and validation (VAL.1), removed many rarely used processes (32 remain), renamed work products to information items and tests to verification measures, merged traceability and consistency, moved verification strategies into GP 2.1.1 and made the VDA scope a plug-in set. 4.1 is evolutionary: same processes and levels, edits such as the MLE.2 resource-consumption practice folded into the architecture analysis and ISO/SAE 21434 referenced. The 3rd edition of the VDA guidelines added guidance on AI-assisted development and maintenance.

How do you combine Scrum with ASPICE and fixed OEM milestones?Intermediate

Nested cadences: sprints inside increments, increments aligned with the OEM's releases, releases aligned with hardware samples. Fixed dates and content commitments to the customer, team autonomy inside each increment. The ASPICE expectations go into the definition of done (requirement linked, design updated, tests in CI, review recorded), so evidence is produced in every sprint and collected in the release baseline.

How do you estimate a task you have never done before?Intermediate

Break it into parts that are closer to known work, use a three-point estimate for each (PERT mean (O + 4M + P) / 6), compare with a reference class of similar past work, and state the uncertainty instead of a single number. Put the margin as a visible buffer before the milestone, not inside each task, and re-estimate as soon as the first part is done.

What is the critical path and why does it matter?Basic

The longest chain of dependent tasks through the plan, the one with no slack. Any delay on it delays the end date one-to-one; delays elsewhere are absorbed by slack until they make a new critical path. It tells the project lead where to put the best people, the earliest risk mitigation and the closest monitoring. In ML-based functions it often runs through data and training rather than hardware.

How do you run risk management in a project?Intermediate

Identify risks continuously, write them as cause, event, effect; rate probability and impact; choose a treatment (avoid, reduce, transfer, accept) with an owner, a date, a trigger and a contingency; review the top risks weekly and report the high ones to the steering committee. When a risk happens it becomes an issue with actions. Compare the summed exposure of critical-path risks with the schedule buffer to check whether the date is credible.

A customer asks for a new feature two months before SOP. What do you do?Intermediate

Acknowledge it, restate it, and promise an impact analysis by a date rather than answering on the spot. The analysis covers requirements, design, retest scope, safety and security impact, schedule and cost. Near SOP the default is a post-SOP update; if the customer needs it for SOP, show what must be dropped or what risk is accepted, and let the change control board and, if needed, the steering committee decide, with the decision recorded.

What is a baseline, and what belongs in one?Basic

A named, frozen set of versions of all configuration items that together make a release: requirements, architecture, source, ML model with dataset id and training configuration, toolchain and build environment, parameters, test specifications and results, release note, safety and security work products. It must be reproducible years later, for field analysis and maintenance.

What would you put on a release-gate checklist?Intermediate

Scope matches the agreed release plan; baseline complete and reproducible, built in the controlled environment, SBOM done; requirements in scope verified or justified; release verification passed on the candidate itself with all failures analysed; ML model frozen and tested on target; no defects above the agreed severity, the rest listed as known issues; budgets met; safety and security impact analysed and cases updated; release note with restrictions; signed approval by named roles.

What goes into a good status report?Basic

Decisions needed first; milestones with current forecast and trend; top risks and issues with owners and next actions; a few measures from the tools (requirements verified against plan, defects by severity and age, budget, key supplier deliveries); traffic lights with agreed definitions. It should let a manager decide in a few minutes whether to act.

When and how do you escalate?Intermediate

By agreed rule: when a decision needs money, people or scope outside your authority, when a milestone is at risk beyond the agreed tolerance, or when an issue is stuck longer than agreed. Escalate to your counterpart's level, never around it, with a one-sentence problem, the impact, options and a recommendation, and a date by which the decision is needed. Early escalation keeps options open.

How do you manage a Tier 2 supplier whose delivery you depend on?Intermediate

Agree delivery dates well before your own need, with acceptance criteria per delivery; hold a regular joint review with an action list; get visibility into their defects that affect you; have a fallback for the critical deliveries; for safety and security, agree a DIA and a cybersecurity interface agreement. Name one owner who holds both the technical and the contractual picture. The OEM holds you responsible for their delivery.

What does an ASPICE assessment look like and how do you prepare?Intermediate

Planned with the OEM and the lead assessor (scope, target level, project, PAM version, dates), then a few days of interviews and evidence review, preliminary findings, a report, and an improvement plan with a possible re-assessment. Preparation: an internal pre-assessment months before, owners per process, a golden thread from one customer requirement to its test result and release, and above all evidence that comes from daily work rather than being produced for the assessor.

What roles would you expect in a Tier 1 ECU project?Basic

Project lead, technical project lead, sub-project leads (software, hardware, ML, mechanics), system architect, test manager; independent roles for quality assurance, configuration management, safety and cybersecurity; supplier management with purchasing and supplier quality; a key account manager for the commercial side. Each has a counterpart at the OEM.

Q.2Why and what if

Why does automotive need more process than a web company?Why

Because being wrong after delivery is very expensive (fleet in the field, recalls, safety, regulation), several companies build one product, the delivery date is fixed by a vehicle programme, and the product must be supported for about fifteen years. Proving correctness before delivery and keeping it reproducible is cheaper than repairing it afterwards.

Why plan the buffer before a merge milestone instead of inside each task?Why

Margins inside tasks tend to be used up, and they do not address merge bias: a milestone that waits for several inputs is late when any one is late, so its risk is concentrated at the merge point. A visible buffer there, owned by the project lead, protects the commitment where the uncertainty accumulates (with five inputs at 25 % each, about 4.6 weeks for 80 % confidence in the example).

Why is a plan built from most-likely estimates usually late?Why

Engineering durations are right-skewed: little room to be early, much room to be late. The most likely value is below the mean and the median, so each task is more likely late than early, and along a chain the gaps add up (21 weeks most likely vs 23.7 weeks mean in the example). At merge points it is worse, because the latest input decides.

What if requirements are never frozen?What if

Every release is verified against a moving target, regression never ends, the customer cannot plan its vehicle tests, and the project cannot price changes because there is no agreed baseline. The cure is not to forbid change but to agree when each kind of change is still possible (anything before feature freeze, fixes after code freeze, only safety or legal changes near SOP) and to baseline requirements per release.

What if the trace matrix is built only before the assessment?What if

It will be inconsistent with the actual work, it cannot have supported impact analysis during the project, and its dates reveal it. Assessors rate weaknesses in the traceability and consistency practice and often in PA 2.2. More importantly, the project lost the main benefit: knowing what a change affects when it happens.

Why does a team that passes all verification still fail validation?Why

Because verification checks against the requirements, and the requirements can be wrong or incomplete: a minimum pedestrian height that excludes crouching children, a false-alarm rate that drivers find unbearable, a scenario nobody specified. Only checking the product in real use, against the real need, finds those.

What if the HIL rack is the only test environment?What if

It becomes the bottleneck of every release: 1,200 tests at three minutes already need three rack-days per full run, and variants and release candidates multiply it. Tests queue, regression is cut in the last week, and developers get feedback days late. Moving everything that does not need real I/O to SIL frees the racks for what only they can test.

Why does ASPICE care whether the plan was adjusted?Why

Because a plan that was never changed in a project that obviously deviated shows that nobody monitored it; the plan was a document, not a steering tool. PA 2.1 rates planning, monitoring and adjustment together, so the evidence it looks for is a deviation, a decision and an updated plan with the people informed.

What if the OEM changes the warning range from 3 m to 5 m at M14?What if

The change request goes through impact analysis: the traceability gives the affected system and software requirements, the detector, the model and its dataset, the post-processing and the tests; the plan shows whether a new data campaign and retraining fit before the model freeze at M22. The result is a priced offer with a target release (C1 if decided quickly, otherwise C2) and, if needed, a trade-off with other content, decided by the CCB and the customer.

Why are freezes applied to release branches instead of to the whole code base?Why

So that the release can stabilise without stopping development for the next one. The release branch accepts only approved fixes; main keeps integrating features. The cost is that fixes must be ported between branches, which is why problem reports record which branches were checked.

What if a key Tier 2 is three months late with no recovery plan?What if

Escalate at your level first (supplier's project lead, with reference to the agreed milestones), then through purchasing and supplier quality to the supplier's management. In parallel, activate the contingency (fallback implementation, alternative source, reduced scope for the next release), inform your customer early with the impact and options, and record it as an issue. Do not hide it behind "the supplier is late"; the customer holds you responsible.

Why is quality assurance given an independent reporting line?Why

Because QA must be able to say that the project does not follow its agreed process even when the project lead would rather not hear it, and to escalate when it is ignored. If QA reports only to the project lead, its findings can be silenced by schedule pressure, which is exactly when they matter most. SUP.1 asks for objectivity for this reason.

What if the team uses AI coding assistants in an ASPICE project?What if

That is allowed; the VDA guidelines' 3rd edition does not prescribe or forbid tools, but expects AI-generated output to be reviewed before it is accepted, and assessors look at how this is controlled in quality assurance, configuration management and work product management. So the project needs a documented rule (who reviews, how it is recorded) and the usual evidence of reviews and verification for that code.

Why does a status report need defined traffic-light colours?Why

Without definitions, colours express mood: one lead's amber is another's green, and the first red appears when it is too late. Defined colours (red means the milestone is missed unless someone above acts) make the report a decision tool and protect the reporter, because following a rule is not a personal judgement.
