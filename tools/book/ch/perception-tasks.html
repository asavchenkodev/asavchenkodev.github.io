Perception tasks and the research frontier

A walk through the perception tasks of a driving stack one at a time: detection, tracking, segmentation, lanes and road topology, traffic lights and signs, depth, flow, occupancy, open-vocabulary models, neural reconstruction, temporal and multi-task perception, and robustness. For each task: what it outputs, why it is hard, the classic approach, how current methods work, what the 2023–2026 papers changed, how it is measured, and what survives the trip to an embedded target. Small real experiments on CPU (detectors, open-vocabulary prompts, two depth models, a tracker simulation) keep the claims concrete, and one street photograph runs through the chapter.

1The big picture: a map of perception tasks

A driving stack does not "see"; it answers a list of specific questions about the scene, each with its own output format, data, metric and failure modes. Knowing the list, how the answers depend on each other, and how to judge a claimed improvement is what lets you read the research without being carried away by it.

1.1Perception turns sensor data into a world model a planner can use

A planner cannot use pixels. It needs a description of the world in the ego vehicle's coordinate frame: which objects exist, where they are in metres, how fast they move and which way they face; where the drivable space ends; which lanes exist and how they connect; which traffic light governs the ego path and what it shows; which signs apply. Perception is the set of functions that produce this description from cameras, lidar, radar and the ego vehicle's own motion. Every perception task is defined by its output, and the output is what makes the tasks different from each other, even when the networks inside look alike.

It helps to sort the tasks by what they describe. Object tasks describe countable things: 2D and 3D detection answer "what is where", tracking adds identity and history ("the same pedestrian as one second ago, now walking faster"). Pixel and space tasks describe the scene as a field: semantic segmentation gives a class per pixel, depth a distance per pixel, optical flow a motion per pixel, occupancy a state per cell of 3D space. Road and rule tasks describe structure that the traffic rules hang on: lane geometry and the lane graph, traffic lights with their state and relevance, signs with their meaning and the lane they apply to. Around these sit three cross-cutting themes that change every task at once: using time, using large pretrained "foundation" models, and staying correct when inputs leave the training distribution.

1.2Tasks depend on each other, and the dependencies decide the architecture

Perception tasks are not independent boxes. A 3D box from a camera needs depth, whether a network predicts it explicitly or learns it implicitly inside a BEV transform. Tracking needs detections, and good tracking in turn improves detection, because an object seen for ten frames is more certain than one seen once. Traffic-light relevance needs the lane graph. Free space needs both the static scene (road, kerbs) and the dynamic objects. Prediction of other road users' motion needs tracks and the lane graph. These dependencies are why production stacks drifted from many separate networks towards one shared backbone with several heads, and why research drifted further, towards models that pass learned queries from task to task (UniAD, Section 12.2).

The dependencies also explain where errors come from. A wrong lane graph makes a correct traffic-light detection useless; a pitch error in the camera calibration turns a correct 2D detection into a 3D box at the wrong distance (Section 7.6). When a metric gets worse, the first question is often not "what is wrong with this network" but "which upstream answer changed".

Why it matters

When someone proposes a new network for one task, ask what it consumes and who consumes its output. A lane detector that is 2 points better on a benchmark but outputs image-space curves instead of 3D polylines may be worse for the planner than the old one.

1.3What changed between 2023 and 2026

Five shifts run through almost every section of this chapter, so it is worth naming them once.

Set prediction replaced hand-made post-processing. DETR's idea, predicting a set of objects with a one-to-one match to the ground truth instead of many overlapping candidates that non-maximum suppression (NMS) must thin out, became fast enough for real time (RT-DETR, D-FINE, DEIM, RF-DETR) and reached the YOLO line too (YOLOv10, YOLO26). The same idea now drives lane and map heads (MapTR) and trackers (MOTR, MOTIP).

Sparse queries replaced dense grids in 3D. Instead of computing a full bird's-eye-view (BEV) feature map and reading boxes out of it, query-based detectors such as StreamPETR and Sparse4D keep a few hundred object hypotheses and let each one look up the image features it needs. Time enters by carrying those queries from frame to frame, which is cheap.

Foundation backbones replaced ImageNet backbones. Self-supervised vision transformers trained on hundreds of millions to billions of images (DINOv2, DINOv3) give features good enough that a light head on top beats a specialised network trained from scratch. Depth (Depth Anything), detection (DEIMv2, RF-DETR) and segmentation (EoMT) all moved this way.

Closed vocabularies opened up. Detectors and segmenters that take a text prompt (Grounding DINO, YOLO-World, YOLOE, SAM 3) made "find every stroller" a query rather than a training project. In production they mostly change labelling and data mining, not the on-board network yet (Section 10.3).

Geometry became learnable at scale, and so did the world. Monocular metric depth (Metric3D v2, UniDepth, Depth Pro, MoGe-2), multi-view geometry transformers (VGGT, Depth Anything 3) and stereo foundation models (FoundationStereo) generalise across cameras in a way the 2019 methods did not. Neural reconstruction with 3D Gaussian splatting turned recorded drives into editable scenes, now in seconds instead of hours (Section 11).

Talk track

"Three years ago every task had its own architecture. Now most of them are a pretrained transformer backbone, a set of learned queries, and a one-to-one matching loss; the tasks differ mainly in what a query is asked to output. That is why progress in one task, such as better box refinement in D-FINE, shows up in others within months."

1.4Reading a perception paper and a leaderboard critically

Most of what you will read about a perception task arrives as a paper with a results table. The table is the least reliable part, not because authors lie, but because a single number hides the choices that produced it. A short list of questions catches most of the problems.

Is the comparison like for like? Backbone, input resolution, training epochs, extra pretraining data and test-time augmentation each move a detection score by points. RT-DETR's paper reports 53.1 AP on COCO for the ResNet-50 model, while D-FINE-X reaches 59.3 AP only with Objects365 pretraining; DEIMv2's paper itself notes that its gains over DEIM come mainly from medium and large objects. Look for the row with the same backbone and schedule as the baseline. On nuScenes, compare test with test and validation with validation, and note the backbone: a V2-99 or ViT-L number is not comparable with a ResNet-50 number.

Is the split honest? In online map construction, the original nuScenes and Argoverse 2 splits contain the same streets in training and validation, so a network can partly memorise the map. StreamMapNet introduced geographically disjoint splits, and a CVPR 2024 study (Lilja et al., "Localization is all you evaluate") showed how much of the reported map quality was location memorisation. When a paper's split differs from its baselines', the numbers are not comparable at all.

Is the metric measuring what you need? Voxel mIoU rewarded thick occupancy surfaces until RayIoU replaced it (Section 9.1); MOTA hides identity errors that HOTA exposes (Section 3.4); mAP at IoU 0.5 ignores how well the box fits.

Is the gap larger than the noise? A test set is a sample. Even two identical models would show a gap on it, and two training runs of the same model with different seeds differ too. Figure 1.2 makes the size of that noise visible with a deliberately simple model: the two models' per-unit results agree 60% of the time beyond chance (correlation 0.6, typical when both fail on the same hard cases), the metric sits around 50%, and each training run adds its own seed spread.

With 5,000 independent units and a true gap of half a point, the observed gap spreads by ±1.24 points (95%), and the worse model ranks first about one time in five (21.5%). Adding a seed spread of 0.2 points per run raises this to 23.5%; a test set four times larger brings it down to 11.9%. Only with 100,000 units does a 0.3-point gap become reliable (1.7% wrong order). The model is simplified (real metrics such as AP are not plain proportions, and objects in one image are not independent, which makes the effective number of units smaller), but the lesson holds: improvements below one point on a single run on a validation set of a few thousand images are not evidence. Papers that report the mean and spread over several seeds, or a test-server number, deserve more trust.

Pitfall

Picking the best checkpoint on the validation set and reporting its validation score is selection on the test data. The number is optimistic by roughly the noise band of Figure 1.2. Use a held-out split for the final number, or report the last checkpoint.

Does it transfer? A paper's speed claim is for a specific GPU, precision and runtime (often an NVIDIA T4 with TensorRT FP16), a specific input size, and frequently excludes pre- and post-processing. Operators such as deformable attention, grid sampling or top-K selection may be slow or missing on an automotive accelerator. Section 13.4 collects what tends to transfer.

In practice

A five-minute paper check: (1) which table row is the fair baseline; (2) which split and is it the same as the baselines'; (3) gap versus seed spread, if reported; (4) latency hardware, precision, input size, and what is excluded; (5) one failure figure, if any. If (5) is missing, assume the failures are where you need the model most.

Metric mechanics (precision, recall, AP, confusion matrices) and error analysis belong to Training and evaluation 7 and 10.

2Object detection in 2D and 3D

Detection is the task everyone knows and the one where research moves fastest. The 2023–2026 story is that transformer detectors became as fast as YOLO, YOLO borrowed their NMS-free training, and camera 3D detection moved from dense BEV grids to sparse queries that carry memory across frames.

2.1Recap: dense candidates and NMS, or a set of objects

A detector outputs, for each object, a box (2D in pixels, or 3D in metres with size and heading), a class and a confidence. The Deep learning chapter explains the two classic families: dense one-stage detectors such as YOLO, which predict a candidate box at every location of every feature map and remove duplicates with NMS, and DETR, which predicts a fixed-size set of boxes from learned queries and is trained with a one-to-one Hungarian match (8.2), so duplicates are penalised during training and no NMS is needed.

The difference is easy to see on a real image. YOLO11n, a 2.6-million-parameter detector, finds 13 objects with score at least 0.25 on the street image of this chapter after NMS at IoU 0.7. Switch NMS off (IoU threshold 0.999) and the same network returns 57 boxes, most of them near-copies of each other (Figure 2.2). NMS is cheap, but it is a hand-made rule with two thresholds that interact with crowding: in a dense group of pedestrians, two people standing close together overlap like two duplicate boxes, and NMS deletes one of them. Removing NMS also makes the latency deterministic, because its runtime depends on how many candidates survive the score threshold.

2.2Real-time detection transformers: RT-DETR, D-FINE, DEIM, RF-DETR

The original DETR (2020) was slow to train (500 epochs) and slow to run, because its encoder applied global attention to every token of a high-resolution feature map. RT-DETR (Zhao et al., CVPR 2024, "DETRs Beat YOLOs on Real-time Object Detection") fixed the inference cost with a hybrid encoder: attention runs only on the coarsest feature map (1/32 resolution, few tokens), where global context matters most, and a CNN module fuses the scales. The decoder starts from the top-K encoder tokens (K = 300) rather than from blank queries, so it begins with good guesses. The paper reports 53.1 AP at 108 FPS for the ResNet-50 model on an NVIDIA T4 with TensorRT FP16, in the range of the YOLO models of the time.

D-FINE (Peng et al., ICLR 2025) changed how a box is regressed. Instead of predicting four coordinates directly, each decoder layer predicts, for each edge of the box, a probability distribution over offsets, and the next layer refines that distribution with a residual correction (Fine-grained Distribution Refinement, FDR). A weighting function makes the bins fine near zero offset and coarse far away, so small corrections are precise and large ones are possible. The final layer's distributions then teach the earlier layers (Global Optimal Localization Self-Distillation, GO-LSD), so early layers already predict good boxes. D-FINE-L reaches 54.0 AP at 124 FPS and D-FINE-X 55.8 AP at 78 FPS on a T4; with Objects365 pretraining, 57.1 and 59.3 AP.

DEIM (CVPR 2025) attacked training speed: one-to-one matching gives each object exactly one positive query, so the gradient signal is sparse and training is slow. DEIM adds more positives per image (by augmenting images so they contain more objects) and a matching-aware loss, which reaches the same accuracy in far fewer epochs. DEIMv2 (September 2025) replaces the backbone with DINOv3 features for the larger models, adapted to multiple scales by a light "Spatial Tuning Adapter", and pruned CNNs for the smallest: DEIMv2-X reaches 57.8 AP with 50.3 M parameters, DEIMv2-S 50.9 AP with 9.7 M, and an "Atto" variant with 0.5 M parameters still 23.8 AP. RF-DETR (Robinson et al., ICLR 2026) uses a DINOv2 backbone and weight-sharing neural architecture search: one network is fine-tuned once, and thousands of sub-configurations (resolution, patch size, decoder depth, number of queries) are evaluated without retraining, so you can pick the accuracy/latency point for your device. Its nano model reports 48.0 AP on COCO, 5.3 points above D-FINE-nano at similar latency, and the paper emphasises transfer to new domains (Roboflow100-VL).

Interesting detail

Why do distributions help? A direct regression head has to commit to one number even when the edge is ambiguous (a pedestrian's arm, a car's mirror, an occluded rear). A distribution can stay wide where the evidence is weak and the later layers can resolve it, and its width is a free localisation-uncertainty estimate per edge, useful for a tracker or for fusion.

2.3The YOLO line: from NMS-free training to YOLO26

The YOLO family kept its dense heads but adopted the set-prediction lesson. YOLOv10 (NeurIPS 2024) trained two heads: a one-to-many head with rich supervision, used only in training, and a one-to-one head used at inference, with "consistent dual assignments" so both learn the same thing; no NMS is needed at inference. YOLO11 (Ultralytics, September 2024, no paper) refined the blocks. YOLO26 (Ultralytics; arXiv paper June 2026) makes the end-to-end dual head the default, removes the Distribution Focal Loss (which had bounded the regression range and complicated export), trains with a Muon-SGD hybrid optimizer, and adds a label assignment that guarantees positives for small objects. Its five sizes span 40.9 to 57.5 mAP on COCO at 1.7 to 11.8 ms on a T4 with TensorRT.

2.4Sparse query-based 3D detection: StreamPETR, Sparse4D and successors

Camera 3D detection must turn image evidence into boxes in metres. The BEV chapter explains the two dense routes: lift image features into 3D with a predicted depth distribution and splat them into a BEV grid (Lift-Splat, BEVDepth), or let a BEV grid of queries attend back into the images (BEVFormer). Both compute a full grid, typically 200 × 200 cells or more, every frame, whether or not anything is there.

Sparse methods skip the grid (BEV 5.3). A few hundred learned queries, each tied to a 3D anchor (a box hypothesis with position, size and heading), are refined layer by layer. In Sparse4D (Lin et al., 2022–2023), each query generates a handful of 3D key points inside its box, projects them into every camera with the calibration, samples image features there (deformable sampling across views and scales), fuses them and updates its box. PETR and StreamPETR (Wang et al., ICCV 2023) instead add 3D position embeddings to the image features and let queries attend to them globally. The decisive step in both lines was temporal: StreamPETR keeps the top-K queries of the previous frame in a memory queue, moves them by the ego motion (and their own estimated velocity), and feeds them into the next frame as extra queries. A tracked object thus starts each frame with a good prior, and the cost of using history is a few hundred extra queries instead of a second BEV grid. Sparse4D v3 added temporal denoising of queries and a quality head, and turned the propagated queries into track identities, so the detector tracks without a separate tracker.

The numbers show how far the sparse temporal line moved camera-only detection. On the nuScenes test set with a V2-99 backbone, StreamPETR reports 63.6 NDS and 55.0 mAP (as tabulated in later papers); DySS (2025), which processes the sampled features with a state-space model and merges, removes and splits queries dynamically, reports 65.3 NDS and 57.4 mAP, and 56.2 NDS on validation with a ResNet-50 at 33 FPS on an RTX 3080. A 2026 preprint, CAM3DNet, combines image-detector proposals, temporal memory and global BEV queries and reports 55.1 NDS (ResNet-50, 256 × 704) and 60.2 NDS (V2-99) on validation. For comparison, the best camera-only methods of 2021 scored below 50 NDS on the nuScenes test set. (NDS, the nuScenes detection score, mixes mAP with five true-positive errors: translation, scale, orientation, velocity and attribute; see Section 14.1.)

Why it matters

Sparse queries change the engineering, not just the score: memory and compute scale with the number of objects and queries, not with range squared, and the same queries are the tracker's state. The cost is irregular memory access (feature sampling at projected points), which some accelerators handle poorly, and a stronger dependence on calibration, because every sample point is projected with it.

2.5Small, far and rare objects

Benchmarks are dominated by cars and pedestrians at moderate range; safety cases are dominated by the rest. Three problems recur. Small objects: at 100 m, a 1.7 m pedestrian is about 17 pixels tall through a 1,000-pixel focal length; one 1/32 feature cell covers 32 pixels, so the object lives on two cells of the finest stride-8 map at best. Higher input resolution, a stride-4 level, tiled inference on the region around the horizon, or a dedicated far-range crop are the usual fixes, each paid in compute. YOLO26's small-object-aware assignment and DEIMv2's observation that its gains are mainly on medium and large objects both point at the same fact: small objects improve slowest. Rare classes (debris, animals, overturned vehicles, people on the road surface) are under-represented by orders of magnitude; class-balanced sampling, copy-paste augmentation and open-vocabulary auto-labelling for mining (Section 10.3) are the tools. Unusual appearance of common classes (a person in a wheelchair, a child behind a pram, a truck carrying cars) breaks detectors in ways the class list does not show; Section 13 covers how to find these cases.

Pitfall

A mean over classes (mAP) can rise while the class you care about drops, because large classes dominate the data and the per-class APs are averaged equally only across classes that exist in the split. Always read the per-class and per-distance breakdown for safety-relevant classes.

2.6What transfers to an embedded target

Most of the 2D progress transfers. Real-time DETRs export to ONNX and TensorRT, and NMS-free heads remove a variable-time post-processing step, which helps latency budgets and worst-case timing analysis. The parts that need care are deformable attention (a gather of sampled features; supported on NVIDIA through plugins or newer TensorRT versions, not on every NPU), top-K query selection (data-dependent, sometimes unsupported or slow), and INT8 quantization of attention and of the distribution heads, which are more sensitive than convolutions (Edge AI 5). For camera 3D, sparse query detectors have been deployed by several suppliers, but the multi-view feature sampling must be written as an efficient custom operator, and the temporal memory adds state that must be reset correctly after dropouts and camera faults.

Classic detector mechanics (anchors, IoU, NMS, focal loss) are in Deep learning 5–9; BEV view transforms and lidar networks in BEV 4–7; truck-specific VRU detection in Truck perception 5.3.

3Multi-object tracking

Detection says what is where in one frame; tracking says which detection is the same object as before. Without identity there is no velocity, no prediction, no "this pedestrian has been walking towards the kerb for two seconds". The field split into fast heuristic trackers on top of detectors and end-to-end networks that carry identity in their queries, and on hard benchmarks the second group now leads.

3.1Tracking-by-detection: SORT, DeepSORT, ByteTrack, OC-SORT

The standard loop (Deep learning 12.2) has four steps. A motion model, usually a Kalman filter with constant velocity, predicts where each existing track should be in the new frame. A cost matrix compares every predicted track with every new detection, by box overlap (IoU), centre distance or appearance similarity. An assignment step (the Hungarian algorithm) picks the best one-to-one pairing, ignoring pairs whose cost is above a gate. Track management creates a tentative track from an unmatched detection, confirms it after a few hits, and deletes a track after it has gone unmatched for a maximum age. SORT (2016) is exactly this with IoU cost. DeepSORT (2017) added an appearance embedding from a re-identification network, so a track can be recovered after a long occlusion by what it looks like.

Two later refinements matter in practice. ByteTrack (Zhang et al., ECCV 2022) observed that trackers threw away exactly the detections they needed: when an object is partly occluded, its detection score drops, falls below the threshold, and the track starves. ByteTrack associates in two passes: first the high-score detections with all tracks, then the remaining tracks with the low-score detections (down to about 0.1), which are trusted only if they fit an existing track's prediction. Low-score detections never start new tracks, so clutter does not create false objects. On MOT17 it reported 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1 and 63.1 HOTA at 30 FPS. OC-SORT (Cao et al., CVPR 2023) fixed the Kalman filter's behaviour during occlusion: while a track coasts without measurements, its error accumulates, and when the object reappears the filter's velocity is wrong. OC-SORT re-estimates the trajectory from the last observation to the new one ("observation-centric re-update") and adds a direction-consistency term to the cost, which helps for non-linear motion such as dancers or turning cyclists. BoT-SORT (2022) added camera-motion compensation and a better Kalman state; the family is fast, easy to tune and dominant in production.

Figure 3.1 runs both association rules on the same simulated detections. Three people move across the image: A walks right, B walks left behind A, and C, a cyclist, moves slowly and stays visible. While B passes behind A, its detection score drops to about 0.3, and in the middle third of the occlusion it is not detected at all. Some frames also contain a low-score false detection somewhere in the image (grey dots). Both trackers use the same constant-velocity prediction, the same gate and a track that needs two hits to be shown.

With the defaults (threshold 0.5, occlusion 12 frames, memory 3 frames), the SORT-style tracker loses B as soon as its score drops, deletes the track after three frames and starts a new identity when B reappears: B ends with two identities and the run has one identity switch. ByteTrack bridges the occlusion with the low-score detections and keeps one identity. Raising the memory to 30 frames also rescues SORT here, because B reappears where the prediction expects it; in a crowd, a long memory instead lets a coasting track grab the wrong person. Lowering the threshold to 0.2 makes both trackers keep B, but now a clutter detection is accepted as a high-score detection and both start a false track. An occlusion of 20 frames hides B completely for about seven frames, longer than the 3-frame memory, and both lose it. The point of the exercise is that the thresholds are not independent knobs: detection threshold, track memory and the detector's score behaviour under occlusion must be tuned together, on sequences, not on single images.

3.3Query-based end-to-end tracking: MOTR, MOTRv2, MOTIP

Tracking-by-detection keeps identity outside the network, in a hand-written state machine. End-to-end trackers put it inside. MOTR (Zeng et al., ECCV 2022) extends DETR with track queries: after each frame, every query that found an object becomes a track query for the next frame, alongside fresh detect queries for new objects. A track query attends to the new frame and finds its object again, so identity is simply "same query"; it is trained over video clips with a loss that assigns each track query to its own object. MOTR learned association well but detected worse than a dedicated detector, because detect and track queries compete. MOTRv2 (CVPR 2023) fed proposals from a strong YOLOX detector into MOTR as anchors, and jumped to the top of DanceTrack. MOTIP (Gao et al., CVPR 2025) reframes association as ID prediction: tracks carry labels drawn from a small ID dictionary, and an ID decoder predicts, for each detection of the current frame, which label it should get, from the history of labelled detections. The detector stays a plain DETR, which keeps detection strong, while the association is learned end to end. MATR (2025) adds explicit motion prediction to the queries.

On DanceTrack, a benchmark built to defeat motion heuristics (dancers in similar clothes with erratic motion), the test-set HOTA scores tabulated in a 2025 paper show the shift: ByteTrack 47.7, MOTR 54.2, OC-SORT 55.1, MOTRv2 69.9, MOTIP 69.6 (without extra data), MATR 71.3. On MOT17-style pedestrian and car scenes with mostly linear motion, the gap is far smaller, and heuristic trackers remain competitive at a fraction of the cost.

Talk track

"Tracking-by-detection is a filter plus an assignment problem; it is cheap, explainable, and the thresholds are interpretable. End-to-end trackers learn the association from data and win when appearance and motion are ambiguous, but they need video-level training data, carry state inside the network, and are harder to certify. In a car, the common answer is a learned detector with temporal queries feeding a classical multi-sensor tracker."

3.43D tracking and how tracking is measured

In 3D, the same choice repeats. CenterPoint-style trackers associate 3D boxes by their predicted velocity (a greedy closest-point match after moving each box back by its velocity), which is enough on lidar because 3D boxes rarely overlap. Camera trackers either run a 3D Kalman filter on detector outputs or use the propagated queries of Sparse4D v3 and similar models as tracks. Multi-sensor tracking (camera, radar, lidar into one track list with a filter per object) is the fusion problem of the Calibration and fusion chapter.

Three metric families are in use, and they disagree on purpose. MOTA (CLEAR MOT) counts misses, false positives and identity switches against the number of ground-truth objects; it is dominated by detection errors, so a tracker that switches identities often can still score well. IDF1 measures how long each true object keeps the same identity, so it is dominated by association. HOTA (Luiten et al., IJCV 2021) is the geometric mean of a detection accuracy (DetA) and an association accuracy (AssA), averaged over localisation thresholds, so both matter equally; it is the main metric on DanceTrack and in the KITTI tracking benchmark. nuScenes uses AMOTA, MOTA averaged over recall thresholds.

Pitfall

An identity switch on a pedestrian who steps off the kerb resets the velocity estimate and the prediction for that person, exactly when the planner needs it. MOTA barely notices one switch among thousands of correctly detected frames; look at IDF1 or AssA, and at switches per safety-relevant object.

4Semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation

Segmentation describes the scene per pixel, which is the natural format for things that are not boxes: road surface, kerbs, vegetation, sky, and objects whose outline matters. Two developments define the period: one architecture now handles all segmentation variants, and promptable models (SAM, SAM 2, SAM 3) turned segmentation into an interactive and video-capable tool that reshaped labelling.

4.1One architecture for all three: Mask2Former and its successors

Semantic segmentation assigns a class to every pixel; instance segmentation separates individual objects of countable classes; panoptic segmentation does both, "stuff" (road, sky) as classes and "things" (cars, people) as instances (Deep learning 10.1). For years each had its own design: per-pixel classifiers with an encoder–decoder for semantic, Mask R-CNN for instance, and merging heuristics for panoptic. Mask2Former (Cheng et al., CVPR 2022) unified them by predicting a set of masks, each with a class: N queries attend to the image (with attention restricted to each query's current mask, "masked attention"), and each query outputs one binary mask and one label. Semantic, instance and panoptic outputs are just different ways of combining the same set. OneFormer (CVPR 2023) trained one model for all three tasks with a task token. EoMT (CVPR 2025, "Your ViT is secretly an image segmentation model") showed that with a large pretrained ViT, most of the specialised machinery can be removed: queries are appended to the ViT tokens in the last blocks, and the model is simpler and faster at similar accuracy, the same "foundation backbone plus light head" pattern as in detection.

In a car, segmentation is used where boxes are a poor fit: drivable surface and free space for parking and low speed, kerbs and road edges, lane markings in some stacks, and as an auxiliary training target that improves a shared backbone. Cityscapes (19 evaluated classes, 5,000 finely annotated images), Mapillary Vistas and BDD100K are the usual datasets, and mIoU (mean intersection over union per class) the metric (10.4).

4.2Promptable segmentation: SAM, SAM 2, SAM 3

SAM (Kirillov et al., ICCV 2023) segments whatever you point at: a click, a box or a rough mask in, a precise mask out, for any object, trained on 1.1 billion masks (Generative vision 8.3). SAM 2 (Ravi et al., ICLR 2025) extended this to video. Its design is a streaming memory: each frame is encoded once; memory attention lets the current frame's features attend to a memory bank that holds features and predicted masks of recent frames and of every frame the user prompted, plus compact "object pointer" tokens; a mask decoder produces the mask and an occlusion score; a memory encoder writes the result back into the bank. When the object disappears behind something and comes back, the memory of earlier frames lets the model find it again. SAM 2 reports better video segmentation with about three times fewer user interactions than earlier approaches, and on images it is more accurate than SAM and about six times faster. Its training set, SA-V, has 50.9 thousand videos with 642.6 thousand "masklets" (masks tracked through time).

SAM 3 (Meta, November 2025) changes the prompt from "this object" to "this concept": given a short noun phrase ("yellow school bus"), an image exemplar, or both, it finds, segments and tracks every instance. Architecturally it puts a DETR-style detector and a SAM 2-style tracker on one shared backbone, with a separate presence head that first decides whether the concept occurs in the image at all, so the object queries only have to localise. The training data pipeline combines human and model annotators to cover 4 million unique phrases; the benchmark (SA-Co) has 207 thousand concepts. Meta reports that SAM 3 doubles the accuracy of existing systems on this "promptable concept segmentation", and about 30 ms per image with 100+ objects on an H200 GPU.

In practice

Promptable models are labelling and data tools first. A labeller clicks once per object in one frame and SAM 2 propagates the mask through the clip; SAM 3 can pre-label every instance of a new class across a dataset for humans to correct. On the vehicle, their image encoders (hundreds of millions of parameters for the larger variants) are usually too heavy; smaller distilled variants exist, but the production pattern is to use SAM-class models offline to train a compact task-specific network.

5Lane detection and road topology

Lanes look like the easiest objects in the image, painted lines on a flat road, and are among the hardest perception outputs to get right, because what the planner needs is not paint but a graph: which lanes exist, where they lead, which ones may be entered, and which signals apply to them. The research moved from image-space curves to vector maps built online, and from lanes to lane topology.

5.1Why lanes are hard

Lanes are thin (a 15 cm marking at 50 m is two or three pixels wide), long (they cross the whole image, so a detector needs a large receptive field), often invisible (worn paint, snow, glare, a truck in front), sometimes contradictory (old markings next to new ones in road works) and sometimes absent (an unmarked village road, an intersection where the "lane" is a convention). And their meaning is topological: a lane that splits into a left-turn and a straight lane is one object becoming two, which no per-pixel or per-curve representation captures.

5.2Image-space lane detection: masks, row anchors, line anchors

Four representations dominate (Figure 5.1). Segmentation (SCNN, 2018) classifies lane pixels and needs clustering and curve fitting afterwards, which is fragile. Row anchors (UFLD, ECCV 2020) turn detection into classification: for each of a fixed set of image rows, choose which column the lane crosses (or "none"); this is very fast (hundreds of FPS for small backbones in the paper) and uses global features naturally. Line anchors (LaneATT, CVPR 2021; CLRNet, CVPR 2022) start from prior lines radiating from the image border and regress horizontal offsets per row; CLRNet refines the anchors from coarse to fine feature levels and introduced a line-IoU loss that treats the lane as a whole. CLRNet reached about 80.5 F1 on CULane and became the standard baseline; successors such as CLRerNet (WACV 2024) mostly refine the loss and the matching. Parametric curves (polynomials, Bézier curves) are compact and smooth but brittle for complex shapes.

All image-space methods share one limitation: their output is a curve in pixels, and turning it into metres needs a ground-plane assumption, which fails on slopes and is sensitive to pitch (Section 7.6). OpenLane (ECCV 2022, with PersFormer) introduced 3D lane labels to push methods to predict lanes in 3D directly.

5.3Vectorized online mapping: MapTR, StreamMapNet, MapTracker

HD maps give a vehicle lane geometry for free, but they are expensive to build, go stale, and need precise localisation to use. Online mapping predicts the local map from the sensors every frame. HDMapNet (2022) predicted a rasterised BEV map and vectorised it in post-processing. MapTR (Liao et al., ICLR 2023) made it a set prediction: each map element (lane divider, road boundary, pedestrian crossing) is a query that outputs an ordered set of points, and the matching loss is "permutation-equivalent", so a closed polygon can start at any point and a line can run either way without being penalised. MapTRv2 (IJCV 2024) added auxiliary depth and segmentation losses and centerlines. StreamMapNet (WACV 2024) added temporal memory, by propagating queries and a BEV memory across frames, and proposed geographically disjoint splits, because the original splits of nuScenes and Argoverse 2 share locations between training and validation. MapTracker (ECCV 2024) treated mapping as tracking of map elements, with explicit memories per element; HisTrackMap (2025) tracks a global history map.

The progress is large, and also a lesson in reading tables. In the HisTrackMap paper's nuScenes table (24 epochs), MapTRv2 scores 61.4 mAP, a StreamMapNet-style baseline 63.4, MapTracker 71.9 and HisTrackMap 73.8; with 72 epochs, 76.6 against MapTracker's 76.1. These are on the original splits, where location memorisation helps; on geographically disjoint splits all methods score much lower, which is closer to what a car meets on a new road.

Interesting detail

The metric is AP over map elements with Chamfer distance thresholds of 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 m between predicted and true polylines. A divider shifted by 40 cm is a true positive at every threshold, which is a lot for lane keeping; benchmarks reward topology and coverage more than centimetre accuracy.

5.4Lane topology: OpenLane-V2, TopoNet, LaneSegNet and SD-map priors

A planner needs the lane graph: lanes as nodes, "can drive from this lane into that one" as edges, and the traffic elements (lights, signs) attached to the lanes they control (Planning 3.2 describes the map side). OpenLane-V2 (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks), built on Argoverse 2 and nuScenes, made this a benchmark: predict 3D lane centerlines, detect traffic elements in the front image, and predict two adjacency matrices, lane-to-lane and lane-to-element. Its OpenLane-V2 Score (OLS) averages four parts: lane detection (DET_l, with a Fréchet distance), traffic element detection (DET_t), and the square roots of the two topology scores (TOP_ll, TOP_lt), the square root lifting the low topology numbers so they count.

TopoNet (2023) put lanes and traffic elements into a graph neural network over queries. TopoMLP (ICLR 2024) showed that strong detection with simple MLP topology heads does as well. LaneSegNet (ICLR 2024) replaced centerlines with lane segments (centerline plus left and right boundaries plus boundary types), because a centerline alone does not say whether the boundary may be crossed. A strand of work adds standard-definition (SD) navigation maps, which every car has, as a prior: SMERF (ICRA 2024) and TopoSD (2024) encode the SD road graph and improve topology substantially, especially at range. The newest direction is generative: TopoGPT (June 2026 preprint) pre-trains an autoregressive transformer on 3.3 million map-only scenes to generate lane tokens (quantised Bézier centerlines), then adapts it to BEV features, and reports average gains of about 6 points on lane-level metrics over TopoNet, TopoMLP and successors. The idea is that road layouts follow strong regularities that can be learned from maps alone, without images.

5.5Mapless driving: what it means and what it costs

"Mapless" usually means: no HD map, but an SD navigation map plus online perception of lanes and topology. The benefits are scale (no map fleet, no map updates, works on any road the navigation map covers) and robustness to changes (road works appear in perception immediately). The costs are range and stability. An HD map knows the lane graph 300 m ahead around a blind corner; perception knows what the cameras see, typically reliable to 50–100 m, and its output can flicker from frame to frame, which a planner must not follow. Temporal map memories (StreamMapNet, MapTracker) and SD priors attack exactly these two problems. Several production systems announced since 2023 use mapless approaches for urban assistance, typically with a fallback to a reduced function when perception confidence is low.

Why it matters

The choice between HD map and mapless is a system decision with consequences for perception's requirements: with an HD map, perception must localise against it and detect deviations; without one, perception must produce a stable lane graph at range. The second is the harder perception task, and the reason road topology became one of the most active research areas.

6Traffic lights and traffic signs

Traffic lights and signs are small objects with a large meaning. Detecting them is the easy part. Reading their state reliably through LED flicker and glare is harder, and deciding which light or sign applies to the ego vehicle's path is the part that decides whether the car stops at the right line.

6.1Detection and state: small, bright, flickering

A traffic light head is about 30 cm wide; at 80 m and a 1,000-pixel focal length that is under 4 pixels. Lights are therefore detected in a high-resolution crop around the horizon or with a dedicated narrow-angle camera, and classified for state (red, amber, green, arrows, off, unknown) in a second stage on an enlarged crop. Three physical effects shape the design. LED lights are driven by pulse-width modulation; with a short exposure the camera can catch the "off" phase, so a red light appears dark in some frames. Sensors with LED-flicker mitigation, or exposure times longer than the PWM period, avoid this, and the state classifier must still smooth over frames. Bright lights saturate easily, so red and amber become white-yellow blobs whose colour is in the halo, not the core. And state changes are discrete events: a temporal filter must not report green for half a second after the light turned amber. Figure 6.2 in the next section shows the size and bloom problems on a real approach in fog.

Datasets are modest by modern standards: the Bosch Small Traffic Lights Dataset (ICRA 2017), the DriveU Traffic Light Dataset (DTLD, 2018, with detailed state and pictogram labels) and LISA. Detectors are ordinary (YOLO variants, RT-DETR); the work is in resolution, state classification and temporal logic.

6.2Relevance: which light is mine?

At a large intersection the camera sees five or more lights: a left-turn arrow, the straight-ahead light, lights for the cross street seen at an angle, a pedestrian signal, a tram signal. Only one or two apply to the ego path, and they may show different states. Getting this wrong is worse than missing a light, because the car may stop on green or, much worse, go on red.

Five cues are used, usually together. An HD map stores the link between each light and the lanes it controls; with lane-level localisation this solves the problem, and it is how most robotaxi stacks work. Without such a map, geometry helps (the relevant light is usually above or right of the ego lane, facing the car) but fails at wide, skewed or multi-level junctions. Pictograms on the light (arrows) can be matched to arrow markings on the road: TLD-READY (2024) detects road arrows, classifies each as relevant to the ego lane with a gradient-boosting model, and matches them to light pictograms, reporting 96% relevance accuracy on DTLD without any prior map. Learned topology (OpenLane-V2's lane-to-element relation, Section 5.4) predicts the association from the scene. And the behaviour of other traffic, cars in the same lane starting to move, is a weak but useful cue. The output should be a state per ego path (straight, left, right) with confidence and history, not a list of lights.

Pitfall

Evaluating a traffic-light function on detection mAP misses its main failure. The metric that matters is the relevant-light state per approach to an intersection, with false-go (reported green or no light, actual red) counted separately from false-stop, because their severity differs by orders of magnitude.

6.3Traffic signs: classification, supplementary panels and lane association

Sign recognition (TSR) is one of the oldest camera ADAS functions, and today it is the usual input to Intelligent Speed Assistance, mandatory for new cars in the EU since July 2024 (Standards and regulations). Detection is a small-object problem like lights; classification is a fine-grained problem with hundreds of classes (the Mapillary Traffic Sign Dataset, ECCV 2020, has over 300 classes from around the world; the older GTSRB has 43) and country-specific variants. Three things make it harder than it looks. Supplementary panels change the meaning: "60" with a panel "7–19 h" or a truck symbol applies only sometimes or only to some vehicles, and reading the panel needs text and symbol recognition. Applicability depends on the lane: a sign on a gantry above the exit lane, or at a slip road parallel to the motorway, does not apply to the main carriageway, which needs the same lane association as traffic lights. And validity depends on context: a speed limit ends at the next junction in some countries, at an end sign in others, and at the town exit for implicit limits. TSR is therefore a perception task plus a rules engine that fuses camera detections with the navigation map's speed data. Figure 6.3 shows the three difficulties as the classifier sees them.

In practice

Open-vocabulary detectors (Section 10) are useful for finding new sign types in fleet data, but the on-board classifier is still closed-set and trained per country group, because a 1% confusion between "60" and "80" is unacceptable and must be measured per class.

7Monocular and metric depth

Depth from one image is mathematically ambiguous and practically indispensable. The field went from self-supervised networks that worked on the camera they were trained on, to foundation models that produce excellent relative depth for any image, to models that recover metric scale and even the camera itself. The physics of the ambiguity still sets the limits.

7.1Why one image does not determine depth

A camera projects a 3D point onto the image by dividing by its depth, which throws the depth away (Mathematics). Every pixel corresponds to a ray, and any point on the ray gives the same pixel. A monocular depth network therefore cannot measure depth; it infers it from cues it has learned: the known size of familiar objects, the position of the contact point on the ground relative to the horizon, texture gradients, occlusion order, haze. Two ambiguities remain. Scale: a toy car close to the camera and a real car far away can produce the same image, so without a known size or a known camera height the absolute scale is undetermined. Focal length: the same image fits a near scene seen through a wide lens and a far scene through a narrow one, so a model that should output metres must know the intrinsics or estimate them (Figure 7.1).

This is why the field distinguishes relative (affine-invariant) depth, correct up to an unknown scale and shift, usually as inverse depth (disparity), from metric depth in metres. Relative depth is easier and generalises across cameras; metric depth is what a car needs.

7.2Self-supervised depth: Monodepth2

Labelled depth is scarce, but video is free. Monodepth2 (Godard et al., ICCV 2019) trains a depth network and a pose network together from monocular video: predict the depth of frame t and the camera motion to frame t+1, use them to warp frame t+1 into frame t, and minimise the photometric difference. If depth and motion are right, the warped image matches. Three tricks made it work: take the per-pixel minimum of the reprojection error over the neighbouring frames (so a pixel occluded in one of them does not dominate), mask out pixels that do not change between frames (objects moving at the camera's speed, or a stationary camera), and compute the loss at full resolution. The Deep learning chapter 11.2 explains the training. The result is up to scale (monocular video cannot fix it; a stereo pair or known speed can), fails on moving objects that move with the camera (a car ahead at the same speed is predicted at infinite depth), and is tied to the training camera.

7.3Foundation depth: MiDaS to Depth Anything V2

The generalisation problem was solved by mixing datasets. MiDaS (Ranftl et al., TPAMI 2022) trained on many datasets with incompatible depth formats by using a scale- and shift-invariant loss, so each image only has to be right up to its own affine transform; DPT (ICCV 2021) moved it to a ViT. Depth Anything (Yang et al., CVPR 2024) scaled this with 62 million unlabelled images pseudo-labelled by a teacher. Depth Anything V2 (NeurIPS 2024) made a counter-intuitive change: train the teacher only on synthetic images, whose depth labels are exact and sharp, then pseudo-label real images with it, because real depth labels (lidar, stereo) are noisy and blurry at edges. Marigold (CVPR 2024) repurposed a Stable Diffusion model as a depth estimator, showing that generative image priors know a lot about geometry.

We ran two of these on CPU on the chapter's street image (both ONNX exports take a square input, so the 1164 × 450 image is squashed; both output relative inverse depth). MiDaS v2.1 small (2020) needs 33 ms at 256 × 256 on four threads; Depth Anything V2 Small (24.8 M parameters) needs 437 ms at 518 × 518. The difference in Figure 7.2 is the progress of five years, and the fog makes it stark: Depth Anything V2 separates the poles, the signal arm, the cars and the pedestrian from the background, ranks the far parked cars (0.08 on a 0–1 inverse-depth scale) well behind the pedestrian (0.37), and puts the fog and the sky at infinity; MiDaS sees a smooth ramp from the road upwards with no objects in it, and gives the far parked cars the same inverse depth as the pedestrian at the crossing (0.42), an ordering error.

7.4Metric depth and camera-aware models

Metric models must handle the focal-length ambiguity of Figure 7.1. Metric3D (ICCV 2023) and Metric3D v2 (TPAMI 2024) transform every training image into a canonical camera (a fixed focal length) and transform predictions back, so the network learns one consistent relation between appearance and metric depth; v2 also predicts surface normals. UniDepth (Piccinelli et al., CVPR 2024) predicts the camera (as a dense field of ray directions) together with depth, so it needs no intrinsics at test time. Depth Pro (Bochkovskii et al., Apple, ICLR 2025) produces 2.25-megapixel metric depth maps in about 0.3 seconds on a standard GPU, estimates the focal length from the image, and focuses on sharp boundaries (with new boundary metrics). MoGe-2 (Microsoft, NeurIPS 2025) predicts a metric 3D point map from one image: an affine-invariant point map for relative geometry plus a separate metric scale, with training data refined using sharp synthetic labels.

The frontier has moved to multi-view geometry in one network. VGGT (CVPR 2025 best paper) takes one to many images and outputs camera parameters, depth maps, point maps and point tracks in a single feed-forward pass, replacing much of a structure-from-motion pipeline. Depth Anything 3 (ByteDance, November 2025) uses a single plain transformer with a depth-plus-ray target for any number of views, and reports about 44% better camera pose accuracy and 25% better geometric accuracy than VGGT on its benchmark, while also beating Depth Anything V2 on monocular depth.

Numbers

Depth error is usually reported as AbsRel (mean of |predicted − true| / true) and δ1 (fraction of pixels within a factor 1.25 of the truth). Zero-shot metric models on driving data are typically in the range of 5–15% AbsRel, depending on dataset and distance; a 10% error at 40 m is 4 m, more than a car length, which is why metric monocular depth is a prior or an auxiliary signal, not a range sensor.

7.5Stereo and stereo foundation models

Stereo removes the scale ambiguity with geometry: depth Z = f·B / d, from focal length f in pixels, baseline B and disparity d. Differentiating gives the error: δZ = Z² / (f·B) · δd, quadratic in distance. With f = 1,000 pixels, a 30 cm baseline and a quarter-pixel disparity error, depth is good to about 0.3 m at 20 m but only about 2.1 m at 50 m. Learned stereo replaced block matching (Classic CV) with networks that build a cost volume and refine it iteratively; RAFT-Stereo (3DV 2021) brought RAFT's recurrent refinement (Section 8.1), and IGEV (CVPR 2023) combined it with a geometry-encoding volume. The generalisation step came with FoundationStereo (Wen et al., NVIDIA, CVPR 2025), trained on a large synthetic dataset and using monocular depth priors from a foundation model inside the stereo network, which works zero-shot across datasets without fine-tuning. The recurring idea is that monocular foundation depth supplies shape and edges, stereo supplies metric scale, and the network combines them.

7.6The ground-plane trick and why pitch matters

Many production camera systems get distance to an object from its contact point with the road: if the camera is h metres above a flat road, an object whose bottom edge is at angle α below the horizon is at Z = h / tan α. This is metric, cheap and independent of object size, but it depends on knowing the horizon, that is, the camera pitch, very precisely. Braking, acceleration, load and road slope change the pitch by fractions of a degree to over a degree. Figure 7.3 shows the consequence for h = 1.5 m.

A pitch error of 0.5° makes an object at 50 m appear at about 39 m (−23%); in the other direction the estimate runs away and at 172 m becomes infinite. This is why ground-plane ranging is combined with online pitch estimation (from lane vanishing points, visual odometry or the IMU), with size-based ranging, and with radar; and why learned metric depth, which uses many cues at once, is attractive even when its average error is no better.

Talk track

"Monocular depth is a prior, stereo and lidar are measurements. The foundation models made the prior excellent and camera-independent, but scale still needs a reference: known intrinsics and object sizes, the camera height, a second camera, or another sensor. When I see a monocular range number I ask at which distance and under which pitch it was measured."

8Optical flow and scene flow

Motion is a cue that appearance cannot give: a pedestrian about to step out is distinguished from one standing still only by motion, and an object of an unknown class is noticed because it moves differently from the background. Optical flow measures motion in the image, scene flow in 3D.

8.1Optical flow: RAFT and its successors

Optical flow is a 2D vector per pixel saying where that pixel moved between two frames. Classic methods minimised a brightness-constancy energy with a smoothness term (Classic CV). RAFT (Teed and Deng, ECCV 2020 best paper) defined the modern design: encode both frames into features at 1/8 resolution, compute the correlation between every pixel of frame t and every pixel of frame t+1 once (a 4D volume, pooled into a pyramid), and then refine a flow estimate iteratively with a recurrent unit that looks up the correlation around the current estimate and predicts an update. Iterating, rather than predicting flow in one shot, is what made it accurate and robust. Its cost is the all-pairs volume, which grows with the square of the number of pixels.

SEA-RAFT (ECCV 2024) simplified training, predicted a mixture-of-Laplace distribution instead of a point estimate (giving a per-pixel uncertainty), and ran faster at similar accuracy. WAFT (Princeton, 2025) removed the cost volume altogether: it warps high-resolution features of frame t+1 with the current flow and lets a transformer compare them with frame t, which uses less memory, allows higher resolution and, per the authors, is up to 4.1 times faster than methods of similar accuracy while ranking first on Spring and KITTI when published. FlowIt (2026) adds global matching with optimal transport for large displacements and reports Sintel-test clean EPE 0.85 against WAFT's 0.94 and SEA-RAFT's 1.31, and KITTI Fl-all 3.59% against 3.56% and 4.30%: the leading methods are now within a few hundredths on KITTI, a sign that the benchmark is saturating.

Figure 8.2 shows what a flow field looks like on a real drive. It was computed with DIS, a fast classic method in OpenCV, not with RAFT, but the structure of the answer is the same: when the camera moves forward, static points stream outwards from the point the car is heading to, faster the nearer they are, and anything that breaks this pattern is moving on its own.

8.2Scene flow, motion segmentation and point tracking

Scene flow is the 3D motion of each point, usually lidar points between two sweeps. It answers "what moves" without needing to know what it is, which makes it a class-agnostic safety net for unknown objects. Labels are expensive, so the 2021–2026 progress was largely self-supervised. Neural Scene Flow Prior (NeurIPS 2021) optimised a small network per sweep pair at test time (accurate but slow); ZeroFlow (ICLR 2024) distilled such pseudo-labels into a fast feed-forward network; DeFlow (ICRA 2024) and SeFlow (ECCV 2024) improved feed-forward architectures and self-supervision; Flow4D (2024) uses a 4D voxel network over several sweeps; TeFlow (CVPR 2026) brings multi-frame supervision to self-supervised feed-forward flow. The Argoverse 2 scene flow challenge drove the field, and its evaluation moved to a class- and speed-bucketed normalised error, because averaging over all points is dominated by the static background and hides errors on pedestrians and cyclists.

Motion segmentation (which pixels or points move independently of the ego motion) is the direct consumer, both for detecting unknown moving objects and for excluding moving objects from SLAM and self-supervised depth. A related task that grew quickly is point tracking: following individual points across long video (TAP-Vid, CoTracker), useful for labelling and for training motion-aware models.

Why it matters

A detector misses what it has not been trained on; motion does not care about classes. Flow and scene flow are therefore part of the argument for coverage of unknown objects in a safety case, alongside occupancy (Section 9).

93D occupancy and free space

Boxes describe objects that fit a class list; occupancy describes space, which also covers the overturned trailer, the open car door and the pile of gravel. Since 2023 occupancy became a standard output of camera BEV stacks, with its own benchmarks, a better metric, and new representations that make it cheaper.

9.1Recap, benchmarks, and why the metric had to change

An occupancy network predicts, for each cell of a 3D grid around the car (for example 200 × 200 × 16 voxels of 0.4 m), whether it is occupied and by what class, and sometimes its motion (BEV 8.2). Occ3D (Tian et al., NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks) built voxel labels for nuScenes and Waymo by aggregating lidar over time and estimating which voxels are visible to the cameras; SurroundOcc and OpenOccupancy built similar labels. The CVPR 2023 and 2024 occupancy challenges made Occ3D-nuScenes the standard benchmark.

The first metric was voxel mIoU, computed only on voxels marked visible to the cameras. SparseOcc (Liu et al., ECCV 2024) showed that this rewards a degenerate behaviour: predicting a thick surface. Because voxels behind the true surface are outside the visibility mask, extra predictions there cost nothing, and extra predictions in front of the surface are partly offset by better coverage. Their fix, RayIoU, evaluates what a planner actually uses: cast rays from the camera (as a lidar would), take the first occupied voxel along each ray in prediction and ground truth, and count it as a true positive if the classes match and the depth difference is under a threshold (1, 2 and 4 m, averaged).

9.2Representations: dense voxels, tri-planes, sparse sets and Gaussians

A dense 3D grid is expensive: 200 × 200 × 16 cells with 64 features each is 41 million numbers per frame, and most cells are empty air. Four ideas reduce the cost. Tri-plane features (TPVFormer, CVPR 2023) store three 2D planes (top, front, side) and describe a voxel by summing its three projections. Channel-to-height (FlashOcc, 2023) keeps a 2D BEV map and reshapes its channels into height bins at the end, which runs on any accelerator that runs a BEV detector. Sparse methods (SparseOcc) predict only occupied voxels, coarse to fine, with mask queries for classes. 3D Gaussians (GaussianFormer, ECCV 2024; GaussianFormer-2, CVPR 2025) describe the scene as a set of semantic Gaussians, each with position, extent and class, that are "splatted" into voxels only when needed; a few tens of thousands of Gaussians can describe what a grid needs millions of cells for, because they adapt to where things are.

Labels are the other bottleneck: building voxel ground truth needs lidar and offline aggregation. Self-supervised occupancy trains from images and pseudo depth (or lidar) without voxel labels. GaussianFlowOcc (2025) and QueryOcc (Chalmers and Zenseact, November 2025) are examples: QueryOcc samples query points in 4D space-time from neighbouring frames, supervises them with pseudo point clouds from a metric depth model and semantic labels from a vision foundation model, and reports a semantic RayIoU of 23.6 on Occ3D-nuScenes against 18.7 for GaussianFlowOcc, about 26% better, at 11.6 FPS. Fully supervised methods score far higher on the same benchmark; the self-supervised line matters because it scales to data without lidar.

9.3Free space and what production uses

Free space is the 2D answer to the same question: where can the car drive? It is usually a polygon or a per-ray distance in the BEV, with three states that must not be merged: free (observed and empty), occupied, and unknown (not observed, for example behind a parked truck) (BEV 9.2). Production camera stacks commonly output both a 2D free-space boundary for parking and low-speed manoeuvres and a coarse 3D occupancy (often BEV with height bins) for general obstacles, with occupancy flow (motion per cell) as a further step. The value of occupancy in a safety argument is that it does not need the obstacle to be in the class list; its weakness is that it is only as good as its depth, so thin, distant or dark obstacles are where it fails.

Pitfall

Treating "unknown" as "free" because the network has no unknown class turns every occlusion into a hole in the obstacle map. Occupancy outputs need an explicit visibility or confidence channel, and the planner must treat low-confidence space conservatively.

10Open-vocabulary and foundation-model perception

A closed-set detector knows the 10 or 80 classes it was trained on. Open-vocabulary models take the class list as input, as text or example images, and foundation backbones give every task better features. Both are changing perception, but more in the data pipeline than on the vehicle so far.

10.1The models: Grounding DINO, OWLv2, YOLO-World, YOLOE, SAM 3, DINOv2/v3

The mechanism is explained in Generative vision 8.2: region features from the image are compared with text embeddings of the prompts (from a CLIP-style text encoder), so a class is a vector, not an output neuron. The main models differ in where they fuse text and image and how fast they are. Grounding DINO (ECCV 2024) fuses text and image deeply in a DETR-style detector and reached 52.5 AP zero-shot on COCO; it is accurate and heavy. OWL-ViT and OWLv2 (NeurIPS 2023) attach box heads to a CLIP ViT and scale training by self-labelling billions of web image–text pairs. YOLO-World (CVPR 2024) brought the idea to a YOLO detector with a re-parameterisable vision-language fusion, so the text embeddings of a fixed vocabulary can be baked into the weights: 35.4 AP on LVIS at 52 FPS on a V100. YOLOE (ICCV 2025) supports text prompts, visual prompts (an example box) and a prompt-free mode with a built-in large vocabulary, and reports 3.5 AP more than YOLO-Worldv2-S on LVIS with a third of the training cost. SAM 3 (Section 4.2) adds segmentation and tracking of every instance of a concept.

Under all of them sit foundation backbones. DINOv2 (TMLR 2024) and DINOv3 (August 2025) are self-supervised vision transformers whose patch features are strong enough that linear heads on frozen features give good segmentation and depth. DINOv3 scaled the teacher to 7 billion parameters and introduced "Gram anchoring", a regulariser that stops the dense patch features from degrading during long training, which had been a known problem; it ships a family of distilled smaller models. DEIMv2 and RF-DETR (Section 2.2) and Depth Anything (Section 7.3) are examples of task models built on these backbones.

10.2Experiment: prompts are a new failure surface

We ran YOLOE-11S (13.7 M parameters, 88 ms per image on four CPU threads) on the street image with two prompt lists. With COCO-style words, "car, person, traffic light, street lamp, street sign, tree, house", it found six cars, four traffic lights, the street sign (0.41) and the pedestrian at the crossing, but the pedestrian scored only 0.25, below a false "person" (0.43) on the pole and the striped rear of the minivan; "street lamp", "tree" and "house" found nothing (score threshold 0.15). With synonyms, "vehicle, pedestrian, traffic signal, lamp post, road sign, SUV, crosswalk", it found one "traffic signal" (0.46), the "road sign" (0.38), "SUV" on the minivan (0.35) and on a hatchback (0.21), a single "vehicle" (0.19) where "car" had found six, and no "pedestrian" and no "crosswalk" at all.

This is typical, not a defect of one model. Open-vocabulary detectors are trained on detection data plus grounding data whose captions favour some words over others; "person" is a COCO class, "pedestrian" is not, and large "stuff" regions such as trees, houses and crosswalks are rarely annotated as boxes. Prompt wording is therefore a hyperparameter that must be tested per class, with synonyms, and recall on a rare class must be measured, not assumed.

Pitfall

"Zero-shot" numbers on LVIS or COCO measure generalisation to common web concepts photographed from human eye height. Driving-specific rare classes (a tyre on the motorway, a fallen motorcyclist, a traffic cone lying flat) seen from a bumper camera at night are exactly where recall collapses.

10.3What they change per task, and their limits on embedded hardware

Today the main uses are off-board. Auto-labelling: Grounding DINO or SAM 3 pre-labels a new class across a dataset and humans correct it (Generative vision 9.1). Data mining: text queries over fleet data ("person lying on the road", "horse-drawn carriage") find rare scenes for the training set. Evaluation: open-vocabulary detections act as a second opinion that flags what the production detector missed. On board, two patterns appear: the YOLO-World/YOLOE trick of baking a fixed vocabulary into a fast detector (an open-vocabulary training method for a closed-set deployed model), and foundation backbones distilled into smaller networks to improve robustness. Running a text encoder and a large ViT on the vehicle is usually not justified: it costs latency and memory, its failure modes (Section 10.2) are hard to bound, and a safety case needs a fixed, tested class list.

"Open-vocabulary models moved the cost of a new class from a labelling project to a query. We use them to label and to mine data, and we ship a compact closed-set model trained on what they found, because on the vehicle we need a known class list with measured recall per class."

11Neural reconstruction for perception and data

Recorded drives are the most valuable test data a perception team has, but they are fixed: you cannot ask what the camera would have seen if the car had been half a lane further left. Neural reconstruction turns a recording into a scene that can be rendered from new viewpoints, with actors moved or removed, which serves simulation, data generation and labelling.

11.1From NeRF to 3D Gaussian splatting

NeRF (Mildenhall et al., ECCV 2020) represents a scene as a neural network that maps a 3D point and viewing direction to colour and density; an image is rendered by marching along each pixel's ray and integrating. It produced photorealistic novel views, but training took hours per scene and rendering needed hundreds of network evaluations per pixel. Block-NeRF (CVPR 2022) scaled it to city blocks, UniSim (CVPR 2023) used it for closed-loop sensor simulation with movable actors, and EmerNeRF (ICLR 2024) separated static and dynamic parts self-supervised.

3D Gaussian splatting (Kerbl et al., SIGGRAPH 2023) replaced the network with millions of explicit 3D Gaussians, each with position, covariance, opacity and view-dependent colour, optimised by rendering them with a fast differentiable rasteriser: project each Gaussian to the image as a 2D ellipse, sort by depth, alpha-blend. Rendering is real time (over 30 FPS at 1080p in the paper) and training takes minutes, because rasterising is what GPUs are built for (Rasterization vs. path tracing). It became the default representation for driving scenes within a year.

11.2Street scenes: dynamic actors, sky, exposure and real camera models

A driving log is harder than a static object captured from all sides. The camera moves along one path, so most surfaces are seen from few angles; other cars and people move; the sky is at infinity; each camera has its own exposure and white balance; and automotive cameras have fisheye distortion and rolling shutter. The street-scene methods add a component for each. Street Gaussians (ECCV 2024) and DrivingGaussian (CVPR 2024) model the static background and each vehicle as separate Gaussian sets, the vehicles placed by their tracked 3D boxes, so they can be moved or removed. OmniRe (ICLR 2025) adds deformable models for pedestrians and cyclists (including body models for people) and builds a scene graph of all actors. 3DGUT (NVIDIA, CVPR 2025) replaces the projection step with an unscented transform, so Gaussians can be rendered through distorted lenses and with rolling shutter, which matters for surround cameras.

Per-scene optimisation still takes minutes to hours per log, which limits scale. Feed-forward reconstruction removes it: a network trained on many scenes predicts the Gaussians of a new scene directly. Instant NuRec (NVIDIA, July 2026 preprint) turns a 10–20 second multi-camera log into a simulatable Gaussian scene with static and dynamic layers, a sky map and per-camera ISP corrections in about 1.5 seconds, and reports 2.01 dB higher PSNR on the Waymo Open Dataset than the strongest baseline it evaluates.

11.3Uses and limits for perception

Three uses dominate. Closed-loop sensor simulation: the planner drives in the reconstructed scene, and the perception stack sees rendered camera (and lidar) data from the new trajectory, which tests the whole loop on real places with counterfactual behaviour (Simulation and validation). Data augmentation: render the same scene from a different camera height or rig (a truck camera from car data, see Truck perception 10.1), with actors inserted or moved. Labelling: a reconstructed scene gives consistent 3D geometry across all frames, which helps label depth, occupancy and static map elements.

The limits are as important as the uses. Quality degrades as the rendered viewpoint departs from the recorded one: the unseen side of a parked car, the road surface under it, and the space between lanes appear as blur, holes or floating artefacts. Actor motion is replayed from tracks, so its accuracy is the tracker's. Lighting and weather are baked in. And rendering artefacts are a domain shift of their own: a perception network may score differently on rendered images than on real ones for reasons unrelated to the scenario. Teams validate this by rendering the original trajectory and comparing perception outputs on real and rendered frames before trusting results on new trajectories.

Why it matters

Reconstruction moves test data from "what happened" to "what could have happened here". That is the bridge between replay testing and scenario-based simulation, and the reason reconstruction is now part of validation toolchains, not just a research topic.

12Temporal and multi-task perception

A single frame is a poor basis for driving: objects are occluded, velocity is invisible, and depth from one view is ambiguous. And a car runs many perception tasks on the same images. Both facts push towards networks that keep state over time and share computation across tasks.

12.1Three ways to use time

Figure 12.1 shows the three designs (BEV 5.4 covers the BEV side). A sliding window stacks features of the last T frames, aligned by ego motion, and processes them together (BEVDet4D, BEVDepth with two frames); it is simple and stateless, but the cost per frame grows with T. A recurrent dense state keeps one BEV feature map, warps it by the ego motion and fuses it with the new frame (BEVFormer's temporal self-attention); the cost is constant, but the whole grid is state. Sparse query propagation carries only the top-K object queries, with their estimated velocity, into the next frame (StreamPETR, Sparse4D); the state is small and directly interpretable as tracks. Long-term memories go further: StreamMapNet and MapTracker keep a map memory, SAM 2 keeps a memory bank of frames.

Temporal state brings engineering obligations: it must be reset or decayed correctly when a camera drops out, when the ego motion estimate jumps, or at start-up; it makes the output depend on history, which complicates testing (the same frame gives different outputs in different sequences); and its latency must be accounted for, because a streaming network trained on 2 Hz keyframes (nuScenes annotation rate) behaves differently at 10 or 30 Hz.

12.2Multi-task networks and unified perception-prediction

One backbone with many heads (detection, segmentation, lanes, depth, occupancy) saves compute and lets tasks help each other, because depth supervision improves 3D detection and segmentation supervision improves the backbone's features. The costs are real: gradients from different tasks conflict (a loss weighting that is good for lanes may be bad for small objects), a change for one task forces revalidation of all, and the release cycle of every function is tied to one network. Teams handle this with loss balancing (uncertainty weighting, gradient normalisation), freezing the backbone for late-added heads, and careful ownership rules.

UniAD (Hu et al., CVPR 2023 best paper) took the idea to its end: tracking, online mapping, motion prediction, occupancy prediction and planning as a chain of query-based modules trained jointly, with each stage passing queries, not hand-designed outputs, to the next. VAD (ICCV 2023) and SparseDrive (2024) made it vectorised and sparse for speed. Whether this end-to-end style replaces modular stacks is a question for planning (Planning 4.3, Generative vision 10); for perception the lasting result is that query-based modules with explicit intermediate outputs give both joint training and interpretable interfaces.

In practice

A common production compromise: one shared camera backbone and BEV encoder, task heads owned by different teams, intermediate outputs (boxes, lanes, occupancy) kept explicit and tested separately, and joint fine-tuning only after each head passes its own gate.

13Robustness, the long tail and out-of-distribution inputs

Benchmark scores are averages over typical scenes; a car fails on atypical ones. Robustness work asks three questions: how much does performance drop under conditions the training data underrepresents, can the system notice when an input is unlike anything it knows, and how do you find and fix the rare cases systematically. The answers also decide which research results are worth taking into a product.

13.1Weather, light and sensor degradation

The chapter's street image is a mild example: morning fog that washes out the far end of the street, seen through a windscreen with a hazy reflection of the dashboard in its upper right. YOLO11n missed the pedestrian and the distant parked cars, the open-vocabulary detector scored a pole above the real pedestrian, and MiDaS lost every object in the fog. Real conditions are worse: night with headlight glare, rain drops and spray on the lens, fog, snow covering lane markings, dirt, a cracked windscreen, condensation, and sensor faults such as a stuck exposure or a failing pixel column. Benchmarks measure parts of this. ACDC (ICCV 2021) provides segmentation labels for fog, night, rain and snow. nuScenes-C and the RoboBEV benchmark (2023) apply synthetic corruptions (brightness, low light, fog, snow, motion blur, camera crash, frame loss, colour quantisation) to camera BEV models and showed large drops, especially when a camera is missing or frames are lost, and that temporal models degrade differently from single-frame ones. ImageNet-C (ICLR 2019) set the pattern of corruption benchmarks. Figure 13.1 shows two real degradations from the chapter's own drive.

The remedies are layered. Data first: collect and label adverse conditions deliberately rather than waiting for them in fleet data. Augmentation second: photometric and weather augmentation, simulated lens dirt and drops, dropped cameras during training. Architecture third: models that use temporal memory and multiple cameras can bridge a short degradation, but only if trained with it. And the system around the network: blockage and degradation detection on every camera (an output in its own right), with the function degrading gracefully when a camera is unusable.

13.2Out-of-distribution detection and anomaly segmentation

An out-of-distribution (OOD) input is one the model was not trained to handle: an unknown object class, an unseen environment, a sensor fault. Two research strands address it. OOD detection scores how unfamiliar an input or a region is, from the classifier's confidence (maximum softmax probability, energy scores), from feature-space distance to the training data, or from ensembles and other uncertainty estimates (Training 8.3, 9). Anomaly segmentation marks pixels belonging to objects of no known class on the road: Fishyscapes (IJCV 2021) and SegmentMeIfYouCan (NeurIPS 2021 Datasets and Benchmarks) are the benchmarks; mask-based methods (RbA, ICCV 2023, which scores a pixel as anomalous when it is "rejected by all" class masks) made large gains. CODA (ECCV 2022) collected real corner cases from driving datasets and showed that standard detectors miss many of them.

The honest summary is that OOD scores help rank inputs for review, and they help raise alarms for gross failures, but they are not reliable enough to be the only safeguard. Production systems combine class-agnostic outputs (occupancy, scene flow, free space) that do not need the class list with independent sensors (radar sees an object whatever its class), and use OOD scores mainly in the data engine.

Why it matters

The SOTIF standard ISO 21448 asks exactly these questions: which triggering conditions (glare, unusual objects) cause hazardous behaviour without any fault, and how the residual risk of unknown unsafe scenarios is reduced (Classic CV 17.2, Functional safety). Robustness benchmarks and OOD methods are the perception-side tools for that argument.

13.3Finding the long tail: corner-case mining

Rare cases are found, not imagined. The loop is the data engine (Deep learning 15.1, Training 14.4): run the current model on fleet or test-drive data; flag frames by triggers (disagreement between camera and radar, a track that appears and disappears, low confidence, a driver intervention, an OOD score, an open-vocabulary query for something specific); review and label the flagged frames; retrain; measure on a fixed set of known hard cases so the fix does not break something else. Neural reconstruction and generative models (Section 11, Generative vision 9.3) add variations of the rare cases found. The quality of this loop, more than the architecture, separates a demo from a product.

14Datasets, benchmarks and metrics per task

Every number in this chapter is tied to a dataset and a metric. Knowing what each benchmark contains, what its metric rewards, and how far it is from a production domain is half of reading a paper.

14.1The driving datasets

KITTI (CVPR 2012) was the first driving benchmark: stereo cameras and a lidar in Karlsruhe, for detection, tracking, stereo, flow and depth; small by today's standards and saturated, but still used. Cityscapes (CVPR 2016): 5,000 finely annotated frames from 50 cities for segmentation, 19 evaluated classes. Mapillary Vistas (ICCV 2017): 25,000 street-level images from around the world, diverse cameras. BDD100K (CVPR 2020): 100,000 videos from US dashcams with many tasks and conditions. nuScenes (CVPR 2020): 1,000 scenes of 20 s in Boston and Singapore, six cameras, one lidar, five radars, 3D boxes at 2 Hz, and the NDS metric; the default for camera BEV work. Waymo Open (CVPR 2020): larger and higher quality lidar, five cameras, metrics weighted by heading accuracy (mAPH). Argoverse 2 (NeurIPS 2021 Datasets and Benchmarks): seven ring cameras and two stereo cameras, 26 object categories, range beyond 150 m in its long-range tasks, and a large map-and-lidar dataset used for scene flow and mapping. Task benchmarks build on these: OpenLane and OpenLane-V2 (lanes, topology), Occ3D (occupancy), the Argoverse 2 scene flow challenge. Lane detection still uses CULane (AAAI 2018) and TuSimple; tracking uses MOT17, DanceTrack (CVPR 2022) and the 3D tracking tracks of nuScenes and Waymo.

nuScenes' NDS deserves a sentence because it is quoted so often: NDS = ⅒ × [5 × mAP + Σ (1 − min(1, error))], where the sum runs over five true-positive errors (translation, scale, orientation, velocity, attribute). Half the score is mAP (computed with centre-distance matching, not IoU), half is how good the matched boxes are, which is why temporal models that estimate velocity well gain more NDS than mAP.

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

Why can DETR-style detectors skip NMS while YOLO-style detectors traditionally need it?Basic

Dense detectors are trained with one-to-many assignment: several anchors or locations near an object are all positives, so at inference several of them fire for the same object and NMS removes the duplicates. DETR is trained with a one-to-one Hungarian match between predictions and ground truth, so a second prediction on the same object is a false positive in training and the model learns to suppress it itself. YOLOv10 and YOLO26 get the same effect with a one-to-one head trained alongside a one-to-many head. In our run YOLO11n produced 57 candidates that NMS reduced to 13.

What did RT-DETR change to make a detection transformer real time?Intermediate

The original DETR encoder ran global attention on a high-resolution feature map, which is quadratic in the number of tokens. RT-DETR's hybrid encoder runs attention only on the coarsest scale (1/32, few tokens), where global context matters, and fuses the scales with convolutions. Its decoder starts from the top-K encoder tokens instead of empty queries, which speeds up convergence and improves accuracy. The paper reports 53.1 AP at 108 FPS on a T4 for the ResNet-50 model.

Explain D-FINE's box regression in one minute.Advanced

Instead of regressing four numbers, each decoder layer predicts a probability distribution over offsets for each box edge, and each later layer adds a residual refinement to the previous distribution (FDR). The bins are non-uniform: fine near zero for small corrections, coarse further out. The last layer's distributions are used to distil the earlier layers (GO-LSD), so early layers already localise well. The distribution width doubles as a localisation uncertainty per edge. D-FINE-L reaches 54.0 AP at 124 FPS on a T4.

How does a sparse query-based 3D detector such as Sparse4D or StreamPETR work, and why is it efficient?Intermediate

It keeps a few hundred queries, each tied to a 3D anchor box. In each decoder layer a query projects key points (Sparse4D) or attends with 3D position embeddings (StreamPETR) into the camera images, gathers features from the views that see it, and updates its box. No dense BEV grid is computed, so cost scales with the number of queries rather than range squared. Temporal fusion is done by carrying the top-K queries into the next frame after ego-motion compensation, which gives memory and track identity almost for free.

What does ByteTrack do differently from SORT?Basic

SORT discards detections below a confidence threshold before association. ByteTrack keeps them: it first matches high-score detections to tracks, then matches the remaining tracks to low-score detections, which are often partly occluded true objects. Low-score detections are never allowed to start new tracks, so clutter does not create false objects. The effect is fewer lost tracks and identity switches through occlusions, as the interactive in Section 3.2 shows.

MOTA, IDF1 and HOTA: what does each measure and which would you report?Intermediate

MOTA counts misses, false positives and identity switches relative to the number of ground-truth objects; it is dominated by detection. IDF1 measures how consistently each true object keeps one identity; it is dominated by association. HOTA is the geometric mean of a detection accuracy and an association accuracy averaged over localisation thresholds, so both count equally. I would report HOTA with its DetA and AssA parts, plus identity switches on safety-relevant classes, because MOTA can look good while identities are unstable.

What is the difference between MOTR-style and MOTIP-style end-to-end tracking?Advanced

MOTR carries identity in track queries: queries that found an object become track queries in the next frame, so the same query is the same object, but detect and track queries compete, which hurts detection. MOTIP keeps a standard DETR detector and adds an ID decoder that predicts, for each current detection, which label from a small ID dictionary it should receive, given the labelled history. Association is still learned end to end, while detection quality stays that of the detector. On DanceTrack test, MOTIP reports 69.6 HOTA without extra data.

What is new in SAM 2 compared with SAM, and in SAM 3 compared with SAM 2?Intermediate

SAM segments one object in one image from a prompt. SAM 2 adds a streaming memory for video: a memory bank of recent and prompted frames and object pointers that the current frame attends to, plus an occlusion score, so it tracks and re-finds an object across frames; it is also about six times faster than SAM on images. SAM 3 changes the prompt to a concept (a noun phrase or an exemplar) and finds, segments and tracks all instances, using a DETR-style detector with a presence head on the shared backbone together with the SAM 2-style tracker.

How would you go from image-space lane detection to what a planner needs?Intermediate

Image-space lanes (row or line anchors, CLRNet) must be lifted to 3D with the camera geometry and a road model, which is sensitive to pitch and slope. The planner needs lanes in the ego frame with connectivity: which lanes lead where and which signals control them. Current practice predicts vectorized map elements in BEV (MapTR family), adds temporal map memory for stability (StreamMapNet, MapTracker), predicts lane segments and topology (LaneSegNet, TopoNet-style heads), and uses the SD navigation map as a prior for range and topology.

How do you decide which traffic light is relevant to the ego vehicle?Intermediate

By combining cues: an HD map with light-to-lane links and lane-level localisation is the strongest; without it, geometry (light above or near the ego lane), pictogram matching between arrows on the light and arrows on the road (TLD-READY reports 96% on DTLD), learned lane-to-element topology, and behaviour of vehicles in the same lane. The output should be the state per ego path with confidence and history, evaluated per intersection approach with false-go errors counted separately.

Why is monocular depth ill-posed, and how do metric depth models cope?Basic

Projection divides by depth, so every pixel corresponds to a ray and absolute scale is lost; in addition, an unknown focal length makes near-wide and far-narrow scenes look alike. Networks infer depth from learned priors such as object sizes and ground contact. Metric models handle the focal ambiguity by working in a canonical camera (Metric3D), by predicting the camera rays themselves (UniDepth), or by estimating the focal length (Depth Pro), and they still need priors on object sizes for scale.

What was the key data idea in Depth Anything V2?Advanced

Train the teacher only on synthetic images, whose depth labels are exact and sharp at edges, then use it to pseudo-label tens of millions of real images and train the students on those. Real depth labels from lidar or stereo are sparse and noisy at boundaries, which teaches blurry edges. The synthetic-to-real gap is bridged by the large real pseudo-labelled set. Our CPU run shows the result: sharp people and kiosks where MiDaS is blurry.

How does stereo depth error grow with distance, and what does that mean for a camera system?Basic

Z = f·B/d, so δZ = Z²/(f·B)·δd: quadratic in distance. With f = 1,000 px, B = 0.3 m and a quarter-pixel disparity error, the error is about 0.3 m at 20 m but 2.1 m at 50 m. Stereo is therefore excellent at short and medium range and needs a longer baseline, higher resolution or another sensor for long range.

Explain RAFT and what WAFT changed.Intermediate

RAFT encodes both frames, computes the correlation of every pixel of one frame with every pixel of the other once, and then refines a flow field iteratively with a recurrent unit that looks up correlations around the current estimate. The all-pairs volume costs memory quadratic in the number of pixels. WAFT removes the cost volume and instead warps high-resolution features of the second frame with the current flow, which saves memory, allows higher resolution, and per its authors is up to 4.1 times faster at similar accuracy.

Why did occupancy benchmarks move from voxel mIoU to RayIoU?Advanced

Voxel mIoU was computed on voxels visible to the cameras. Predicting thick surfaces was barely penalised, because voxels behind the true surface lie outside the mask, so models could gain mIoU with geometrically wrong outputs. RayIoU casts rays, takes the first occupied voxel along each ray in prediction and ground truth, and counts a hit if the class matches and the depth is within 1, 2 or 4 m. That measures what a planner uses: where the first obstacle along a direction begins.

What are the advantages of 3D Gaussians as an occupancy representation?Advanced

A dense voxel grid spends memory on empty space. Semantic Gaussians (GaussianFormer) place a few tens of thousands of primitives where things are, each with position, extent and class, and splat them into voxels only when needed. That adapts resolution to content and reduces memory. The costs are irregular computation (sorting and splatting) and the need to convert to the grid or free-space format downstream modules expect.

What is an open-vocabulary detector and how would you use one in an automotive project?Basic

A detector whose classes are text (or exemplar) embeddings compared with region features, so new classes are added by writing a prompt. Examples: Grounding DINO, OWLv2, YOLO-World, YOLOE, SAM 3. In a project I would use them off-board for auto-labelling, mining rare scenes from fleet data and as a second opinion in evaluation, and ship a compact closed-set model trained on the mined data, because on the vehicle the class list and per-class recall must be fixed and tested.

Why did 3D Gaussian splatting replace NeRF for driving-scene reconstruction?Intermediate

NeRF evaluates a neural network hundreds of times per pixel and trains for hours; Gaussian splatting represents the scene with explicit primitives rendered by a differentiable rasteriser, which trains in minutes and renders in real time. Explicit primitives are also easy to edit: actors are separate Gaussian sets attached to tracked boxes and can be moved or removed. Street-scene methods add sky, exposure and lens models (3DGUT), and feed-forward models now reconstruct a scene in seconds.

How do you judge whether a paper's improvement is real?Intermediate

Find the like-for-like baseline row (backbone, resolution, epochs, pretraining data), check the split is the same and honest (for online maps, geographically disjoint), compare the gap with seed spread and test-set noise (half a point on a few thousand images is within noise), check the latency hardware, precision and what is excluded, and look for failure cases. Prefer test-server results or means over seeds.

What is NDS and why can a temporal model gain more NDS than mAP?Intermediate

The nuScenes detection score is a tenth of five times mAP plus the sum over five true-positive errors (translation, scale, orientation, velocity, attribute) of one minus the capped error. Half of it is box quality, including velocity. A single-frame model cannot see velocity; a temporal model can, so its velocity error drops sharply and NDS rises more than mAP.

Which recent perception research would you expect in a production camera stack, and which not yet?Advanced

Expected: real-time DETR or NMS-free YOLO detectors, BEV or sparse query 3D detection with temporal memory, classic multi-sensor tracking fed by the detector's queries, vector lane and map heads with SD-map priors, BEV occupancy with height bins, foundation backbones distilled into compact models. Not yet on board, but used off-board: large open-vocabulary detectors, SAM 2/3, full-size metric depth and multi-view geometry models, neural reconstruction for simulation and data. The limiting factors are operators, quantisation, latency and testability.

Q.2Why and what if

Why does NMS hurt in a crowd of pedestrians?Why

NMS removes boxes that overlap a higher-scoring box by more than a threshold. Two people standing close together produce overlapping true boxes that look exactly like duplicates, so one is deleted. Raising the threshold keeps them but also keeps real duplicates. Set prediction avoids the rule because the model learns which overlaps are duplicates.

What if you lower the detection threshold of a SORT tracker to keep occluded objects?What if

The occluded object is kept, but clutter and false detections above the new threshold can now start tracks, producing false objects. In the interactive, threshold 0.2 keeps B but creates a false track from clutter. ByteTrack separates the two roles: low-score detections may continue a track but may not start one.

Why do sparse 3D detectors depend more on calibration than dense BEV detectors?Why

Every query samples image features at points projected through the calibration, so an extrinsic error moves every sample point; the update is based on features from the wrong place. Dense lift-splat models also use the calibration, but their learned depth distributions and convolutions over the BEV grid smooth small errors more. Both degrade, but sparse sampling has less redundancy to absorb the error.

What if a streaming detector trained at 2 Hz keyframes runs at 10 Hz in the car?What if

The time between frames is five times shorter, so ego-motion compensation and velocity propagation produce different query positions than in training, and the memory covers a shorter history for the same queue length. Performance can drop or change in subtle ways. Train with the deployment frame rate (or with random intervals), express motion in seconds not frames, and test on sequences at the real rate.

Why is "pedestrian" a worse prompt than "person" for an open-vocabulary detector?Why

The text embedding of a prompt is compared with region features learned from training data; "person" is a COCO class with vast detection data, while "pedestrian" appears in far fewer captions paired with boxes. In our run YOLOE found six persons with "person" and none with "pedestrian". Prompt wording is a hyperparameter to test per class.

What if the camera pitches down by 1° under braking and the system ranges by the ground plane?What if

Objects appear further below the horizon than they are, so distances are underestimated, by about 37% at 50 m for a camera 1.5 m high (Figure 7.3). An AEB function would see the obstacle as closer and might brake earlier or harder; in the opposite pitch direction it would see it as further away. Online pitch estimation, combination with size-based ranging and radar fusion are the remedies.

Why does Depth Anything V2 put the sky at infinity while MiDaS v2.1 small does not?Why

Real depth labels rarely contain sky (lidar returns nothing, stereo fails on texture-less regions), so models trained on them learn little about it. Synthetic training labels have exact depth everywhere, including sky at infinity, and a large teacher pseudo-labels real images consistently. MiDaS also used mixed datasets with scale-shift-invariant losses, which make the sky's absolute value unconstrained.

What if your online map model scores well on the nuScenes validation split?What if

Check whether it is the original split, where training and validation share streets; a model can partly memorise the map. Evaluate on geographically disjoint splits and on your own routes never seen in training. Scores typically drop substantially, and the remaining gap is the generalisation you can expect on new roads.

Why is occupancy useful for the safety argument even when a detector exists?Why

A detector only reports classes it was trained on; occupancy reports that space is filled, whatever fills it. Together with scene flow and radar, it covers unknown objects such as debris or an overturned trailer, which a SOTIF argument must address. Its weakness, depth accuracy on thin or distant objects, is different from the detector's, which is why both together are stronger.

What if a reconstructed scene is rendered from a path two lanes to the left of the recording?What if

Surfaces never seen in the recording appear: the far side of parked cars, road behind them, building facades at new angles. The reconstruction has no data there, so it shows blur, holes or floaters, and a perception network may react to artefacts. Results from such views need checking, for example by measuring how far from the original path real-vs-rendered perception outputs still agree.

Why did multi-task networks win in production despite gradient conflicts?Why

Compute: one backbone instead of five saves most of the inference cost on a budget-limited SoC, and the tasks help each other (depth helps 3D detection, segmentation helps features). Conflicts are managed with loss balancing, staged training and frozen backbones for late heads; the bigger cost is organisational, because every change affects all functions.

What if two papers report 0.4 points of difference on a 5,000-image validation set?What if

That is within the noise of a single run: with the simplified model of Figure 1.2 the 95% spread of an observed gap is over ±1 point at this size, before adding seed variance. Without several seeds or a larger test set, the order of the two methods is close to a coin flip. Decide on other grounds: speed, simplicity, transfer to your data.
