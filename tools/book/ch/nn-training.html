Training, evaluating and debugging neural networks

How a perception team turns data into a network it can trust: dataset design and labels, losses, optimizers and learning rates, normalisation and regularisation, augmentation and fine-tuning, reading training curves, detection metrics and calibration, leakage, domain shift and out-of-distribution inputs, systematic error analysis, reproducibility, mixed-precision and distributed training, what PyTorch does underneath, and how a model is monitored and improved after it reaches the fleet.

1The big picture: from a weak pedestrian detector to a production model

Architecture papers make it look as if a network's quality is decided by its layers. In a product team it is decided mostly elsewhere: by which images were collected and how they were labelled, by a training recipe whose dozen settings interact, by evaluation that measures what the vehicle function needs, and by the discipline of changing one thing at a time and knowing why a number moved. This chapter follows one network through that process, from a first version that looks fine on paper and fails at night to one that a function owner can sign off.

1.1Why training is an engineering discipline of its own

A trained network is the product of three inputs: the data with its labels, the architecture, and the training recipe (loss, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, augmentation, regularisation, number of steps). Ordinary software has a specification and code that implements it. A network has neither in the usual sense. The labelled data plays the role of the specification, the recipe plays the role of the compiler, and the weights are the binary. When the binary misbehaves, the fix is almost never to edit it directly; it is to change the specification (more or better data, clearer labels) or the compiler (the recipe), rebuild, and measure again.

This has two consequences that shape everything in the chapter. First, debugging is indirect. A network that misses pedestrians at night does not throw an exception; it outputs a slightly lower score. The engineer has to infer from curves, metrics and examples whether the cause is missing data, wrong labels, a bug in the input pipeline, a too-small model or a recipe problem, and each cause has a different fix. Second, evaluation is part of the product. A number such as "mAP 0.61" is only meaningful together with the dataset it was measured on, the matching rules and the operating threshold, and most painful surprises in perception projects come from evaluation that did not measure what the vehicle needed.

Why it matters

In mature perception teams most of the effort goes into data, evaluation and the improvement loop, not into new layers. The engineer who can read a loss curve, explain why the night recall dropped, and say which hundred images would fix it is the one who moves the KPI. The same skills decide whether a manager can trust a reported improvement or should ask for the slice breakdown first.

1.2The running example: a pedestrian detector for emergency braking

The network in this chapter is a single-stage, anchor-free pedestrian and cyclist detector for a front camera, of the YOLO/FCOS family described in Deep learning 7. It feeds an automatic emergency braking (AEB) function, and the function owner has written its perception requirement in vehicle terms, not in mAP: pedestrians in the ego corridor up to 40 m must be detected with at least 97% recall within three frames, at no more than one false pedestrian per hour of driving that persists for three frames. The team's first trained version, v0, reaches a respectable mAP@0.5 of 0.74 on the validation set (the numbers of the story itself are illustrative). It still fails the requirement badly: recall on night scenes is far lower than by day, small distant pedestrians are often missed, and it fires on poles and sign posts often enough to cause phantom-braking concerns.

The rest of the chapter is the story of getting from v0 to a releasable version: checking the data and labels, getting the recipe right, finding the weak slices, fixing them with targeted data and augmentation, proving the improvement is real and not noise, and setting up the loop that keeps the model good after it ships. Along the way each technique is explained on its own terms, because the same knowledge is needed to explain batch normalisation to a colleague as to explain to a customer why the night KPI improved.

Where the chapter quotes numbers from training runs, they come from small, reproducible experiments in tools/write/nn-training/experiments/. They do not train the full detector, which needs a GPU cluster and a real dataset. Instead they train a small CNN on 32 × 32 synthetic camera crops that either contain a pedestrian-like figure or only background and clutter, including poles and sign posts that look similar. The crops come from simulated drives: frames of one drive share background and lighting, some drives are at night with low gain and high noise, and pedestrians can be near, far or occluded. The data is crude, but it has the structure of a real dataset (drives, slices, near-duplicate frames, hard negatives), so the effects of splits, augmentation, imbalance, calibration and targeted data collection show up with real numbers. All runs use PyTorch 2.15 on a CPU.

Numbers

Pedestrian detection datasets in industry hold millions of labelled boxes from hundreds of thousands of frames. A front-camera detector of 5–30 million parameters typically trains for 100–300 epochs on 8–64 GPUs in one to three days. The data pipeline that feeds it (recording, selection, labelling, quality checks) costs far more than the compute: labelling a 2D box costs a few cents to tens of cents, a fully annotated frame with many objects and attributes several euros, and 3D cuboids in lidar data more.

1.3The improvement loop

All the techniques in this chapter serve one loop. A version of the model is trained with a recipe on a versioned dataset. It is evaluated on a fixed test set, not as one number but sliced by condition, distance, class and scenario. The errors are analysed, grouped by cause and ranked by how much KPI they cost. The most expensive cause is fixed with the cheapest effective change: a recipe change if the model underfits or the optimiser misbehaves, a label fix if the annotations are inconsistent, new data if a condition is under-represented, an architecture change only if the model demonstrably cannot represent what it needs. Then the loop runs again, with exactly one change per comparison wherever possible.

The loop looks obvious, yet teams lose months by breaking it in small ways. They change the dataset and the recipe in the same run and cannot tell which helped. They evaluate on a test set that has drifted because someone added "a few more hard cases". They compare two runs that differ by less than the run-to-run noise. They fix a slice by adding data and do not notice that another slice regressed. The sections on metrics (Chapter 7), error analysis (Chapter 10) and reproducibility (Chapter 11) exist to make each turn of the loop trustworthy.

Talk track

"We treat the labelled data as the specification and the training recipe as the compiler. Every model version is built from a versioned dataset and config, evaluated on a frozen test set sliced by condition and distance, and every change is justified by an error analysis that says which failures it targets. That way we know why the KPI moved, and we can rebuild any released model."

2Data: collection, annotation and dataset design

The dataset is the network's specification. Everything the model knows about what a pedestrian is, where the boundary of a box lies and which situations matter comes from the images that were recorded and the labels someone drew. A recipe can only extract what the data contains; it cannot add a night scene that was never recorded or fix a guideline that two annotators read differently.

2.1What the data has to cover

The starting point is the operational design domain (ODD) of the function: the countries, road types, speeds, weather, light conditions and camera mounting positions in which the function is supposed to work. For the AEB pedestrian function that means urban and rural roads in the target markets, day, dusk and night with and without street lighting, rain, snow, fog, low sun, tunnels, and every vehicle variant that carries the camera, because mounting height and windscreen change the image. Each dimension becomes a slice of the dataset, and the dataset is designed so that every slice that matters has enough examples to learn from and enough held-out examples to measure.

Recording more hours is the crude way to fill slices. Common situations (daytime urban traffic) fill quickly, while rare ones (a child stepping out between parked cars at night in rain) remain almost empty however long the fleet drives, because their frequency is low. Data collection therefore becomes selective: recording campaigns aimed at missing conditions (night drives in winter, specific countries), triggers in the test fleet that save only interesting moments (hard braking, a disagreement between radar and camera, a low-confidence detection), and mining existing recordings with search queries or embedding similarity for scenes like a given failure. Selecting what to label is as important as recording, because labelling is the expensive step.

Frames from one drive are strongly correlated. At 30 fps, consecutive frames are almost identical, and even frames a minute apart share weather, light, location and camera. A dataset of one million frames from a hundred drives contains far less information than one million frames from ten thousand drives. Sampling sparsely in time (for example one frame per second, or only frames where the scene changed) gives the network more diversity per labelled frame and the labelling budget more value.

Pitfall

A dataset that matches the overall distribution of driving is a poor training set for a safety function, because it is dominated by easy, common scenes. A dataset that over-represents hard cases is a good training set but a misleading test set, because its error rates do not reflect what the fleet will see. Keep the two purposes apart: enrich the training data deliberately, and measure on test sets whose composition is known and documented, reporting per slice so that the mix does not hide anything.

2.2Annotation: the labels are the requirements

A labelling guideline is the requirement specification of the network, written for annotators instead of engineers. It defines the classes (is a person pushing a bicycle a pedestrian or a cyclist; is a person on a poster a pedestrian), where a box starts and ends (around the visible part only, or the estimated full extent of an occluded person), how small an object must be before it is labelled at all, and which attributes each object carries: occlusion level, truncation at the image edge, pose, whether the person is a child. Each of these decisions changes what the network learns and how metrics are computed. A model trained on full-extent boxes and evaluated against visible-part boxes loses IoU on every occluded person without having done anything wrong.

Several mechanisms keep labels consistent. Ignore regions (or "don't care" boxes) mark areas where objects cannot be labelled reliably, such as a dense crowd far away or reflections in a shop window; detections there count neither as true nor as false positives. A minimum size, typically a box height of 20–30 pixels for pedestrians, does the same for objects too small to label consistently. Inter-annotator agreement is measured by having several annotators label the same images and comparing their boxes; disagreements point to ambiguous rules, and the agreement level sets a ceiling on the metric a model can reach. Quality assurance samples labelled batches and re-checks them, and gold sets with known-correct labels are mixed in to measure annotator accuracy.

Label noise is not only random error. Systematic noise is more damaging: one labelling vendor drawing boxes tighter than another, a guideline change halfway through a project, or pre-labels from a model that annotators accept too readily. Random noise mostly slows learning and lowers the ceiling; systematic noise teaches the model a wrong rule and makes the test set measure that rule. Version the guideline together with the labels, and record which guideline version and which vendor produced each label, so that a later analysis can find and fix a systematic shift.

Interesting detail

Pre-labelling with a large offline model, with humans correcting the result, can cut annotation cost several times. It also changes the error profile. Annotators tend to accept boxes that look plausible, so the labels inherit the teacher's misses on unusual objects (a person in a wheelchair, a person lying down), exactly the cases a safety function cares about. Teams that use pre-labels usually audit a random sample against fully manual labels and track misses that the pre-labeller did not propose.

2.3Training, validation and test splits

Data is split into three sets with different jobs. The training set is what the optimiser sees. The validation set is used to make decisions: choosing hyperparameters, choosing the checkpoint, deciding whether a change helped. The test set is used to report the final result and is looked at as rarely as possible, because every decision made by looking at it fits the model a little to that set. In a product team the test set becomes a frozen, versioned release test set plus a regression suite of known hard scenarios, and changing it is a deliberate, reviewed act.

The unit of splitting is what makes a split honest. If frames are assigned to sets at random, consecutive frames of the same drive land in both training and test, the test set contains near-copies of training images, and the measured accuracy says how well the network remembers rather than how well it generalises. The split must be made by drive at least, and preferably by something coarser that matches how the product will meet new data: by recording day, by city or region, by vehicle. Section 9.1 measures how much a frame-level split inflates the result in the companion experiment.

How large must the validation and test sets be? Large enough that the differences you care about are bigger than the statistical noise of the measurement. A recall measured on \(n\) pedestrians has a standard error of about \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\): on 200 night pedestrians at a recall of 0.80 that is 0.028, so a change from 0.80 to 0.83 is not distinguishable from chance. For safety claims the numbers become large: to show a miss rate below 1% with 95% confidence and no misses at all, you need about 300 relevant pedestrians (the "rule of three", upper bound \(\approx 3/n\)); with five misses in 500 the 95% lower bound on recall is 0.979, not 0.990. The test set is sized from the claims it must support, slice by slice.

Pitfall

The validation set wears out. After a hundred experiments selected by validation score, part of the remaining gain is fitting the validation set's peculiarities. Keep the test set for milestones, refresh the validation set occasionally with new drives, and be suspicious of improvements that appear on validation but not on the test set or the regression suite.

2.4Dataset versions and documentation

Because the data is the specification, it is versioned like source code. A dataset version fixes the exact list of frames, the label version of each, the guideline version, and the split assignment. It is immutable: adding mined night drives creates a new version, and the release notes of a model name the dataset version it was trained and tested on. A short data card describes each version in words: where and when it was recorded, which sensors and camera variants, the slice composition, known gaps (for example "no snow in country X"), labelling vendor and guideline, and licence or privacy constraints such as face and licence-plate anonymisation. Chapter 11 covers the tools; the point here is that without versioned data no result in this chapter is reproducible.

Talk track

"Our dataset is designed from the ODD: every condition the function must handle is a slice with enough training data and a held-out test portion sized for the claim we want to make. We split by drive and region, never by frame, so the test set measures generalisation. Labels follow a versioned guideline with ignore regions, occlusion attributes and measured inter-annotator agreement, and every dataset version is immutable and documented."

3Losses, optimizers and learning-rate schedules

Training is an optimisation problem: find weights that make a loss small on the training data, in a way that also keeps it small on data the network has not seen. The loss defines what "wrong" means, the optimizer decides how each weight moves in response, and the learning-rate schedule decides how far it moves at each point of training. Of all the settings in a recipe, the learning rate is the one that most often decides between a good model and no model at all.

3.1The loss: turning "wrong" into a number

For classification the standard loss is cross-entropy: the network outputs a logit per class, softmax turns the logits into probabilities, and the loss is the negative logarithm of the probability given to the correct class, \(L = -\log p_y\). It is small when the network is confident and right, grows slowly as confidence in the right class falls, and becomes very large when the network is confident and wrong (\(-\log 0.01 = 4.6\)). The gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logits is simply \(p - \text{onehot}(y)\), which is why it trains so well: the push on each logit is proportional to how wrong its probability is.

Two values of cross-entropy are worth remembering because they turn a loss curve into a diagnosis. At initialisation, a well-initialised network outputs nearly uniform probabilities, so the loss should start near \(\ln C\) for \(C\) classes: 0.693 for two classes, 2.30 for ten, 4.6 for a hundred. A starting loss far above that means the initial outputs are confidently wrong (a bias or scaling problem); a loss that stays at \(\ln C\) means the network is not learning anything and outputs the prior. And a loss that falls below the entropy of the labels themselves on the validation set is impossible, so label noise sets a floor.

A detector is trained with a sum of losses. The classification term decides whether each location contains a pedestrian; because almost all locations are background, it is usually a focal loss, \(-(1-p_y)^\gamma \log p_y\) with \(\gamma \approx 2\), which down-weights the millions of easy background locations so that the few hard ones dominate the gradient (Deep learning 7.3). The box regression term compares predicted and labelled boxes; L1 or smooth-L1 losses on coordinates were standard, and IoU-based losses (GIoU, DIoU, CIoU) are now common because they optimise overlap directly and are invariant to box size. Anchor-free detectors add a quality or centerness term that predicts how well a location's box will match, so that NMS keeps the best-localised box. Which locations count as positives for a given object is decided by an assignment rule (centre region, IoU with anchors, or a dynamic matching such as SimOTA or the Hungarian matching of DETR), and that rule matters as much as the loss formula.

Summing different losses raises the question of weights. A box loss measured in pixels and a classification loss in nats have different scales, and their gradients compete for the shared backbone. Recipes fix the weights by experience (a typical YOLO recipe weights box, objectness and class terms by fixed factors), and multi-task networks sometimes learn them, for example by weighting each task with its estimated uncertainty. A practical check is to log each term separately: if one term stops falling while the others continue, its weight or its assignment is wrong.

Numbers

Cross-entropy at initialisation: \(\ln 2 = 0.693\), \(\ln 10 = 2.303\), \(\ln 80 = 4.38\) (COCO classes). A confident wrong answer at \(p_y = 0.01\) costs 4.6; a confident right one at 0.99 costs 0.01. Focal loss with \(\gamma = 2\) reduces the loss of a background location already classified at \(p = 0.99\) by a factor of \(10^4\) relative to cross-entropy.

3.2Optimizers: SGD, momentum, Adam and AdamW

Every optimizer starts from the same signal: the gradient of the loss on a mini-batch, computed by backpropagation. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) moves each weight a small step against its gradient, \(w \leftarrow w - \eta\, g\), with learning rate \(\eta\). Its weakness is geometry. The loss surface of a network is badly conditioned: steep in some directions and nearly flat in others. A learning rate small enough to be stable in the steep directions makes almost no progress in the flat ones, and the iterate zig-zags across a narrow valley instead of moving along it.

Momentum fixes the zig-zag by averaging gradients over time: \(v \leftarrow \beta v + g\), \(w \leftarrow w - \eta v\), with \(\beta \approx 0.9\). Components that flip sign from step to step (across the valley) cancel out; components that point the same way (along the valley) add up, so the effective step along the valley grows by about \(1/(1-\beta) = 10\). SGD with momentum is still the optimizer of choice for many CNN detectors because it is cheap and generalises well with a good schedule.

Adam goes further and gives every weight its own step size. It keeps an exponential average of the gradient \(m\) (momentum) and of the squared gradient \(v\), and updates \(w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \hat m / (\sqrt{\hat v} + \varepsilon)\), where the hats denote a bias correction for the first steps. Dividing by the root-mean-square gradient normalises each coordinate: a weight with consistently tiny gradients gets the same step size as one with large gradients. That makes Adam far less sensitive to the scaling of different layers and the default for transformers. AdamW changes one detail that matters a great deal: weight decay is applied directly to the weights, \(w \leftarrow w - \eta \lambda w\), instead of being added to the gradient where Adam's normalisation would scale it differently for every weight (Section 4.2). Today "Adam" in a recipe almost always means AdamW.

The figure shows the mechanism in two dimensions. With plain SGD, raise the learning rate towards 0.08 and the path zig-zags across the steep direction while crawling along the flat one; above 0.08 it diverges, because a step of \(\eta\) on a curvature of 25 overshoots by more than it corrects (the stability condition is \(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\)). With momentum the same learning rate makes several times more progress along the valley, and for very small rates the path is smooth. Adam is insensitive to the curvature but not to the learning rate: it moves each coordinate by roughly \(\eta\) per step at first, so too small a rate crawls and too large a rate makes it rattle around the minimum until the step size is reduced. In a real network the steep directions are set by the largest eigenvalues of the Hessian, which change during training, and that is the main reason learning-rate schedules and warm-up exist.

Pitfall

Copying a learning rate between optimizers is a classic error. SGD with momentum on a CNN typically uses 0.01–0.1; AdamW typically uses \(10^{-4}\)–\(10^{-3}\). An AdamW recipe run with an SGD learning rate diverges in the first steps; an SGD recipe with an Adam rate trains for days without visible progress.

3.3The learning rate and its schedule

The learning rate is the most important hyperparameter, and it should not be constant. Early in training the weights are random, gradients are large and inconsistent, and the curvature of the loss is high; a large step can destroy the network (a "loss spike" from which it never recovers) or push batch-normalised layers into a bad state. In the middle of training, a large step is useful: the noise of large steps keeps the optimiser out of sharp, narrow minima, which tend to generalise worse. At the end, small steps let the weights settle into the bottom of the basin they are in, and the loss drops noticeably whenever the rate is reduced.

Schedules encode this. Warm-up raises the rate linearly from near zero over the first few hundred to few thousand steps; it is essential for transformers and for large batches and cheap insurance for everything else. Step decay divides the rate by 10 at fixed epochs (the classic ResNet recipe); each drop shows as a visible step down in the loss. Cosine decay lowers the rate smoothly along half a cosine to near zero and is the modern default. One-cycle rises to a peak and falls again within one run. The shape matters less than having a warm-up, a high middle phase and a low end; most of the differences between published schedules are within run-to-run noise for a given budget.

How to find the peak rate? The learning-rate range test trains for a few hundred steps while increasing the rate exponentially, from \(10^{-7}\) to 1 or more, and plots the loss against the rate. The loss is flat at first, then falls, then becomes noisy and explodes. A good peak rate lies a little below the point where the loss is lowest, typically a factor of 3–10 below the explosion point. It costs minutes and replaces days of grid search.

The rate is linked to the batch size. Doubling the batch halves the noise of the gradient estimate, so the same number of epochs takes half as many steps with a more reliable direction. The linear scaling rule for SGD says: multiply the learning rate by the same factor as the batch size, and add warm-up, which held for ImageNet training up to batches of about 8 000. For Adam a square-root scaling is often closer. Beyond a critical batch size that depends on the problem, larger batches no longer reduce the number of steps needed and only waste compute, which is one reason distributed training (Chapter 12) does not scale forever.

In practice

A robust default for a CNN detector: SGD with momentum 0.9 and Nesterov, or AdamW; batch 64–256 across GPUs; peak rate from a range test; linear warm-up over 1–3 epochs; cosine decay to 1–5% of the peak; weight decay \(5 \times 10^{-4}\) for SGD or 0.05 for AdamW; an exponential moving average of the weights for evaluation (Section 4.3). Change the schedule last; change data and labels first.

3.4Batch size and what a step really sees

The batch size is mostly decided by memory and hardware, not by learning theory. On one GPU a detector at 960 × 544 may fit 8–16 images; across 32 GPUs the global batch is 256–512. Within that freedom, three effects matter. Small batches give noisy gradients, which regularise a little but make steps unreliable; batch normalisation in particular needs enough samples per GPU to estimate statistics (Section 4.1). Large batches need the rate scaling and warm-up above, and beyond the critical batch size they waste compute. And the composition of a batch matters for detectors: sampling strategies that guarantee a share of night images or rare classes in every batch change what the gradient sees at each step, which is one of the tools against imbalance (Section 6.4).

Talk track

"The loss says what wrong means; for detection that is focal classification plus an IoU-type box loss with a matching rule that decides which locations are positives. The optimizer turns gradients into steps: SGD with momentum for many CNNs, AdamW for transformers. The learning rate matters more than anything else in the recipe, so we find the peak with a range test, warm up, decay with a cosine, and scale the rate with the batch size."

4Normalisation and regularisation

Two families of techniques sit between the optimizer and the data. Normalisation layers keep the signals inside the network at a sensible scale, so that deep networks can be trained with large learning rates at all. Regularisation techniques make it harder for the network to fit the training set in ways that do not carry over to new data. Both are easy to switch on and easy to get subtly wrong, and several of the most common training bugs live here.

4.1Batch normalisation: what it does in training and at inference

Batch normalisation (BN) sits after a convolution and normalises each channel over the current mini-batch: for channel \(c\) it computes the mean \(\mu_B\) and variance \(\sigma_B^2\) over all images and pixels of the batch, standardises \(\hat z = (z - \mu_B)/\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}\), then applies a learned scale and shift, \(y = \gamma \hat z + \beta\). The network can still represent any scale it wants through \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\), but the optimiser no longer has to fight against activations whose scale drifts as the weights of earlier layers change. In practice BN allows learning rates several times higher, makes training much less sensitive to initialisation, and adds a little regularisation, because each image's normalisation depends on the other images in its batch.

The price is that BN behaves differently in training and at inference. During training it uses batch statistics and updates running averages of the mean and variance (with momentum 0.1 in PyTorch). In evaluation mode it uses the running averages instead, which makes it a fixed per-channel affine map that the deployment compiler folds into the preceding convolution (Edge AI 6.1). Everything that goes wrong with BN goes wrong at this boundary.

Forgetting model.eval() before validation is the classic: the network normalises each validation batch with its own statistics, results depend on batch size and composition, and a batch of 1 produces nonsense. The opposite mistake, leaving BN layers in training mode while fine-tuning on a small, narrow dataset, overwrites the running statistics with those of the new data. Small per-GPU batches (2–4 images, common for high-resolution detection) give noisy batch statistics and poor running averages; SyncBN computes the statistics across all GPUs at the cost of a communication step per BN layer, and GroupNorm avoids the batch dimension entirely by normalising over groups of channels within each image. LayerNorm, which normalises over the features of each token, is the standard in transformers and behaves identically in training and inference, but cannot be folded into a convolution.

Pitfall

Training data and deployment data must be normalised identically before the network, not only inside it. A model trained on images scaled to [0, 1] and deployed on images in [0, 255], or trained on RGB and fed BGR, still produces outputs, just worse ones. The symptom is a model that validates well in Python and loses several points on the target. Write the input normalisation into the model or into a single shared function, and test the deployed preprocessing against the training preprocessing on the same raw frame.

4.2Weight decay

Weight decay shrinks every weight a little towards zero at each step. For plain SGD it is identical to adding an L2 penalty \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2\) to the loss, whose gradient \(\lambda w\) pulls every weight inwards. It discourages solutions that rely on a few very large weights, which tend to be sharp and to fit noise. In networks with batch normalisation the effect is more subtle: the output of a BN-normalised layer does not depend on the scale of the weights before it, so decay does not change what the layer computes but keeps the weight norm small, which raises the effective learning rate of those weights. That is one reason why weight decay and learning rate have to be tuned together.

With Adam, the L2-penalty form and real weight decay are no longer the same. If \(\lambda w\) is added to the gradient, Adam divides it by the running RMS of the gradient, so weights with large gradients are barely decayed and weights with small gradients are decayed strongly, which is not what anyone intended. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, decoupled from the adaptive scaling, and generalises noticeably better; this is the reason for its existence. Typical values: \(10^{-4}\)–\(5 \times 10^{-4}\) for SGD on CNNs, 0.01–0.1 for AdamW. Biases and the \(\gamma, \beta\) of normalisation layers are usually excluded from decay.

4.3Dropout, label smoothing, stochastic depth, early stopping and EMA

Dropout sets a random fraction of activations to zero during training and scales the rest up so that the expected value is unchanged; at inference it is switched off. Each training step thus trains a different thinned network, and the full network behaves like an average of them, which reduces co-adaptation of neurons. It was essential for the fully connected layers of early CNNs; in modern convolutional backbones it is rarely used, because BN and data augmentation do the job, but it remains common in transformer blocks and in classification heads. Stochastic depth (drop path) is its counterpart for residual networks: whole residual branches are skipped at random, which regularises very deep networks and shortens training steps.

Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target with, for example, 0.9 for the correct class and the remaining 0.1 spread over the others. The network is then never pushed towards infinite logits, which reduces overconfidence and often improves accuracy slightly; for detectors it is used with care, because it also changes the meaning of the score threshold. Early stopping keeps the checkpoint with the best validation metric instead of the last one. It is cheap insurance against overfitting, but with a cosine schedule the best checkpoint is usually near the end anyway, and selecting among hundreds of checkpoints by validation score is another way of fitting the validation set.

An exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights, \(\bar w \leftarrow 0.9999\,\bar w + 0.0001\,w\) after each step, is kept next to the training weights and used for evaluation and release. It averages out the noise of the last few thousand steps and typically gives a small but consistent gain at no training cost; YOLO-family recipes use it by default. When EMA weights are used, BN running statistics must be those of the EMA model, which libraries handle by averaging the buffers as well.

Talk track

"BatchNorm normalises each channel with batch statistics during training and with running averages at inference, so it lets us train deep nets with high learning rates and then disappears into the convolution on the target. Most BN bugs are train/eval mismatches or batches too small for good statistics. For regularisation we rely mainly on data and augmentation, plus decoupled weight decay with AdamW, an EMA of the weights for the released model, and dropout or stochastic depth where the architecture benefits."

5Augmentation, transfer learning and fine-tuning

The cheapest training data is data you already have, shown to the network in a modified form, and the cheapest training is training that someone else has already done. Augmentation creates new training examples from old ones; transfer learning starts from a network that already knows what edges, textures and objects look like. Together they are the reason a perception team can train a good detector on tens of thousands of labelled frames instead of tens of millions.

5.1Data augmentation: teaching invariances

Augmentation applies random transformations to each training image so that the network sees a slightly different version every epoch. Each transformation encodes a claim: "the label does not change if the image changes like this". Horizontal flips claim that a pedestrian walking left is still a pedestrian when mirrored; random scaling and cropping claim that a pedestrian at 20 m and at 30 m are the same class; brightness, contrast and gamma changes claim that a pedestrian under a street lamp is still a pedestrian. When the claim is true, augmentation multiplies the effective dataset and makes the network invariant to the nuisance; when it is false, it teaches the network a wrong rule (flipping images of traffic signs with arrows or text, or of left-hand traffic scenes for a right-hand market).

For a camera network the most valuable augmentations are the ones that mimic the camera and the environment: exposure and gain changes, sensor noise at realistic levels, motion blur, defocus, the colour shifts of different ISP tunings, lens vignetting, dirt and water drops on the windscreen, and the geometric effect of different mounting heights and pitch angles. Generic photo augmentations designed for internet images often do less. Detection adds a constraint: every geometric transformation must transform the boxes too, and crops must handle boxes that are cut at the edge. Detection recipes also use mosaic (four images tiled into one, which shows objects at many scales and contexts per step), mixup (blending two images and their labels) and copy-paste (pasting segmented objects into other scenes, a way to multiply rare classes).

The companion experiment shows how much a camera-style augmentation can matter. The classifier is trained only on daytime drives and then tested on daytime and night drives. Without augmentation it reaches 98.9% accuracy by day but only 52.0% at night, where the image is darker, noisier and has lamps in it. Adding geometric augmentation alone (flips and shifts) gives 53.1% at night. Adding photometric augmentation that imitates low gain, gamma changes and sensor noise lifts night accuracy to 70.3% with day accuracy at 98.0%, without a single night image in the training set. Augmentation cannot replace real night data, which has phenomena (glare, headlight reflections, motion blur at long exposure) that a simple gain change does not produce, but it buys robustness to the predictable part of the shift.

Pitfall

Augmentation runs in the data loader on the CPU, and heavy augmentation pipelines are a common reason why GPUs sit idle. If GPU utilisation is low and the step time does not change with model size, the input pipeline is the bottleneck (Section 12.6). Equally common is augmentation that is silently broken, for example boxes not transformed with the image. Always look at a grid of augmented training images with their boxes drawn, before the first long run.

5.2Transfer learning: starting from a pretrained network

The early layers of any vision network learn similar things: edges, colour blobs, textures, then parts. A backbone pretrained on a large, diverse dataset (ImageNet classification, or today more often self-supervised pretraining on hundreds of millions of images, as with DINOv2) has already learned these features. Starting a detector from such a backbone instead of random weights converges several times faster, needs much less labelled data, and usually generalises better, because the pretrained features were shaped by far more variety than the task data contains. Training a production backbone from scratch on one's own data alone is the exception.

There are three ways to use a pretrained network, in increasing order of how much they change it. A linear probe freezes the whole backbone and trains only a new head: cheap, robust with very little data, and a good test of how useful the features are, but limited when the new domain differs from the pretraining domain. Full fine-tuning trains everything with a small learning rate. Partial fine-tuning freezes the earliest layers (which are generic) and trains the later ones, or uses lower learning rates for earlier layers (layer-wise learning-rate decay).

The companion experiment pretrains the small CNN on 10 000 daytime crops (98.7% daytime accuracy) and then adapts it to night using only 300 labelled night crops. The result is a useful warning against expecting miracles from pretraining when the domains differ strongly. Used as it is, the day model is at chance at night (51.3%). A linear probe on its frozen features reaches only 57.6%, which is below a network trained from scratch on the night crops (61.9%): features learned from bright, clean crops do not describe dark, noisy ones. Fine-tuning all layers gives 63.8% with a small learning rate and 67.2% with a large one. The daytime accuracy afterwards shows the price. After the linear probe, which only changed the head, it is still 90.4%; after the gentle fine-tune 74.9%; after the aggressive one 51.9%, which means the model has forgotten daytime completely. Fine-tuning on the 300 night crops mixed with 300 daytime crops (replay) reaches 67.6% at night and keeps 97.2% by day.

Interesting detail

Why does the learning rate matter so much when fine-tuning? The pretrained weights sit in a good region of the loss landscape for the old data. Large steps on a small, narrow dataset move them far out of it, towards a solution that fits the few new examples and has forgotten what the pretraining taught; the companion run that gained most at night lost all of its daytime ability. That loss of earlier abilities is called catastrophic forgetting. It is the same effect that makes a model fine-tuned on a new camera worse on the old one unless old data is mixed into the fine-tuning set.

5.3Fine-tuning recipes in a perception project

Fine-tuning appears in three situations in a vehicle project. The first is adapting a pretrained backbone to the task, the default described above. The second is adapting an existing production model to a new camera or vehicle: a different lens, sensor, ISP tuning or mounting position changes the image statistics and the apparent size and perspective of objects. Fine-tuning on a modest amount of labelled data from the new setup, mixed with data from the old setups to avoid forgetting, is usually much cheaper than retraining. The third is continuous improvement: each new model version is trained from the previous one plus newly mined data, rather than from scratch.

The recipe rules follow from the mechanism. Use a learning rate 3–10 times lower than for training from scratch, with a short warm-up; freeze BN statistics (keep BN layers in evaluation mode) when the fine-tuning batches are small or narrow; mix in old data (often called replay) when the old domain must keep working; and evaluate on the old test sets as well as the new one, because forgetting is silent unless measured. Starting each version from the previous one is convenient but has a cost: the lineage of a model becomes long and hard to reproduce exactly, so teams periodically retrain from the pretrained backbone on the full current dataset.

Talk track

"Augmentation encodes the invariances we know are true; for cameras that means exposure, noise, blur, ISP colour and mounting geometry, not just flips and crops. We always start from a pretrained backbone, fine-tune with a lower learning rate, and when we adapt to a new camera we mix in old data and test on the old test sets too, because catastrophic forgetting is silent unless you measure it."

6Training pathologies: reading the curves

A training run produces a handful of curves, and most problems announce themselves in them long before the final metric. Learning to read them is the most practical skill in this chapter: the shape of the training and validation loss, the gradient norm and the learning rate together usually say whether the problem is the data, the model, the recipe or a bug, and therefore what to change next.

6.1Sanity checks before the first long run

Most wasted GPU-days come from bugs that a five-minute check would have found. Experienced practitioners run a short ritual before any long training. Look at the data as the model will see it: a grid of training images after augmentation, with labels drawn, plus a histogram of input values. Swapped colour channels, boxes off by a scaling factor, normalisation applied twice and labels from the wrong frame are all visible here and nowhere else. Check the loss at initialisation: it should be close to \(\ln C\) for classification (Section 3.1), and the detector's box loss should be in the expected range. Overfit a single batch: train on one batch of 8–32 images for a few hundred steps with augmentation off. A correct model and loss drive the training loss to almost zero; if it does not, there is a bug in the model, the loss, the label encoding or the optimizer setup, and no amount of data will fix it.

Two more checks catch the subtler bugs. Compare a few predictions on training images with their labels after the overfit test; the model should reproduce them exactly, which also checks the decoding code. And check that gradients reach every parameter: a parameter whose gradient is None or exactly zero after backward() is disconnected (a layer created but never used, a detached tensor, a frozen layer that should train). A simple baseline (the previous model version, or a simple model trained with a known-good recipe) gives the number against which every change is judged.

In practice

Make the sanity checks a script that runs in CI on every change to the training code: a tiny dataset, 200 steps, asserting that the initial loss is in range, the final loss is below a threshold and no parameter is without gradient. It catches regressions in the data pipeline and loss code within minutes, before they cost a night of cluster time.

Memorising noise is possible for a network with enough parameters, which is why a falling training loss alone proves nothing. A large, growing gap from the very first epochs, with validation never improving, points to labels that do not belong to their images: a misaligned data join, an off-by-one between frames and labels, or a broken annotation export.

Pitfall

Do not read loss curves in isolation from the learning-rate curve. A sudden drop in both losses at an epoch boundary is usually a step in the schedule, not the model "understanding" something; a rise in validation loss right after warm-up ends may be the learning rate peaking, not overfitting. Log the learning rate, the gradient norm and each loss term on the same time axis.

6.3Vanishing and exploding gradients, NaNs and loss spikes

Backpropagation multiplies gradients by the Jacobian of every layer on the way back to the input. If the typical factor per layer is below one, the gradient shrinks exponentially with depth and the early layers stop learning (vanishing gradients); if it is above one, it grows exponentially and the weights explode (exploding gradients). Saturating activations such as the sigmoid make vanishing worse, because their derivative is at most 0.25 and near zero for large inputs. Three inventions made deep networks trainable: careful initialisation that keeps the variance of activations and gradients constant across layers (He initialisation for ReLU), normalisation layers, and residual connections, which give the gradient a path through the identity that bypasses every layer's Jacobian.

The companion experiment measures the gradient of each convolution in a 20-layer stack at initialisation, relative to the size of its weights. With sigmoid activations and no normalisation, the first layer receives a relative gradient \(1.0 \times 10^{18}\) times smaller than the last: it effectively does not learn. ReLU without normalisation still loses 8 orders of magnitude between the last and the first layer, because PyTorch's default initialisation for convolutions draws weights with one sixth of the variance that He initialisation would use for ReLU, so the signal shrinks by a factor of about \(\sqrt 6 \approx 2.4\) per layer in both directions. ReLU with BN keeps the gradients within a factor of 9 across all twenty layers, whatever the initialisation, and adding residual connections gives the flattest profile.

Exploding gradients show up differently: a loss that suddenly jumps by orders of magnitude, then becomes inf or NaN. In the companion experiment, a 7-layer network without BN trained with SGD at a rate of 0.3 produced the loss sequence 0.70, 3.66, 1.57, 0.73, 373, \(3.5 \times 10^{7}\) over its first 6 steps and NaN at step 7, with the gradient norm reaching \(1.3 \times 10^{9}\). Gradient clipping, which rescales the whole gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold, prevents a single bad batch from destroying the weights; it is standard in transformer and detector recipes, with a threshold of 1–10 on the global norm. With clipping at 1.0 the same unstable configuration stays finite for all 150 steps (highest loss 2.82), but its loss ends near \(\ln 2\) (0.702 over the last 20 steps): it survives and learns nothing. Clipping treats the symptom, not the cause, so a run that clips on most steps needs a lower learning rate or a longer warm-up.

When a NaN appears, find its first occurrence rather than its consequences. Common sources are a division by zero or a logarithm of zero in a custom loss (for example IoU of a zero-area box), an exponential that overflows in FP16 (Section 12.2), a corrupt image or label with infinite coordinates, and a learning rate spike. PyTorch's anomaly detection (torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True)) reports the forward operation whose backward produced the first NaN, at a large cost in speed, so it is switched on only to reproduce a failure. Logging the global gradient norm every step is the cheap early warning: spikes in the gradient norm precede spikes in the loss.

6.4Class imbalance

Imbalance is the normal state of perception data. Most image locations contain no object; pedestrians are rarer than cars; children, wheelchairs and people lying on the road are rarer still. Two separate problems hide under the one word. The first is a training problem: the gradient is dominated by the frequent class, so the rare class is learned slowly. The second is a measurement problem: accuracy, and to a lesser degree any average over classes, rewards a model that ignores the rare class.

The companion experiment makes pedestrians rare: 3% of the crops contain one. A model that always says "no pedestrian" would be 97.0% accurate. With plain cross-entropy the trained model reaches 97.0% accuracy and an average precision of 0.072, but at the default threshold of 0.5 it finds 0% of the pedestrians. Weighting the pedestrian class ten times higher in the loss raises the recall at threshold 0.5 to 34%, with average precision 0.222; focal loss gives 0% and 0.259. Read these numbers slowly. The plain model's accuracy equals the always-negative baseline, and its AP is barely above the 3% base rate: with about 300 pedestrian crops, augmentation and 20 epochs, the gradient from the rare class was too weak for the model to learn anything about it, and accuracy hid that completely. Both corrections make the model learn something, and AP roughly triples, but neither makes it good: at 90% precision both find only 2% of the pedestrians. Focal loss also shows a second effect. Its AP is the highest of the three, yet at threshold 0.5 it finds 0%, because the loss leaves all scores low (the mean score of a pedestrian crop is 0.27); its threshold has to come from the precision–recall curve, never from habit. With only 90 pedestrians in the validation set, the difference between the two corrected models is within noise. The fix that would make this detector useful is not in the loss at all: it is more pedestrian crops.

The tools against imbalance follow from the two problems. For training: re-sampling (oversample images that contain rare classes, or guarantee a share per batch), loss re-weighting (class weights, often inversely proportional to frequency or its square root), focal loss for the extreme foreground–background imbalance of dense detectors, and more data for the rare class through mining, copy-paste and synthetic data, which is the only one of these that adds information. For measurement: never report accuracy on imbalanced data; report per-class precision and recall at the operating point, per-class AP, and the confusion matrix, and choose the threshold for each class from its own precision–recall curve.

Pitfall

Re-weighting and re-sampling change the scores' meaning as probabilities. A model trained with the rare class weighted ten times will output higher scores for it than its true frequency justifies. That is harmless if the threshold is chosen on validation data, and harmful if downstream code treats the score as a calibrated probability, for example in a tracker or fusion module. Recalibrate after re-weighting (Section 8.2).

7Metrics: precision, recall, F1, mAP and confusion matrices

A metric is a decision about what matters, written as a formula. Detection metrics in particular hide several such decisions: when a predicted box counts as finding an object, which threshold the score is compared against, how errors on different classes and object sizes are weighted. Knowing how the numbers are computed is what lets you tell a real improvement from an artefact of the evaluation, and translate between the mAP an ML engineer reports and the miss rate a function owner needs.

7.1From boxes to true and false positives

Before any metric can be computed, each predicted box must be declared right or wrong. The standard procedure, per image and per class, sorts the detections by score and walks down the list. Each detection is compared with the ground-truth boxes by IoU (Deep learning 5.3); it is a true positive if it overlaps a ground-truth box above the IoU threshold that has not yet been matched by a higher-scoring detection, and a false positive otherwise, including a second detection of an already matched object. Ground-truth boxes left unmatched at the end are false negatives. Boxes in ignore regions, or marked as too small or too occluded, are removed from both sides before counting.

Every term in that procedure is a setting that changes the numbers. An IoU threshold of 0.5 accepts loose boxes; 0.75 demands tight ones, and averaging over 0.5 to 0.95 (the COCO convention) rewards precise localisation. Matching greedily by score is standard, but some evaluations use optimal (Hungarian) matching. The ignore rules can easily move a recall by several points. When two teams report different numbers for the same model, the evaluation code is the first suspect.

7.2Precision, recall, F1 and the operating point

With TP, FP and FN counted at one score threshold, precision \(P = TP/(TP+FP)\) is the fraction of reported pedestrians that are real, and recall \(R = TP/(TP+FN)\) is the fraction of real pedestrians that were reported. For an AEB function a false positive can cause phantom braking and a false negative a missed brake; both matter, but not equally, and the function decides which trade-off is acceptable. F1, the harmonic mean \(2PR/(P+R)\), is a common single number when both errors are equally bad, which is rarely the case in a vehicle.

The score threshold moves the trade-off. Lowering it reports more boxes: recall rises, and precision usually falls because more of the additional boxes are false. A detector does not have "a recall"; it has a curve of recall against precision, one point per threshold, and the vehicle uses exactly one point on it, the operating point, chosen by the function owner from the curve. Move the threshold in the figure to see how the counts, the metrics and the point on the curve change together.

Two things are worth noticing. With well-separated scores, nearly any threshold between the two humps gives high precision and recall, and the choice hardly matters. With overlapping scores, as for night pedestrians, every threshold trades misses against false alarms, and because background candidates outnumber pedestrians ten to one, even a small fraction of background above the threshold destroys precision. That asymmetry is why detection metrics are built on precision and recall, not on accuracy or false positive rate: a false positive rate of 1% on 2 000 background candidates is 20 false pedestrians, as many as a tenth of all real ones.

7.4mAP variants and pedestrian-specific metrics

mAP is the mean of AP over classes, and in the COCO convention also over ten IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95; COCO additionally reports AP for small, medium and large objects. mAP@0.5 (the Pascal VOC convention) gives much higher numbers than mAP@[.5:.95] for the same model, so a number without its convention is meaningless (Deep learning 5.6). mAP is excellent for comparing models during development because it is threshold-free and stable. It is a poor release criterion, because the vehicle runs at one threshold and cares about specific classes, distances and regions, not about the average.

Pedestrian detection has its own tradition, from the Caltech and CityPersons benchmarks: the miss rate \(1 - \text{recall}\) plotted against false positives per image (FPPI) on log axes, summarised as the log-average miss rate (LAMR, written MR\(^{-2}\)): the miss rate averaged at nine FPPI values evenly spaced in log space between \(10^{-2}\) and \(10^0\). Lower is better. The metric is built around what a driver-assistance function cares about: how many pedestrians are missed at a tolerable false-alarm rate. Benchmarks also report it on subsets: reasonable (pedestrians taller than 50 pixels, less than 35% occluded), heavy occlusion, small.

A function owner goes one step further and asks for metrics in vehicle units: recall per distance band in the ego corridor, time from a pedestrian's first visibility to its first stable detection, false positives per hour of driving that persist long enough to be tracked, and position and velocity errors. Turning per-image detection metrics into those requires the tracker and the function logic, which is why the release evaluation of a perception model usually runs the whole perception stack in re-simulation over recorded drives, not just the network over images.

Talk track

"mAP is our development metric: threshold-free and stable, good for comparing training runs. For release we evaluate at the operating threshold: recall per distance band and slice, false positives per hour, and time to first detection, on the frozen test set and the regression suite. A model that gains mAP but loses night recall at 40 m is a regression for the function."

7.5Confusion matrices for detectors

A classifier's confusion matrix counts, for each true class (rows) and predicted class (columns), how many examples fell into each cell; the diagonal holds the correct ones. It answers questions that one number cannot: does the model confuse cyclists with pedestrians, or does it miss them altogether? For detectors the matrix gets an extra row and column for background: a ground-truth object that no detection matched is a miss (true class, predicted background), and a detection that matched no object is a false alarm (true background, predicted class). Matching is done by IoU regardless of class, so that a pedestrian detected as a cyclist appears as a confusion, not as one miss plus one false alarm.

Read the matrix in the example. Of 1 000 pedestrians, 912 were detected as pedestrians, 23 as cyclists and 65 were missed: the pedestrian problem is mostly misses, not confusion. Of 400 cyclists, 41 were called pedestrians, which for a braking function may be harmless (both trigger a reaction) or not (different motion models in the tracker). And 57 false pedestrians appeared on background, which, depending on how many frames were evaluated, may or may not meet a false-alarm budget. Each cell points to a different fix: misses to recall work (data, resolution, thresholds), confusion to labelling rules and class balance, false alarms to hard negatives.

7.6How much can a test set tell you?

Every metric measured on a finite test set is an estimate with an error bar, and comparisons between models are only meaningful if the difference exceeds that error. For a recall measured on \(n\) objects, the binomial standard error is \(\sqrt{R(1-R)/n}\): 0.028 at \(R = 0.80\) and \(n = 200\), 0.009 at \(n = 2\,000\). Frames from the same drive are correlated, so the effective \(n\) is smaller than the object count, and a bootstrap over drives (resample whole drives with replacement, recompute the metric, take the spread) gives more honest intervals than the binomial formula. For comparing two models on the same test set, a paired comparison (the objects that one finds and the other misses) is far more sensitive than comparing two independent intervals.

Safety-related claims need upper bounds, not point estimates. With no misses in \(n\) relevant pedestrians, the 95% upper bound on the miss rate is about \(3/n\) (the rule of three); with \(k\) misses, the Clopper–Pearson bound applies: 5 misses in 500 is a measured recall of 0.990 but a 95% lower bound of 0.979; 2 misses in 100 is 0.980 measured, 0.938 bounded. These numbers decide how large the test set of each critical slice must be, and they are the bridge from ML evaluation to the statistical arguments of SOTIF and ISO/PAS 8800 (Standards 4, Functional safety 7.6).

Pitfall

A test set of 5 000 images can contain only 40 pedestrians at night beyond 30 m. A recall of 0.85 on that slice has an error bar of ±0.11 at 95%, so the next model's 0.80 or 0.90 tells you almost nothing. Before arguing about a slice's number, count the objects in it.

8Calibration and uncertainty

A detector's score is used as if it were a probability: a tracker weights detections by it, a fusion module combines it with radar evidence, and a function decides whether to brake on it. A score is only a probability if the model is calibrated: among all detections with score 0.8, about 80% should be real. Modern networks are often not, and the reasons and remedies are a frequent topic and a practical problem for anyone who consumes network outputs downstream.

8.1What calibration means, and how to see it

A model is calibrated if its confidence matches its accuracy: \(P(\text{correct} \mid \text{confidence} = c) = c\) for every \(c\). The reliability diagram checks this directly. Predictions are grouped into bins by confidence (0.5–0.6, 0.6–0.7, …), and for each bin the mean confidence is plotted against the fraction that was actually correct. A calibrated model lies on the diagonal; points below the diagonal mean overconfidence (the model claims more than it delivers), points above mean underconfidence.

The expected calibration error (ECE) summarises the diagram in one number: the gap between confidence and accuracy in each bin, weighted by the fraction of predictions in the bin. A worked example with three bins: 200 predictions with mean confidence 0.55 of which 62% are correct, 300 with confidence 0.75 and 71% correct, 500 with confidence 0.95 and 80% correct. Then \(\text{ECE} = 0.2 \times 0.07 + 0.3 \times 0.04 + 0.5 \times 0.15 = 0.014 + 0.012 + 0.075 = 0.101\): on average the model's confidence is off by ten percentage points, almost all of it from the overconfident top bin. ECE depends on the binning and ignores the direction of errors, so it is reported together with the diagram and with the negative log-likelihood, which also rewards calibration.

Why are networks overconfident? Cross-entropy keeps pushing logits apart even after a training example is classified correctly, because the loss approaches zero only as the probability approaches one. A network with enough capacity to fit the training set therefore learns large logits, and on new data the large logits persist where the accuracy does not. Overfitting makes it worse; weight decay, label smoothing, augmentation and early stopping reduce it. Imbalance handling (Section 6.4) shifts it in a known direction.

8.2Temperature scaling and other fixes

The simplest and most widely used fix is temperature scaling: divide all logits by one scalar \(T\) before the softmax, \(p = \mathrm{softmax}(z/T)\), and choose \(T\) on a held-out set by minimising the negative log-likelihood. \(T > 1\) softens overconfident predictions, \(T < 1\) sharpens underconfident ones. Because dividing by a positive constant does not change which logit is largest, temperature scaling changes neither the predicted class nor the ranking of scores, so accuracy, precision–recall curves and AP stay exactly the same; only the meaning of the score changes. That makes it a safe post-processing step: it cannot break the detector, only fix or spoil its probabilities.

The companion experiment uses the overfitted model of Section 6.2, the one whose validation loss rose while its accuracy did not. At \(T = 1\) its ECE on unseen crops is 0.126: its predictions carry a mean confidence of 90.8% while only 78.2% are correct, and in the top bin, which holds 70% of the crops, it claims 98% and delivers 88%. The temperature fitted on a separate half of the validation set is \(T = 2.42\), and with it the ECE on the other half falls to 0.025 and the negative log-likelihood from 0.598 to 0.437, while accuracy stays at 78.2%. The well-regularised healthy model was already close to calibrated (ECE 0.030), which is the more important lesson: good training practice produces usable probabilities, and temperature scaling repairs the rest.

Detectors complicate the picture. Their scores combine a classification probability with a localisation quality (objectness, centerness or an IoU prediction), so "confidence" has to be defined: calibrated with respect to being a true positive at IoU 0.5, or at 0.7? Calibration also varies by slice; a model calibrated on daytime data may be overconfident at night. Detection-specific variants of ECE bin by score and count true positives at a fixed IoU, and calibration is checked per class and per condition. Beyond a single temperature, Platt scaling fits a logistic regression on the scores, isotonic regression fits an arbitrary monotone mapping, and per-class or per-condition temperatures handle the slice differences when there is enough data to fit them.

Why it matters

A tracker that confirms a track after three detections above 0.5, and a fusion module that multiplies camera and radar likelihoods, both assume the score means what it says. A model update that leaves mAP unchanged but changes calibration can therefore change the behaviour of the vehicle. Treat calibration as part of the model's interface: measure it in every release, and recalibrate after every change that might shift it.

8.3Uncertainty: what the model knows that it does not know

Calibration is about the average reliability of scores. Uncertainty estimation asks a sharper question per input: how much should this particular prediction be trusted? Two kinds of uncertainty are distinguished. Aleatoric uncertainty is in the data itself: a pedestrian 80 m away covered by 12 pixels is ambiguous for any model, and more training data will not make it less so. It can be learned directly, for example by predicting a variance for each box coordinate and training with a Gaussian negative log-likelihood, so that the network reports larger position uncertainty for small or occluded objects. Epistemic uncertainty is the model's ignorance: inputs unlike anything in training, where the weights are not constrained by data. More data of that kind reduces it.

Epistemic uncertainty is harder to obtain, because a single network gives a single answer. Deep ensembles train several networks from different random initialisations and measure how much their predictions disagree; they are the most reliable practical method but multiply training and inference cost. Monte Carlo dropout keeps dropout active at inference and runs the network several times; cheaper to train, still several inference passes, and less reliable. Single-pass methods (evidential outputs, distance-aware heads, distances in feature space as in Section 9.3) avoid the repeated inference that embedded targets cannot afford. In production perception the per-object uncertainty that downstream modules use is most often aleatoric (box and depth variances) plus calibrated scores, while epistemic signals are used offline: to find data the model is unsure about (Section 14.4) and to flag inputs outside the training distribution.

Talk track

"Calibration means a score of 0.8 is right 80% of the time; we check it with reliability diagrams and ECE per class and condition. Networks trained with cross-entropy tend to be overconfident, and temperature scaling fixes most of it without changing the ranking, so mAP is untouched. For per-object uncertainty we predict aleatoric variances for boxes and depth; epistemic uncertainty from ensembles we mostly use offline, to mine data the model is unsure about."

9Leakage, domain shift and out-of-distribution inputs

The promise of a validation score is that the model will perform as well on new data. Three things break that promise. Leakage makes the validation score too optimistic from the start. Domain shift makes new data differ from the training data in ways the model was not prepared for. Out-of-distribution inputs are cases so unlike the training data that the model's output is meaningless, while it still produces a confident answer.

9.1Leakage: when the test set is not new

Leakage is any way in which information about the test data reaches the model or the decisions made about it. The most common form in automotive data is the frame-level split of Section 2.3. The companion experiment measures it: 24 drives of 60 frames each are split once by frame (random 75/25) and once by drive (18 drives for training, 6 for testing), and the same model is trained on each. Both models are then also tested on 3 000 crops from 60 completely new drives, standing in for the field.

The drive-split number, 76.9%, is lower but honest: it is within a few points of that model's field result (74.4%), and the remaining difference is the sampling noise of a test set from only six drives. Notice that the frame-split model is not worse in the field; it is even slightly better, because its training frames come from all 24 drives instead of 18. The leaky split did not damage the model, it destroyed the measurement, and with it every decision that would have been based on it.

Other forms of leakage are subtler. Temporal leakage: a model evaluated on data recorded before some of its training data, which matters when the world changes (new road markings, new vehicles). Shortcut features: an attribute that correlates with the label in the dataset but not in the world, such as one camera variant that recorded most night pedestrians, or a watermark on images from one source. Selection leakage: using the test set to choose hyperparameters, thresholds or checkpoints, even informally by looking at results many times. Preprocessing leakage: statistics such as normalisation means computed on the full dataset including test data, or de-duplication done after splitting. And label leakage: auto-labels produced by a model that was itself trained on the test drives.

In practice

Assign each drive a split once, by a hash of its identifier, and store the assignment with the dataset version so that new frames of an old drive inherit its split. Run a near-duplicate search (image embeddings) across splits before each release. Keep a hold-out set that nobody tunes on, and record how often it was looked at.

9.2Domain shift

A domain shift is a difference between the distribution the model was trained on and the one it meets. It is useful to name the kind of shift, because each has different detection and remedies. Covariate shift changes the inputs but not the relationship between input and label: a new camera with a different ISP, a new country with different-looking streets, winter after a summer data collection. Label (prior) shift changes the class frequencies: more cyclists in Amsterdam than in Stuttgart; the model's scores, which encode the training priors, become miscalibrated. Concept shift changes the relationship itself: a new class of e-scooter riders who look like pedestrians but move like cyclists, or a guideline change that redefines a class.

In the companion experiment the day-only model loses 47 accuracy points at night (Section 5.1). That is a covariate shift: pedestrians look the same, the light does not. The drop is visible only because the evaluation is sliced; averaged over a validation set that is 90% daytime, the same model would look only slightly worse. Remedies for covariate shift are data from the new domain (best), augmentation that imitates the shift (Section 5.1), fine-tuning on a small labelled set from the new domain (Section 5.3), and unsupervised domain adaptation, which aligns feature statistics between labelled source and unlabelled target data. The cheapest of these, re-estimating batch-norm statistics on unlabelled target data, sometimes recovers a surprising part of the loss.

Why it matters

Every new vehicle program is a domain shift: different camera, different mounting, often different markets. The cost of a program depends heavily on how much of the existing dataset transfers and how much has to be recorded and labelled again. Knowing which slices degrade, before the new data is collected, is what allows a realistic plan.

9.3Out-of-distribution detection

A network trained to tell pedestrians from background must answer one of the two for every input, including a deer, a person in a dinosaur costume, a camera covered by mud or a frame corrupted by a transmission error. Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection tries to flag such inputs so that the system can lower its trust in the output. The problem is hard because the network's confidence is exactly what fails: a ReLU network's logits grow linearly as an input moves away from the training data in many directions, so far-away inputs often receive higher confidence, not lower.

The common OOD scores are cheap to compute from a single forward pass. The maximum softmax probability (MSP) treats low confidence as a sign of OOD. The energy score uses \(-\log \sum_k e^{z_k}\) of the logits, which keeps the magnitude information that softmax throws away. Feature-space distances, such as the Mahalanobis distance of the penultimate-layer features to the training feature distribution, ask whether the input's internal representation looks like anything seen in training. Each is evaluated by how well it separates in-distribution from OOD inputs, usually as AUROC: 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect separation.

The companion experiment treats night crops as the OOD set for the day-trained model without augmentation. The model's mean confidence is 0.98 on day crops and 0.95 on night crops, although its accuracy at night is 52%. As OOD detectors, MSP reaches an AUROC of 0.50, the energy score 0.48 and the Mahalanobis distance on features 1.00. Softmax confidence and energy are useless here: the model is almost as confident on night crops, where it is no better than a coin, as by day. The feature-space distance separates the two almost perfectly, because the internal representation of a dark, noisy crop is far from anything seen in training even though the final logits are not. Train the same model with photometric augmentation and the picture changes: its night accuracy rises to 70%, its night confidence falls to an honest 0.82, and the softmax and energy scores now flag night crops with an AUROC of about 0.80, while the Mahalanobis score drops to 0.96 because night-like images are no longer foreign to the features.

OOD detection is useful but not a safety mechanism on its own: no score separates all unknown inputs from known ones, and a shift that the features do not see (because the network never needed to represent it) is invisible to feature-based scores. In practice it serves three purposes: monitoring the fleet for drift (Section 14.3), selecting data for labelling (Section 14.4), and providing one input among several to a plausibility check that also uses other sensors and image-quality monitors (blockage, blur, exposure).

9.4Rare scenarios, corner cases and robustness

Between the common case and the truly unknown lies the long tail: situations that are in the ODD and that the model should handle, but which occur so rarely that the training data holds few examples. A child running out between parked cars, a person carrying a large board, a pedestrian lying on the road, a person in a wheelchair at night, a pedestrian reflected in a shop window, a large poster of a person on a bus. Average metrics are blind to them, because they contribute almost nothing to the average.

The engineering answer is to make the tail explicit. A scenario catalogue lists the corner cases the function must handle, derived from accident statistics, test protocols such as Euro NCAP, field reports and brainstorming; each entry gets examples in a regression suite and a requirement. Targeted data fills the catalogue: mining recordings for similar scenes, staged recordings on test tracks, and synthetic data from simulation or neural reconstruction, which can produce dangerous scenes on demand with perfect labels but brings its own domain gap. Robustness testing applies systematic perturbations (noise, blur, rain streaks, lens dirt, compression, brightness) at increasing strength and measures how fast the metric degrades; benchmarks of the ImageNet-C type standardise this. Adversarial robustness, the sensitivity to small crafted perturbations, is a research topic and a security concern but usually not the dominant failure mode of a camera in traffic.

Talk track

"We split by drive and region and run near-duplicate checks, so our test numbers measure generalisation. We slice every evaluation, because domain shift shows up in slices long before it shows in the average. OOD scores like energy or feature distance help us monitor and mine data, but we do not rely on them for safety; the long tail is handled with a scenario catalogue, targeted data and regression suites."

10Systematic error analysis: from v0 to a releasable detector

When a model misses its KPI, the tempting reaction is to try things: a bigger backbone, a different loss, another hundred thousand random frames. Systematic error analysis replaces guessing with a short investigation: look at the failures, group them by cause, estimate what each group costs, and fix the most expensive one with the cheapest change that addresses it. It is the core activity of a perception team and the subject of many questions of the form "your model is weak at X, what do you do?".

10.1An error taxonomy for detectors

Every detection error falls into a small number of types, and the TIDE toolkit made a standard taxonomy out of them. A false positive can be a localisation error (the right object, but IoU below the threshold), a duplicate (a second box on an already detected object, usually an NMS issue), a classification error (a cyclist reported as a pedestrian), a both error (wrong class and poor box), or a background error (a box on nothing: a pole, a sign, a reflection). A false negative is a miss: no box at all above the threshold. TIDE's useful trick is to report each type as the mAP that would be gained if that type alone were fixed, which ranks the error types by their cost instead of by their count.

The types point to different fixes. Localisation errors point to the box regression loss, the assignment rule, the input resolution or inconsistent box labelling. Duplicates point to NMS settings or a weak quality score. Classification errors point to class definitions in the guideline and to class balance. Background errors point to missing hard negatives in training. Misses point to recall problems: too few examples of that appearance, too small objects for the resolution, or a threshold set too high.

10.2Slicing: finding where the model is weak

The second tool is slicing the evaluation by metadata: time of day, weather, distance band, object height in pixels, occlusion level, camera variant, country, road type. Metadata comes from the recording (time, GPS, vehicle), from the labels (occlusion, truncation) and from automatic tagging (a weather classifier, a brightness measure). A slice is interesting when its metric is low and it is large or safety-relevant enough to matter; a 30-point recall gap on twelve pedestrians is first of all a reason to collect more test data.

Beyond predefined slices, failures can be clustered automatically: embed the missed and false-positive crops with a pretrained image model, cluster the embeddings, and look at a sample from each cluster. Clusters such as "pedestrians in dark clothing at dusk", "people partly hidden by car bonnets" or "sign posts with round signs" appear that nobody had defined as a slice. Engineers then name the cluster, find more examples of it in unlabelled data by embedding similarity, and add it to the test set as a new slice.

In practice

Read the failures. A sample of 100 misses and 100 false positives, looked at by an engineer with a spreadsheet of tentative causes, finds more than any dashboard: label errors, ambiguous guideline cases, a camera with a dirty lens in one recording, a time-synchronisation problem between images and labels. Schedule it for every model version.

10.3Data, labels or model?

Once a weak slice is found, the decisive question is why the model fails there, because the answers lead to different work. If the slice is under-represented in training, more data of that kind is the fix, and the curve of slice metric against slice training examples (train on 25%, 50%, 100% of the slice) shows whether more data will still help. If the labels are inconsistent in that slice, the fix is the guideline and a relabelling pass; the symptom is that the model's "errors" on inspection are often right and the labels wrong, or that two annotators disagree on the same images. If the information is not in the input at the resolution used (a pedestrian of 10 pixels at night), no data will fix it, and the answer is higher input resolution, a better camera or a different sensor. Only if the slice is well represented, consistently labelled and visible in the input does the architecture or recipe become the main suspect.

Label errors deserve their own tool. A model trained with cross-validation predicts each training example without having seen it; examples where the model confidently disagrees with the label are label-error candidates. This idea, formalised as confident learning, typically finds a few percent of wrong or missing labels in real datasets, concentrated in exactly the hard cases. Fixing them helps training somewhat and evaluation a lot, because test-set label errors make good models look bad.

11Experiment tracking, reproducibility and versioning

A team that trains hundreds of models a month needs to answer, at any time, questions such as: what exactly was the released model trained on, why was this recipe chosen, and is the new run better than the old one or just luckier? Tracking, reproducibility and versioning are the bookkeeping that makes those answers possible. They are dull to set up and painful to miss, especially when an assessor or a field incident asks about a model released a year ago.

11.1What to record for every run

A run is reproducible only if everything that influenced it is recorded. That means the code version (commit hash, with uncommitted changes refused or stored as a patch), the full resolved configuration (every hyperparameter, including defaults, not just the ones changed on the command line), the dataset version including split and label versions, the random seeds, the environment (container image digest, framework and CUDA versions, GPU type and count), and the pretrained weights the run started from, by hash. During the run, the tracker logs the curves of Chapter 6 (each loss term, learning rate, gradient norm, throughput, GPU memory), periodic validation metrics per slice, and sample predictions. After the run, it stores the checkpoints, the evaluation report and the exported model as artefacts linked to the run.

Tools such as MLflow, Weights & Biases, Neptune or ClearML, or an in-house equivalent, do the logging and offer comparison views. The tool matters less than the discipline: launch scripts that refuse to start without a clean commit and a dataset version, configs stored as files rather than spread across command-line flags, and one place where every run of the team can be found.

11.2Reproducibility and determinism

Fixing seeds (Python, NumPy, PyTorch, and each data-loader worker) makes a run repeatable in principle. In the companion experiment on a CPU, two runs with the same seed and configuration give bit-identical results: validation accuracy 86.90% both times. On GPUs this is not automatic. Several CUDA kernels use atomic additions whose order varies between runs (for example the backward pass of some convolutions, scatter and index operations), cuDNN may choose different algorithms depending on timing, and mixed precision changes rounding. PyTorch's torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True) plus CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG forces deterministic implementations or raises an error where none exists, at some cost in speed; distributed training adds the order of all-reduce operations and the data sharding.

In practice teams aim for two levels. Bit-exact reproducibility is needed for debugging (reproduce a NaN at step 41 213) and for the released model's lineage, and is achieved with deterministic settings on the same hardware and software. Statistical reproducibility, getting the same quality within run-to-run noise when re-running a recipe, is what matters for decisions, and it is the reason to know how large that noise is.

11.3Seed noise and comparing runs honestly

Two runs that differ only in the random seed (initialisation, data order, augmentation draws) give different models. In the companion experiment, five seeds of the same healthy recipe reach validation accuracies between 86.6% and 88.0%, a standard deviation of 0.53%, and average precisions of 0.9578 ± 0.0031. A change to the recipe that improves accuracy by 0.3 points in a single run is therefore not evidence of anything: one seed of the old recipe could have produced the same number. For real detectors the seed noise in mAP is typically 0.1–0.5 points, larger on small slices.

The honest comparison runs both variants with several seeds (three to five), compares means against the spread, and uses the same seeds for both where possible, which pairs the comparison. On the evaluation side, the bootstrap over drives (Section 7.6) gives the measurement noise of each number. A useful rule of thumb in a team: no change is called an improvement until its gain exceeds twice the combined standard deviation of seeds and test-set sampling, on the slice that matters.

Pitfall

Running twenty variants with one seed each and keeping the best is selection on noise: the winner is partly the luckiest seed, and its advantage shrinks when re-run. Confirm the winner of any search with fresh seeds before it enters the recipe.

11.4Data and model versioning, lineage and the registry

Datasets are too large for Git, so they are versioned by manifest: an immutable list of frame identifiers with content hashes, label versions and split assignments, stored in Git or in a data-versioning tool (DVC, lakeFS, or a database of dataset versions), while the images themselves stay in object storage addressed by hash. A trained model is registered in a model registry with its lineage: the run that produced it, which points to code, config, dataset version and parent weights; the evaluation reports; and its stage (candidate, validated, released, retired). The deployment artefacts of Edge AI 10.5 (ONNX file, calibration set, compiled binary) hang off the same registry entry.

Lineage is what makes a model auditable. For a released perception model, ISO/PAS 8800 and the safety case expect the team to show which data the model was trained and verified on, how that data was selected and labelled, and how the evaluation supports the claims. With a registry and versioned datasets, that is a query; without them, it is an archaeology project.

Talk track

"Every run records commit, full config, dataset version, seeds, container digest and parent weights, and every released model has its lineage in the registry, so we can rebuild it and explain it. We know our seed noise, so we compare recipes over several seeds and only accept gains above the noise on the slice that matters."

12Mixed-precision and distributed training

A detector that trains in two days on eight GPUs would take two weeks on one, and a large backbone or a multi-camera BEV network does not fit into the memory of a single GPU at all. Mixed precision makes each GPU faster and lets more fit into its memory; data parallelism spreads the batch across GPUs; sharding spreads the model's state across them. These techniques are now standard knowledge, and their mechanics explain most of the strange behaviours of large training jobs.

12.1Where the memory and the time go

Training memory has four parts. The weights; their gradients, the same size; the optimizer state, which for Adam is two more values per weight (the first and second moments); and the activations saved during the forward pass for use in the backward pass. With mixed precision and AdamW the per-parameter bill is usually 16 bytes: 2 for the FP16 or BF16 weights, 2 for their gradients, 4 for an FP32 master copy of the weights, and 4 + 4 for the two Adam moments. A 7-million-parameter detector therefore needs only about 0.11 GB for its state, a 300-million-parameter ViT-L about 4.9 GB, and a 7-billion-parameter model 112 GB, more than any single GPU holds.

For vision networks the activations usually dominate. Every layer's output that the backward pass needs is kept in memory until it is used, and their total grows with batch size and image resolution. A small measurement in the companion code makes the point: an 8-layer stack of 32-channel convolutions with BN and ReLU has 0.3 MB of parameters, but a forward pass on 16 images of 64 × 64 pixels saves 202 MB of tensors for the backward pass (72 tensors), 24 times the size of its input. Under torch.no_grad() it saves nothing, which is why inference needs a fraction of the memory of training.

Two techniques trade compute for activation memory. Activation checkpointing (gradient checkpointing) keeps only some activations, for example at block boundaries, and recomputes the rest during the backward pass: roughly a third more compute for a large memory reduction. And the batch can be split into micro-batches whose gradients are accumulated (Section 12.3).

12.2Mixed precision: FP16, BF16 and loss scaling

Modern GPUs run matrix multiplications several times faster in 16-bit formats than in FP32, and 16-bit tensors halve memory traffic. Automatic mixed precision (AMP) uses 16 bits where it is safe and 32 bits where it is not. In PyTorch, torch.autocast runs each operation in a precision chosen by an operation list: matrix multiplications and convolutions in 16 bits; reductions, softmax-with-loss, normalisation statistics and other numerically sensitive operations in FP32. The weights themselves stay in FP32 (the master copy), so that tiny updates are not lost to rounding. The companion run under CPU autocast with BF16 shows it: the linear layer's output is bfloat16, the cross-entropy loss is float32, and the layer's weight remains float32.

The two 16-bit formats differ in where they put their bits (Mathematics 11). FP16 has 10 mantissa bits and 5 exponent bits: good precision (machine epsilon \(9.8 \times 10^{-4}\)) but a small range, with a largest value of 65 504 and normal numbers only down to \(6.1 \times 10^{-5}\). Gradients are often smaller than that. Converting gradients of \(10^{-3}, 10^{-5}, 10^{-7}, 10^{-8}\) to FP16 keeps the first two, keeps \(10^{-7}\) only as a subnormal rounded to \(1.19 \times 10^{-7}\), and flushes \(10^{-8}\) to zero. Loss scaling fixes this: multiply the loss by a large factor \(S\) before backward(), so all gradients are \(S\) times larger and representable, then divide by \(S\) in FP32 before the update. With \(S = 65\,536\) all four values survive and are recovered exactly. Dynamic loss scaling (PyTorch's GradScaler) starts with a large \(S\), halves it and skips the step whenever a gradient overflows to infinity, and doubles it again after a run of good steps.

BF16 has the 8 exponent bits of FP32 and only 7 mantissa bits: the full FP32 range (up to \(3.4 \times 10^{38}\)) with coarse precision (epsilon \(7.8 \times 10^{-3}\)). It needs no loss scaling, because gradients do not underflow, but small increments vanish: \(1 + 10^{-3}\) is exactly 1 in BF16, while FP16 represents it as 1.00098. That is why master weights and optimizer states stay in FP32 even with BF16. On hardware that supports it (NVIDIA Ampere and later, TPUs, recent CPUs), BF16 is the simpler and more robust choice; FP16 with loss scaling remains for older GPUs. FP8 training, with per-tensor scaling, is used for the largest models on the newest GPUs.

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")          # only needed for FP16
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        loss = criterion(model(x), y)            # matmuls in FP16, loss in FP32
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()                # gradients of S * loss
    scaler.unscale_(opt)                         # back to true scale, in FP32
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 10.0)
    scaler.step(opt)                             # skipped if any grad is inf/NaN
    scaler.update()                              # adapt S
Pitfall

Clip gradients only after unscaling, otherwise the threshold applies to \(S\) times the gradient and clipping either always or never triggers. Custom losses with exponentials or divisions should be computed in FP32 (outside autocast or with explicit casts); an exp that overflows FP16 at 11.1 is a classic source of NaNs.

12.3Gradient accumulation

If the desired batch does not fit into memory, it can be processed as several micro-batches: run forward and backward on each, let the gradients add up in .grad, and step the optimizer once at the end. Dividing each micro-batch loss by the number of micro-batches makes the accumulated gradient equal to the gradient of the mean loss over the full batch. The companion check confirms it numerically: four micro-batches of 4 give a gradient that differs from one batch of 16 by \(1.2 \times 10^{-7}\) relative, which is float rounding.

The equivalence breaks with batch normalisation. Each micro-batch is normalised with its own statistics, so the forward pass, and therefore the gradient, differs from that of the full batch: with one BN layer in the same check, the relative difference is \(3.5 \times 10^{-2}\), five orders of magnitude larger. Accumulation gives the large batch's optimisation behaviour but not its BN statistics; for BN networks with tiny micro-batches, SyncBN across GPUs or GroupNorm is the answer. Accumulation also changes the number of optimizer steps per epoch, so the schedule must count optimizer steps, not micro-batches.

12.4Data parallelism: DDP and the all-reduce

Distributed data parallel (DDP) is the workhorse of multi-GPU training. One process runs per GPU, each holds a full copy of the model, and each processes a different slice of the global batch (a DistributedSampler gives each rank a disjoint shard of the dataset). After the backward pass, every gradient is averaged across all processes with an all-reduce, so that every process applies the identical update and the copies stay identical. Mathematically, the result is the same as training on one giant GPU with the global batch.

The companion experiment runs DDP with two processes on a CPU using the gloo backend, each process with 8 of 16 samples. Before synchronisation, each rank's local gradient differs from the full-batch gradient by up to 0.20; after DDP's all-reduce, both ranks hold identical gradients that match the single-process full-batch gradient to \(5.6 \times 10^{-8}\). On GPUs the backend is NCCL, which uses NVLink inside a node and InfiniBand or RoCE between nodes.

The all-reduce is usually implemented as a ring. With \(N\) GPUs, each gradient buffer is cut into \(N\) chunks. In the reduce-scatter phase, \(N-1\) steps pass chunks around the ring, each GPU adding its own contribution, until every GPU holds the complete sum of one chunk. In the all-gather phase, another \(N-1\) steps pass the completed chunks around until everyone has all of them. Each GPU sends \(2(N-1)/N\) times the gradient size in total, almost independent of \(N\), which is why the ring scales well. Step through it below.

Each of the four GPUs has computed the gradient of its own quarter of the batch. All four buffers have the same shape and different values. The goal is that every GPU ends with the sum (or mean) of all four, without sending every buffer to every GPU.

Each GPU sends one chunk to its right neighbour: GPU 0 sends chunk a, GPU 1 chunk b, and so on. The receiver adds the incoming chunk to its own copy of that chunk. After this step each chunk has been summed over two GPUs somewhere in the ring. All four links are busy at once, each carrying a quarter of the buffer.

Each GPU forwards the partial sum it just produced to the right, and the receiver adds its own contribution again. Now partial sums over three GPUs exist.

After \(N - 1 = 3\) steps, each GPU holds the complete sum over all four GPUs for exactly one chunk (highlighted). This intermediate state is exactly what FSDP needs for its sharded update (Section 12.5): each GPU owns the summed gradient of its shard.

Three more steps pass the completed chunks around the ring, this time overwriting instead of adding. At the end every GPU has the complete summed gradient. Each GPU sent \(2 \times 3/4\) of a buffer in total: for 8 GPUs and a 7-million-parameter detector in BF16, 25 MB per step, about 1 ms at 25 GB/s; for a 7-billion-parameter model, 24.5 GB, which is why such models need fast interconnects and sharding.

DDP hides most of the communication behind computation. Gradients are grouped into buckets of about 25 MB, and as soon as the backward pass has produced all gradients of a bucket (the last layers finish first), its all-reduce starts while the backward pass continues with earlier layers. When the model is small relative to the batch compute, communication becomes almost free; when it is large or the network is slow, the step time grows and scaling efficiency drops.

Pitfall

Things that break silently in DDP: forgetting sampler.set_epoch(epoch), so every epoch sees the same shuffle; logging or evaluating on every rank and multiplying the results; saving checkpoints from every rank at once; computing metrics on one rank's shard only; and parameters that receive no gradient in some steps (unused branches), which make DDP hang waiting for an all-reduce unless find_unused_parameters is set.

12.5Sharding: FSDP and ZeRO, and the other parallelisms

DDP replicates everything: weights, gradients and optimizer states sit on every GPU. For models whose state does not fit, fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP in PyTorch, ZeRO stage 3 in DeepSpeed) gives each of \(N\) GPUs only \(1/N\) of every parameter, gradient and optimizer state. Before a layer's forward pass, its full weights are assembled with an all-gather and discarded right after use; the backward pass gathers them again, and the gradients are reduce-scattered so that each GPU receives only the summed gradient of its own shard, which it uses to update its shard of the weights and optimizer state. The 112 GB of state of a 7-billion-parameter model becomes 14 GB per GPU on eight GPUs. The price is about 1.5 times the communication of DDP and more complex checkpointing; ZeRO stages 1 and 2 are intermediate points that shard only the optimizer state, or the optimizer state and gradients.

When even one layer is too large, or the batch per GPU becomes too small, other dimensions are split. Tensor parallelism splits individual matrix multiplications across GPUs (each holds some columns or rows of a weight matrix), at the cost of communication inside every layer, so it is kept within a node with fast links. Pipeline parallelism puts different layers on different GPUs and streams micro-batches through them, with idle "bubbles" at the start and end of each batch. Large language models combine all three with data parallelism (GenAI 3). Vision perception networks of 10–300 million parameters are normally trained with DDP alone, or FSDP for the largest backbones, plus mixed precision and activation checkpointing.

Numbers

16 bytes of training state per parameter with AdamW and mixed precision; a ring all-reduce sends \(2(N-1)/N\) of the gradient size per GPU; NVLink 4 in an H100 node offers 900 GB/s per GPU, InfiniBand NDR 50 GB/s per port. DDP on 8 GPUs in one node commonly scales at 90–95% efficiency for CNN detectors; across nodes 80–90% is typical with a good network.

12.6The input pipeline

Fast GPUs expose slow data loading. Decoding JPEG or video, augmenting and collating batches on the CPU must keep pace with the GPU, or the GPU waits. PyTorch's DataLoader runs this work in num_workers separate processes, prefetches a few batches ahead, and with pin_memory=True places batches in page-locked memory so that the copy to the GPU can run asynchronously. When that is not enough, decoding and augmentation move to the GPU (NVIDIA DALI, torchvision's GPU transforms), data is stored in formats that read sequentially (WebDataset tar shards, TFRecord), and images are pre-resized to the training resolution once instead of at every epoch. The quick diagnosis: if GPU utilisation is well below 100% and the step time does not change when the model is made smaller, the bottleneck is the input pipeline.

Talk track

"Training memory is weights, gradients, optimizer state and activations; with AdamW in mixed precision that is 16 bytes per parameter plus activations, which dominate for vision. We train in BF16 where the hardware supports it, otherwise FP16 with dynamic loss scaling, keeping FP32 master weights. We scale with DDP: one process per GPU, gradients averaged by a ring all-reduce overlapped with the backward pass. If the state doesn't fit we shard it with FSDP, and if the batch doesn't fit we accumulate gradients, keeping in mind that BN sees the micro-batch."

13PyTorch internals: autograd, compile, export and custom ops

Most of the time PyTorch can be used without knowing how it works. The exceptions are exactly the situations that matter in a project: a backward pass that fails with a cryptic error, a memory blow-up, a torch.compile that does not speed anything up, an export that refuses a model, or a hand-written kernel that has to work with all of them. This chapter explains the machinery underneath, with real output from PyTorch 2.15.

13.1Tensors, views and the dispatcher

A PyTorch tensor is a view onto a block of memory (the storage) described by a data type, a shape, strides (how many elements to skip to move one step along each dimension) and an offset. Transposing, slicing or view-reshaping creates a new tensor over the same storage with different strides, without copying; that is why such tensors may be non-contiguous, and why some operations call .contiguous() to copy them into a dense layout first. Channels-last memory format (NHWC strides on an NCHW-shaped tensor) is the same idea used for speed: tensor cores and many accelerators prefer channels innermost.

Every operation call goes through the dispatcher, which chooses an implementation from a table indexed by dispatch keys: the device (CPU, CUDA), whether autograd must record the operation, whether autocast should change its precision, whether the call is being traced. Autograd, autocast, torch.compile's tracing and the fake tensors used to compute shapes without data are all implemented as layers in this dispatch stack. Custom operators (Section 13.5) plug into the same table, which is why they must register implementations for the keys they want to support.

13.2Autograd: the graph recorded during the forward pass

PyTorch builds the backward graph while the forward pass runs. Every operation on a tensor that requires gradients creates a node, a grad_fn object, that knows how to compute the gradient of its inputs from the gradient of its output, and that keeps references to the tensors it will need for that (its saved tensors). Parameters are the leaves; their nodes are AccumulateGrad, which adds incoming gradients into .grad. Calling loss.backward() walks this graph from the loss back to the leaves in reverse topological order, applying the chain rule node by node (Mathematics 7), and then frees the graph.

The companion code computes a one-neuron logistic loss, \(z = w \cdot x + b\), \(p = \sigma(z)\), \(L = -\log p\), with \(w = (0.5, -1)\), \(x = (2, 1)\), \(b = 0.1\), and prints the recorded graph by following grad_fn.next_functions from the loss:

Several everyday behaviours follow from this design. SigmoidBackward0 saves its output, because \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\) needs only that; other nodes save inputs. Those saved tensors are the activation memory of Section 12.1. Calling backward() a second time fails with "Trying to backward through the graph a second time", because the graph and its saved tensors were freed after the first call (retain_graph=True keeps them). Modifying a saved tensor in place breaks the computation, and autograd detects it with a version counter on every tensor: in the companion code, an in-place mul_ on a sigmoid output produces "one of the variables needed for gradient computation has been modified by an inplace operation: … is at version 1; expected version 0 instead". torch.no_grad() and torch.inference_mode() switch recording off, so no graph and no saved tensors are created; .detach() cuts a tensor out of the graph, which is how targets and EMA weights are kept out of the gradient.

Interesting detail

Because the graph is rebuilt on every forward pass, Python control flow is free: an if or a loop that depends on data simply records a different graph each time. This "define-by-run" design is why PyTorch won researchers over from graph-first frameworks, and it is also exactly what makes compiling and exporting PyTorch programs hard.

13.3torch.compile: Dynamo, AOTAutograd and Inductor

Eager PyTorch launches one kernel per operation and reads and writes every intermediate tensor from memory. torch.compile captures sequences of operations into graphs and compiles them into fewer, fused kernels. It has three stages. TorchDynamo hooks into CPython's frame evaluation, reads the Python bytecode of the function as it is called, and extracts the tensor operations into an FX graph, while leaving all other Python to run normally. It records guards, conditions on the inputs (shapes, dtypes, values of Python flags) under which the captured graph is valid, and recompiles when a guard fails. AOTAutograd traces the backward pass ahead of time from the forward graph, so that forward and backward can both be optimised. TorchInductor lowers the graphs to generated code: Triton kernels on GPUs, C++/OpenMP on CPUs, with element-wise and reduction operations fused into their neighbours.

When Dynamo meets something it cannot put into a graph, it inserts a graph break: the graph so far is compiled, the offending Python runs in the interpreter, and capture resumes after it. The companion function contains a data-dependent if y.sum() > 0 and a print; torch._dynamo.explain reports 3 graphs and 2 graph breaks for it. Each break costs a return to Python, prevents fusion across it, and in a training loop can cost more than compiling saves. TORCH_LOGS="graph_breaks" lists them, and the fixes are to move side effects out of the compiled region and to replace data-dependent Python branches with tensor operations such as torch.where.

What does compiling buy? On the companion CPU, a small MLP dominated by matrix multiplications runs in 2.08 ms eager and 2.37 ms compiled, so compiling bought nothing at all: the matrix multiplications were already calls into an optimised library, and there was little to fuse. A chain of eleven element-wise operations on 4 million floats is a different matter: 24.55 ms eager against 3.31 ms compiled, 7.4 times faster, because Inductor fuses the chain into one loop that reads the input once and writes the output once. The first call of the MLP, with an empty compilation cache, took 14 seconds. That pattern carries over to GPUs: compilation pays off for models with many small or element-wise operations (normalisations, activations, attention variants, custom losses) and in long training runs that amortise the compile time, and much less for models that are already a few large convolutions.

13.4torch.export: one complete graph for deployment

For deployment a partial graph with Python fallbacks is useless; the target has no Python interpreter. torch.export.export uses the same Dynamo capture but in strict mode: it must produce a single graph of ATen operations covering the whole model, or fail with an error that names the construct it cannot handle. The result, an ExportedProgram, contains the graph, the parameters and buffers as explicit inputs, and the conditions under which it is valid. The companion export of a small conv–BN–ReLU block shows the form:

def forward(self, p_c_weight, p_c_bias, p_bn_weight, p_bn_bias,
            b_bn_running_mean, b_bn_running_var, b_bn_num_batches_tracked, x):
    conv2d = torch.ops.aten.conv2d.default(x, p_c_weight, p_c_bias, [1, 1], [1, 1])
    batch_norm = torch.ops.aten.batch_norm.default(conv2d, p_bn_weight, p_bn_bias,
                     b_bn_running_mean, b_bn_running_var, False, 0.1, 1e-05, False)
    relu = torch.ops.aten.relu.default(batch_norm)
    mean = torch.ops.aten.mean.dim(relu, [2, 3])
    return (mean,)

Shapes are fixed by default; a dimension declared dynamic with torch.export.Dim("batch", min=1, max=16) becomes a symbol with a range constraint (here VR[1, 16]), and calling the exported module with a batch of 32 fails the guard x.size()[0] <= 16. Data-dependent control flow must be expressed with torch.cond or removed. The exported program is the input to the ONNX exporter in its current form (torch.onnx.export(..., dynamo=True)), to ExecuTorch for on-device inference, and to AOTInductor for compiled C++ deployment; the deployment side is covered in Edge AI 2.2.

Pitfall

Export in evaluation mode. The exported batch-norm call above has training=False (the first False) because the model was in eval(); exported in training mode, it would normalise with batch statistics on the target. The same applies to dropout. Check the exported graph, not just that the export succeeded.

13.5Custom operators

Sometimes a model needs an operation PyTorch does not have, or a faster kernel for one it has: a fused box decoder, a deformable attention sampler, a special NMS. Writing it as an opaque Python function or a raw CUDA extension breaks compile and export, because the tracers cannot see inside it and do not know its output shape. The torch.library API registers a proper operator instead. @torch.library.custom_op("ns::name", mutates_args=()) declares the operator with a typed schema and its real implementation; register_fake gives a function that computes only the output's shape and dtype (used by Dynamo, export and FSDP, which trace with "fake" tensors that have no data); register_autograd supplies the backward formula. torch.library.opcheck tests that the pieces are consistent.

@torch.library.custom_op("guide::box_decode", mutates_args=())
def box_decode(offsets: torch.Tensor, stride: float) -> torch.Tensor:
    ...                                   # real kernel (C++/CUDA/Triton) or reference code

@box_decode.register_fake
def _(offsets, stride):
    return torch.empty_like(offsets)      # shape/dtype only, no computation

box_decode.register_autograd(backward_fn, setup_context=setup_fn)

In the companion code, opcheck reports success for the schema, the fake implementation, the autograd registration and dynamic-shape tracing; torch.export keeps the operator as a single node guide.box_decode.default next to aten.relu; and torch.compile with a backward pass works through it. For deployment the same operator name must then be implemented on the target side, as a custom ONNX operator or a vendor plugin (a TensorRT plugin, a TIDL custom layer), which is why teams think twice before adding one.

Talk track

"Autograd records a graph of backward nodes during the forward pass and keeps the tensors they need; that's where training memory goes and why in-place changes can break backward. torch.compile captures Python into FX graphs with Dynamo, traces the backward with AOTAutograd and generates fused kernels with Inductor; graph breaks are the main thing to watch. torch.export is the strict version: one full graph with explicit shape constraints, which is what ONNX and on-device runtimes consume. A custom kernel becomes a torch.library op with a fake implementation and an autograd formula, so compile and export can see through it."

14Production ML: from release to the fleet and back

Releasing a perception model is not the end of its development. The fleet meets situations the test set did not contain, the world changes, cameras age, and every incident or customer complaint becomes a question about the model. A production ML setup closes the loop: it watches the deployed model, brings the interesting data back, labels it efficiently, and ships improved versions safely. In automotive this loop runs under constraints that web companies do not have: limited bandwidth from the vehicle, privacy law, safety approval of every change and over-the-air updates regulated by UN R156.

14.1The loop after start of production

The data engine of Deep learning 15.1 becomes concrete after release. Vehicles run the released model and, in parallel, triggers: small rules that decide which moments are worth sending home, such as a low-confidence detection, a disagreement between camera and radar, a driver braking hard without a detected object, a track that appears and vanishes, or an embedding far from the training distribution. Selected snippets are uploaded, auto-labelled by large offline models, checked by humans where it matters, added to a new dataset version, and used to train a candidate model. The candidate is evaluated offline, then run in the fleet in shadow mode, and finally rolled out in stages.

14.2Shadow mode, A/B tests and staged rollout

In shadow mode a new model runs in the vehicle on the live camera stream next to the released one, but its outputs drive nothing. They are compared with the released model's outputs and with other evidence (radar, driver behaviour, the scene a few seconds later), and disagreements are logged and uploaded. Shadow mode measures the candidate on the real distribution of the fleet, including situations that no test set contains, without any risk. It needs spare compute on the target, which is one reason for keeping headroom in the SoC budget.

Web-style A/B testing, in which a fraction of users gets the new version and outcome metrics are compared, is used in automotive mostly for comfort functions and infotainment; for safety functions the new version must be validated before it acts, so the comparison happens in shadow mode and on test fleets. The staged rollout remains: a new model goes first to internal test vehicles, then to a small percentage of the customer fleet, then wider, with monitoring between stages and a defined rollback. Every software update with safety relevance follows the manufacturer's software update management system under UN R156 and is assessed for its impact on type approval.

14.3Drift monitoring

Ground truth is not available in the vehicle, so monitoring watches what can be measured without labels. Input drift: statistics of the images (brightness, contrast, sharpness, colour), of the camera (exposure times, gain), and of the scene metadata (country, weather, time of day) compared with the training distribution. Output drift: the rate of detections per kilometre per class, the distribution of scores, the fraction of low-confidence detections, track lengths. Feature drift: distances of internal embeddings to the training distribution, as in Section 9.3. A change in any of them does not prove the model got worse, but it says where to look and which data to bring home.

A common drift score for a single variable is the population stability index (PSI): bin the reference distribution into deciles, measure the fraction \(c_i\) of current data and \(r_i\) of reference data in each bin, and sum \((c_i - r_i)\ln(c_i/r_i)\). Rules of thumb read PSI below 0.1 as stable, 0.1–0.25 as a moderate shift, and above 0.25 as a major shift. In the companion data, the mean brightness of crops from new daytime drives against the daytime training data gives a PSI of 0.061; night drives give 8.28; and a stream with 10% night frames gives 0.17. The last number is the useful lesson: a model that suddenly sees a tenth of its input at night, where it is much weaker, produces a drift signal that is clearly visible on a simple input statistic, long before any labelled evaluation is available.

Pitfall

Drift monitors that alarm on every seasonal change are switched off within a month. Compare against references that match the season and region, alert on changes that persist and that correlate with a model-relevant signal (more low-confidence detections, more camera–radar disagreements), and route alerts to someone who owns the response.

14.4Active learning and auto-labelling

Labelling everything the fleet uploads is unaffordable, so the question becomes which frames to label. Active learning chooses them by expected value to the model. Uncertainty sampling picks frames where the model is unsure: scores near the threshold, high disagreement within an ensemble, or between the released model and a candidate. Diversity sampling picks frames that cover regions of the embedding space that the labelled data covers poorly, so that the selection is not a thousand near-copies of one confusing scene. Practical systems combine both, add the targeted triggers and the error clusters of Chapter 10, and keep a random sample alongside so that the test sets stay representative.

Auto-labelling makes labelling itself cheaper. Large offline models that would never fit in a car (big detectors, vision foundation models, multi-frame and multi-sensor models that can look into the future of a recording and use lidar) produce labels that are often better than a single human pass on easy objects. Humans then correct, verify samples, and label the cases where the auto-labeller is uncertain. The risks were noted in Section 2.2: inherited blind spots and confirmation bias. Teams measure auto-label quality on a manually labelled audit set per slice, and never use auto-labels from a model as test labels for the same model family.

14.5The KPI owner's view

For the engineering manager who owns a perception KPI, the chapter reduces to a few questions to ask of every model version. On which test set version, and is it frozen? What are the KPIs per slice at the operating threshold, with their confidence intervals, and did any slice regress beyond its noise? What changed relative to the previous version, data, labels or recipe, and which error analysis motivated it? Is the model calibrated, and did calibration change? Is the deployed binary's parity with the trained model shown on the target (Edge AI 10.1)? What did shadow mode show? And can the release be rebuilt from its lineage? A team that can answer these quickly is in control of its model; a team that answers with one mAP number is not yet.

Talk track

"After release we keep the loop running: triggers in the fleet upload interesting moments, offline models auto-label them with human checks, and active learning picks what is worth labelling. Candidates run in shadow mode against the released model before they act, and roll out in stages under our R156 update process. We monitor input, output and feature drift without labels, and every release answers the same questions: which test set, which slices, which change, calibrated, parity on target, rebuildable."

QSelf-check questions

Core questions with short model answers; the chapter sections hold the depth. The second group checks understanding with "why" and "what if" questions rather than definitions.

Q.1Core questions

How do you split a driving dataset into training, validation and test sets?Basic

By drive at minimum, preferably by region or recording day, never by frame, because neighbouring frames are near-duplicates and a frame-level split measures memory, not generalisation. Validation is for decisions, test for milestones only, and both are sized from the claims they must support per slice. Split assignments are stored with the dataset version so new frames of an old drive inherit their split.

What is the difference between SGD with momentum, Adam and AdamW?Basic

SGD steps against the gradient; momentum averages gradients over time so oscillations across a valley cancel and consistent directions accelerate. Adam additionally divides each coordinate by its running RMS gradient, giving every weight its own step size, which makes it robust to badly scaled layers. AdamW applies weight decay directly to the weights instead of through the gradient, where Adam's normalisation would distort it; it generalises better and is the standard for transformers.

Why use a learning-rate warm-up and a decaying schedule?Basic

At the start the weights are random, gradients are large and curvature high, so a full-size step can destroy the network or BN statistics; warm-up ramps the rate up over the first steps. A high rate in the middle explores and avoids sharp minima; decaying it at the end lets the weights settle, which is where the final loss drop happens. Cosine decay with linear warm-up is the common default.

How does batch normalisation behave differently in training and inference?Basic

In training it normalises each channel with the mean and variance of the current batch and updates running averages. In evaluation mode it uses the running averages, which makes it a fixed affine map that is folded into the preceding convolution for deployment. Forgetting eval(), tiny batches with noisy statistics, and fine-tuning that overwrites the running statistics are the classic problems.

Your training loss goes down but validation loss goes up. What do you do?Basic

That is overfitting. First check the validation data is not different in kind (preprocessing, domain). Then, in order of effectiveness: more and more diverse data, stronger augmentation, weight decay and dropout, a smaller or pretrained model, and early stopping on the validation metric. Also check whether accuracy actually degrades or only the loss, which would point to overconfidence and a calibration fix.

What sanity checks do you run before a long training job?Basic

Look at a grid of augmented training images with labels drawn; check the initial loss is near ln C; overfit a single small batch to near-zero loss with augmentation off; check every parameter receives a gradient; reproduce a known baseline. These catch data pipeline, label, loss and wiring bugs in minutes.

What causes vanishing and exploding gradients, and how are they handled?Intermediate

Backpropagation multiplies one Jacobian per layer, so gradients shrink or grow exponentially with depth when the typical factor is below or above one; saturating activations make vanishing worse. Proper initialisation (He for ReLU), normalisation layers and residual connections keep the factors near one; gradient clipping and warm-up protect against explosions; monitoring the gradient norm per step gives early warning.

How do you deal with class imbalance in detection?Intermediate

Separate training from measurement. For training: focal loss for the foreground–background imbalance, re-sampling or class weights for rare classes, and above all more rare-class data through mining, copy-paste or synthesis. For measurement: no accuracy; per-class precision and recall at the operating point, per-class AP, a confusion matrix, and per-class thresholds chosen on validation data. Recalibrate scores after re-weighting.

Explain precision, recall and how AP is computed.Basic

Precision is the fraction of detections that are real, recall the fraction of real objects detected, both at one score threshold after IoU matching. Sweeping the threshold gives the precision–recall curve. AP sorts detections by score, computes precision and recall at each, replaces precision by its maximum at equal or higher recall, and sums precision times recall step. mAP averages over classes and, in COCO, over IoU thresholds 0.5–0.95.

Why is mAP not enough to release a pedestrian detector?Intermediate

mAP averages over thresholds, classes, sizes and conditions, while the vehicle runs one threshold and depends on specific slices, such as pedestrians in the ego corridor at 30–40 m at night. A model can gain mAP on easy cases and lose there. Release criteria are recall and false positives per hour at the operating point, per slice and distance band, with confidence intervals, plus time to first detection measured through the tracker.

What is model calibration and how do you fix a miscalibrated model?Intermediate

A calibrated model's confidence equals its accuracy: of all predictions at 0.8, 80% are right. It is checked with a reliability diagram and ECE, per class and condition. Cross-entropy training tends to make networks overconfident. Temperature scaling divides logits by a scalar fitted on held-out data; it does not change the ranking, so AP is unchanged. Platt scaling or isotonic regression are alternatives.

What is data leakage? Give automotive examples.Basic

Information about the test data reaching training or model selection. Examples: frame-level splits putting neighbouring frames of one drive on both sides; tuning thresholds on the test set; normalisation statistics computed on all data; auto-labels from a model trained on the test drives; a shortcut such as one camera variant recording most night pedestrians. It makes reported numbers optimistic and is found by drive-level splits, near-duplicate searches and held-out sets nobody tunes on.

How would you detect that the fleet is seeing data the model was not trained for?Intermediate

Monitor without labels: input statistics (brightness, exposure, sharpness, scene metadata), output statistics (detections per km, score distributions, low-confidence rate, track stability) and feature-space distances or energy scores against the training distribution, each compared with a seasonal reference using a drift score such as PSI. Persistent changes trigger data upload for those conditions, labelling and a slice-specific evaluation.

Your detector is weak at night. Walk me through how you improve it.Intermediate

Measure first: night recall per distance band with error bars, and read a sample of misses and false positives. Classify causes: too few night examples, inconsistent labels at night, objects not visible at that resolution, or model limits. Cheap fixes first: camera-style photometric augmentation and checking preprocessing; then targeted night data via fleet triggers and mining, labelled under the same guideline; hard negatives if false positives are lamps or reflections. Compare against the frozen test set with several seeds and check that no other slice regressed.

How do you make training runs reproducible?Basic

Record the commit, full resolved config, dataset version with splits and label versions, seeds for every random source including data workers, the container digest and the parent weights; store checkpoints and reports linked to the run. For bit-exactness on GPUs enable deterministic algorithms and fix the hardware; for decisions, rely on statistical reproducibility across several seeds.

Explain mixed-precision training. Why does FP16 need loss scaling and BF16 not?Intermediate

Autocast runs matrix multiplications and convolutions in 16 bits and numerically sensitive operations in FP32, with an FP32 master copy of the weights. FP16 has a small exponent range: gradients below about \(6 \times 10^{-5}\) become subnormal and below about \(6 \times 10^{-8}\) zero, so the loss is multiplied by a scale before backward and the gradients divided afterwards, with dynamic adjustment on overflow. BF16 has FP32's exponent range, so gradients do not underflow, at the cost of only 7 mantissa bits.

How does DDP work?Intermediate

One process per GPU, each with a full model replica and a disjoint shard of the data. After backward, gradients are averaged across processes with an all-reduce, typically a ring implemented by NCCL, launched bucket by bucket while the backward pass continues, so every replica applies the identical update. The result equals large-batch training on one device; the learning rate is scaled with the global batch.

What is the difference between DDP and FSDP?Intermediate

DDP replicates weights, gradients and optimizer state on every GPU and only all-reduces gradients. FSDP (ZeRO-3) shards all three across GPUs, all-gathers each layer's weights just before it is used and frees them after, and reduce-scatters gradients so each GPU updates only its shard. FSDP fits models whose state exceeds one GPU at roughly 1.5 times DDP's communication.

What is gradient accumulation, and when is it not equivalent to a larger batch?Intermediate

Running several micro-batches through forward and backward, letting gradients sum, and stepping once, with each loss divided by the number of micro-batches. It reproduces the large-batch gradient exactly for networks without batch-dependent layers. With batch normalisation, each micro-batch is normalised with its own statistics, so the result differs; the schedule must also count optimizer steps, not micro-batches.

How does autograd work in PyTorch?Intermediate

During the forward pass each operation on tensors requiring gradients creates a grad_fn node that saves the tensors it needs and links to its inputs' nodes. backward() walks this graph from the loss in reverse topological order, applies each node's local derivative (vector–Jacobian product) and accumulates into the leaves' .grad, then frees the graph. Version counters detect in-place changes to saved tensors.

What does torch.compile do, and what is a graph break?Advanced

Dynamo intercepts Python bytecode and extracts tensor operations into FX graphs guarded by conditions on the inputs; AOTAutograd traces the backward; Inductor generates fused Triton or C++ kernels. A graph break happens where Dynamo cannot trace, such as data-dependent Python control flow, print or unsupported calls: the graph is split, Python runs in between, and fusion across the break is lost. Recompilation happens when guards fail, for example on new shapes.

What is the difference between torch.compile and torch.export?Advanced

Compile is for speed inside Python: it tolerates graph breaks and falls back to the interpreter. Export is for deployment: it must capture the whole model as one graph of ATen operations with parameters as inputs and explicit dynamic-shape constraints, and it errors where it cannot. The exported program feeds the ONNX exporter, ExecuTorch or AOTInductor.

How do you add a custom CUDA kernel so that it works with compile and export?Advanced

Register it with torch.library.custom_op with a typed schema, add a fake (meta) implementation that returns only output shapes and dtypes, register an autograd formula if it needs gradients, and test with torch.library.opcheck. Tracers then treat it as one opaque node. For deployment the same op needs an implementation on the target side, such as an ONNX custom op or a TensorRT plugin.

What is shadow mode and why is it used in automotive?Basic

The candidate model runs on the vehicle's live sensor data next to the released one, but its outputs control nothing. Disagreements are logged and uploaded. It measures the candidate on the true fleet distribution, including scenes no test set has, without risk, and supplies data for the next iteration. Safety functions cannot be A/B tested on customers, so shadow mode plus staged rollout under the OTA update process replaces it.

What is active learning?Basic

Choosing which unlabelled data to label by its expected value to the model: uncertain predictions (scores near the threshold, ensemble or model–candidate disagreement) and diverse samples that cover gaps in the embedding space, combined with targeted triggers and a random sample for unbiased test sets. It gets more improvement per labelling euro than labelling at random.

Q.2Why and what if

Why should the loss of a fresh two-class classifier be about 0.69, and what if it is 3.5?Basic

A well-initialised network outputs nearly equal probabilities, and \(-\ln 0.5 = 0.693\). A starting loss of 3.5 means the network assigns about 3% to the correct class on average: it starts confidently wrong, typically because the last layer's initial scale is too large, a bias is initialised badly, the loss receives probabilities instead of logits, or labels are encoded wrongly.

What if the training loss is stuck at ln 2 from the first epoch?Basic

The network outputs the prior and learns nothing. Likely causes: inputs that carry no signal (all zeros, wrong normalisation, a broken loader), labels not matching inputs, a learning rate so high that the first steps killed the ReLUs (the companion run at SGD 0.5 without BN did exactly that), or parameters not attached to the optimizer. Overfitting one batch distinguishes a pipeline bug from a recipe problem.

Why does SGD diverge on a quadratic with curvature 25 when the learning rate exceeds 0.08?Intermediate

Along that direction the update is \(w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot 25 w = (1 - 25\eta) w\). The iterate shrinks only if \(\lvert 1 - 25\eta \rvert < 1\), i.e. \(\eta < 2/25 = 0.08\). Above it each step overshoots by more than it corrects and the amplitude grows. In a network the largest Hessian eigenvalue plays the role of 25, which is why the stable learning rate is set by the sharpest direction.

Why does Adam with L2 regularisation not behave like weight decay?Advanced

With L2, the term \(\lambda w\) is added to the gradient and then divided by Adam's per-weight RMS gradient. Weights with large gradients are barely decayed, weights with small gradients strongly, so the regularisation depends on gradient history instead of weight size. AdamW subtracts \(\eta \lambda w\) directly, outside the adaptive scaling, which restores uniform decay.

What if validation accuracy is much higher than training accuracy?Basic

Usually fine and explained: training accuracy is measured with augmentation and dropout active, which makes training examples harder, and over the epoch while the model was still worse. If the gap is large, check that the validation set is not easier in kind, not overlapping with training data, and that the model is evaluated in eval mode.

Why can validation loss rise while validation accuracy stays the same or improves?Intermediate

Loss punishes confidence on wrong answers; accuracy only counts the argmax. An overfitting model becomes extremely confident on the few validation examples it gets wrong, which makes their loss huge, while most predictions stay correct. The model has become overconfident, so its probabilities are less trustworthy even if its decisions are not worse; temperature scaling repairs much of that.

Temperature scaling improved ECE. Why did AP not change at all?Intermediate

Dividing logits by a positive constant is a monotone transformation of the scores: it preserves their order, and AP, PR curves and accuracy depend only on the order and the argmax. Only the numeric score values, and therefore calibration and any threshold expressed as a probability, change.

What if you weight the rare class ten times higher and its recall at threshold 0.5 jumps, but AP barely moves?Intermediate

That is the usual result once the model already learns the class: re-weighting shifts the rare class's scores upwards, so more of them cross 0.5, trading precision for recall at that threshold. The ranking of pedestrians against background, which AP measures, changes little because no new information was added. The same recall could have been bought by lowering the threshold; real improvements come from more and better rare-class data. Re-weighting does raise AP when the unweighted model was starved of gradient for the rare class and learned almost nothing about it, as in the experiment of Section 6.4.

Why can a frame-level split report 94% when the model is really at 78%?Basic

Neighbouring frames show the same pedestrian and background with tiny changes, so with a random frame split nearly every test frame has a near-copy in training. The model is rewarded for remembering scenes rather than recognising pedestrians, and the test set stops measuring generalisation. In the companion experiment that inflated the result by 16 points.

Why might a softmax confidence be a poor OOD detector?Intermediate

ReLU networks extrapolate linearly: far from the training data the logits keep growing in many directions, so unusual inputs can receive higher confidence than normal ones. Softmax also discards the logits' overall magnitude. Energy scores keep the magnitude, and feature-space distances ask whether the internal representation resembles training data, which is usually more informative.

What if two runs with identical config and seed on GPUs give different results?Intermediate

Nondeterministic kernels: atomic additions in some backward passes, cuDNN algorithm choice by timing, varying reduction orders in distributed training, plus data-loader ordering if worker seeds are not fixed. Differences start at the last bits and grow through training. Enable deterministic algorithms and fix the environment for bit-exactness, and always judge changes against multi-seed noise.

Why is gradient accumulation with BN not the same as a larger batch, and why does the gradient still match without BN?Intermediate

Without batch-dependent layers each sample's loss depends only on itself, so the mean gradient over micro-batches equals the full-batch gradient (the companion check: \(10^{-7}\) relative). BN normalises each micro-batch with its own statistics, so the forward computation itself differs (\(3.5 \times 10^{-2}\) relative in the check) and no rescaling of the loss can fix that.

Why does the per-GPU traffic of a ring all-reduce hardly grow with the number of GPUs?Advanced

Each GPU sends \(2(N-1)\) chunks of size \(G/N\): \(2G(N-1)/N\), which approaches \(2G\) as \(N\) grows. Every link in the ring is busy in every step, so bandwidth is used fully. The latency term grows with \(N\) (more steps), which is why very large clusters use tree or hierarchical algorithms for small messages.

Why does torch.compile help a model with many element-wise operations much more than one dominated by convolutions?Intermediate

Large convolutions and matrix multiplications already run as single optimised library kernels; there is little left to gain. Element-wise chains in eager mode launch one kernel per operation and read and write the full tensor each time; Inductor fuses them into one kernel that reads the input once and writes the output once, so memory traffic and launch overhead drop by the length of the chain.

What if your exported model gives different results on the target than in PyTorch?Intermediate

First check it was exported in eval mode (BN with running statistics, dropout off) and that the target's preprocessing matches training exactly (range, channel order, resize method). Then climb the parity ladder from Edge AI 10.1: PyTorch vs exported program vs ONNX Runtime vs target, comparing tensors layer by layer to find the first divergence.

Why does fine-tuning with a large learning rate on a small dataset make things worse?Basic

Large steps move the pretrained weights far from the region where their general features live, towards a solution fitting the few new examples: overfitting plus catastrophic forgetting. A small learning rate keeps the update near the pretrained solution; freezing early layers or BN statistics, and mixing in old data, further limit the damage.

Why is drift in an input statistic useful even though it does not measure accuracy?Basic

Labels arrive weeks later, if at all, while input statistics are available immediately and for every vehicle. A drift tells you where the fleet's data departs from what the model was trained and tested on, which is where accuracy is most likely to differ, so it directs data upload, labelling and slice evaluation. It is an early warning, not a verdict.

Active learning
Selecting which unlabelled data to label by expected value to the model: uncertainty plus diversity.
All-reduce
Collective operation after which every process holds the sum (or mean) of all processes' tensors; usually a ring of reduce-scatter and all-gather.
AMP (automatic mixed precision)
Running operations in 16-bit or 32-bit precision by an operation list, with FP32 master weights.
Average precision (AP)
Area under the interpolated precision–recall curve of one class; mAP averages over classes and possibly IoU thresholds.
Batch normalisation
Per-channel normalisation with batch statistics in training and running averages at inference.
Calibration
Agreement between predicted confidence and observed accuracy.
Catastrophic forgetting
Loss of previously learned abilities when a network is trained further on new data only.
Gradient clipping
Rescaling the gradient when its global norm exceeds a threshold, to survive bad batches.
Confusion matrix
Counts of true class versus predicted class; for detectors with an extra background row and column.
Cross-entropy
Classification loss \(-\log p_y\); its gradient on the logits is \(p - \text{onehot}(y)\).
DDP
Distributed data parallel: one model replica per GPU, gradients averaged by all-reduce.
Domain shift
Difference between training and deployment distributions: covariate, prior or concept shift.
Dynamo
The bytecode-level graph capture of torch.compile and torch.export, with guards.
EMA weights
Exponential moving average of the weights during training, used for evaluation and release.
ECE
Expected calibration error: bin-weighted mean gap between confidence and accuracy.
Focal loss
Cross-entropy scaled by \((1-p_y)^\gamma\), down-weighting easy examples; standard for dense detectors.
FSDP / ZeRO
Data parallelism that shards parameters, gradients and optimizer state across GPUs.
Graph break
A point where torch.compile cannot trace and returns to Python, splitting the graph.
Labelling guideline
The annotators' specification of classes, box rules, attributes and ignore regions.
LAMR (MR\(^{-2}\))
Log-average miss rate over FPPI from \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^0\); the standard pedestrian-detection metric.
Leakage
Test information reaching training or model selection, making results optimistic.
Loss scaling
Multiplying the loss before backward so FP16 gradients do not underflow, then unscaling in FP32.
ODD
Operational design domain: the conditions in which a function is designed to work.
Out-of-distribution (OOD)
Inputs unlike the training data, for which the model's outputs are unreliable.
Operating point
The single score threshold (and resulting precision and recall) the product uses.
PSI
Population stability index, a binned divergence used as a drift score.
Learning-rate range test
A short run with exponentially increasing learning rate to find the usable peak rate.
Shadow mode
Running a candidate model on live data without letting its outputs act, to compare it with the released one.
Slice
A subset of data defined by metadata (night, far, occluded, camera) on which metrics are reported separately.
Temperature scaling
Dividing logits by a fitted scalar \(T\) to calibrate confidences without changing their order.
True / false positive, false negative
A detection matched to an unmatched object above the IoU threshold / a detection without such a match / an object without a detection.
Warm-up
Linear increase of the learning rate over the first steps of training.
Weight decay
Shrinking every weight towards zero each step; decoupled from the gradient in AdamW.
