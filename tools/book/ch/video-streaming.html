Cameras and imaging

Video compression and streaming for camera systems

Why a camera stream has to shrink by a factor of a hundred or more, how JPEG and the modern video codecs (H.264, H.265, AV1, VVC) do it, and how rate control, latency, hardware encoders, containers and network transport turn a codec into a working system. It closes with how quality is measured, what compression does to perception algorithms, and where video leaves a vehicle. Running example: a synthetic 1280 × 720, 30 fps forward camera on a city street, encoded for real with FFmpeg, x264, x265, SVT-AV1 and GStreamer, with every number measured in the chapter's experiments.

1The big picture: why video has to be compressed

A camera produces far more data than any network, disk or radio link can carry. Compression exists because the gap is not a factor of two but a factor of a hundred to a thousand, and closing it shapes everything else: quality, latency, power and the way the stream survives a bad network.

1.1Raw camera data rates do not fit anywhere

Start with arithmetic. A 1280 × 720 frame in YUV 4:2:0 with 8 bits per sample needs 1.5 bytes per pixel (one luma sample per pixel, plus one Cb and one Cr sample per 2 × 2 pixels). At 30 frames per second that is 332 Mbit/s, or about 149 GB per hour. A 1080p display stream is 0.75 Gbit/s. A modern 8-megapixel automotive front camera delivers RAW12 data at 30 fps: 3.0 Gbit/s, 1.3 TB per hour, and after the ISP has turned it into YUV 4:2:2 for the rest of the system it is closer to 4 Gbit/s. A development vehicle with eight such cameras and four 3-megapixel surround cameras produces about 28 Gbit/s, or 12.7 TB every hour of driving.

Now compare that with the pipes the data has to go through. A home broadband uplink carries perhaps 20–50 Mbit/s; a mobile uplink on a moving vehicle 2–20 Mbit/s on a good day; a 100BASE-T1 automotive Ethernet link 100 Mbit/s; even 1 Gbit/s Ethernet cannot carry a single raw 8 MP camera. Netflix delivers 1080p to homes at roughly 3–6 Mbit/s and a video call uses 1–2 Mbit/s for 720p. Between the camera and those numbers lies a factor of 100 to 1000. That factor is what a video codec delivers, and on the test sequence of this chapter H.264 at its default quality setting (CRF 23) carried the 332 Mbit/s stream in 1.7 Mbit/s, a ratio of about 190:1.

Inside the vehicle the camera-to-ECU path is usually not compressed at all: SerDes links such as GMSL and FPD-Link carry several Gbit/s per camera precisely so that the ISP and the neural networks see unspoiled pixels (Camera, GPU and display 2.2). Compression enters wherever video leaves that closed, short, high-bandwidth world: to a recorder, to a remote operator, to the cloud, to another ECU over Ethernet, or to the viewer of a streaming service.

Numbers

Measured in this chapter's experiments on 180 frames of 720p30: raw 332 Mbit/s; lossless FFV1 (an intra-only lossless video codec) 102 Mbit/s, only 3.3:1 because the sensor noise in the frames cannot be predicted; MJPEG at high quality 21 Mbit/s; H.264 at CRF 23 1.7 Mbit/s; H.265 at CRF 23 1.2 Mbit/s; AV1 at a matching quality 0.6 Mbit/s.

1.2What a codec exploits: four kinds of redundancy

A codec can shrink video by two orders of magnitude because natural video is extremely redundant, in four distinct ways. Spatial redundancy: neighbouring pixels are similar, so a block of sky or asphalt can be described by a few numbers about its average and slow variation instead of 256 independent values. Temporal redundancy: consecutive frames are almost the same picture, shifted a little by motion; most of frame n can be copied from frame n − 1 if you say where to copy from. Statistical redundancy: after prediction, the remaining numbers are mostly small and some values are far more common than others, so an entropy coder can give them short codes. Perceptual redundancy: the eye is less sensitive to fine colour detail than to fine brightness detail, and less sensitive to high-frequency texture errors than to errors in smooth areas, so information can be thrown away where nobody will notice.

The first three can be exploited without losing anything; that is lossless compression, and it rarely does better than 2:1 to 4:1 on camera images, because sensor noise is genuinely random and cannot be predicted. The 100:1 ratios come from the fourth: lossy compression decides, block by block, which details are worth their bits. The art of an encoder is making those decisions so that what is thrown away is what the viewer (or the algorithm) would not have used anyway.

1.3The trade-off triangle: bitrate, quality, latency (and power)

Every design decision in this chapter moves a point inside a triangle whose corners are low bitrate, high quality and low latency. You can have any two. A streaming service such as Netflix has minutes of buffer, so it buys quality per bit with slow, multi-pass encoding, long groups of pictures and look-ahead; its latency is measured in seconds and nobody minds. A video call needs latency below about 150–200 ms for a natural conversation, so it gives up the encoder tools that look into the future and accepts a higher bitrate for the same quality. A camera monitor system that replaces a truck's mirror cannot afford even that, so it usually avoids compression entirely and pays with a dedicated multi-gigabit link.

A fourth corner sits under the triangle: computational cost, which on an embedded system means power and silicon area. On the test sequence x264 at its medium preset encoded 720p at about 54 frames per second on four CPU cores, x265 at 38 and SVT-AV1 (preset 6) at 19, while MJPEG ran at 100. The better codecs buy their compression with search: more block shapes, more prediction modes, more reference frames, all evaluated per block. That is why cameras and vehicles encode with dedicated hardware blocks (Section 6) and why the newest codec is not automatically the right one.

Talk track

"Raw video is hundreds of megabits to several gigabits per second per camera; every link outside the camera harness is one or two orders of magnitude smaller. A codec closes that gap by predicting each block from its neighbours or from earlier frames, transforming the prediction error into frequencies, and quantising away the detail the viewer won't miss. The knobs then trade bitrate, quality and latency against each other: a streaming service optimises bits, a video call or a teleoperation link optimises latency, and a mirror replacement skips compression altogether."

2Image compression: JPEG step by step

JPEG is thirty years old and still the most common image format in the world, and every video codec's intra frames reuse its core idea: transform a block of pixels into frequencies and quantise the frequencies the eye cares least about. Understanding JPEG well is most of the way to understanding an I-frame.

2.1Colour transform and chroma subsampling

JPEG (ITU-T T.81, ISO/IEC 10918-1, 1992) first converts RGB into YCbCr: one luma channel Y that carries brightness, and two colour-difference channels Cb and Cr. The conversion is a 3 × 3 matrix and almost reversible. It is worth doing because it concentrates the visually important detail in one channel: human vision resolves fine luminance detail much better than fine colour detail. The encoder then subsamples the chroma channels, usually by two in both directions (4:2:0), so each 2 × 2 group of pixels shares one Cb and one Cr value. Half of all samples disappear before any clever processing has started, and for natural images almost nobody can see it. Thin coloured lines and red text on a blue background are where 4:2:0 shows, which is why screen-sharing codecs and some professional formats keep 4:4:4.

The same YUV 4:2:0 layout (in memory usually as NV12: a full-resolution Y plane followed by one interleaved CbCr plane at half resolution) is the native input of nearly every video encoder and hardware block, which is why ISPs output it (From photon to perception: how engineers reduce bandwidth).

2.2The DCT: from pixels to frequencies

Each channel is cut into 8 × 8 blocks, the values are shifted from 0…255 to −128…127, and each block goes through a two-dimensional discrete cosine transform (DCT-II). The DCT expresses the block as a weighted sum of 64 fixed cosine patterns, from the flat pattern (the DC coefficient, the block's average) to the finest checkerboard. It does not compress anything by itself: 64 numbers go in and 64 come out. What it does is concentrate energy. In a smooth block almost all the energy ends up in the DC coefficient and a few low-frequency terms, and the high-frequency coefficients are close to zero. Only blocks with edges or texture have significant high-frequency content, and even then it falls off quickly.

The DCT is used rather than the Fourier transform because its implicit symmetric extension avoids the artificial jump at the block boundary that a DFT would see, so it compacts energy better for real images, and it has fast integer-friendly implementations. Video codecs since H.264 use integer approximations of the DCT so that encoder and decoder compute bit-identical results; JPEG's floating-point DCT allowed small decoder mismatches, harmless for a still image but fatal for a video codec where each frame is predicted from the previous one and errors would accumulate.

2.3Quantisation: where the loss happens

Quantisation divides each coefficient by a step size from a quantisation table and rounds to the nearest integer. The decoder multiplies back, so a coefficient of 37 with step 16 becomes 2, and comes back as 32. That rounding is the loss, and it is the only place in JPEG (besides subsampling) where information disappears. The standard luminance table uses small steps (10–16) for low frequencies and large ones (up to 121) for high frequencies, because errors in high frequencies are less visible. The "quality" slider of a JPEG encoder does nothing but scale this table: in the common IJG convention quality 50 uses the table as printed, quality 75 halves it, quality 90 divides it by five, and quality 20 multiplies it by 2.5.

The effect is that most coefficients become zero. Figure 2.2 uses a real 8 × 8 block from the test frame where a lane marking crosses the asphalt: a sharp diagonal edge between grey 87 and white 225. At quality 90, 35 of the 64 coefficients survive and the decoded block is never more than 7 grey levels off. At quality 75, 21 survive with a maximum error of 15. At quality 20, only 8 survive, the error reaches 65 levels next to the edge, and the edge rings: the high-frequency terms that made it sharp have been rounded away, and the remaining cosines overshoot on both sides. That ringing around edges, and the visible block boundaries when neighbouring blocks are quantised differently, are the classic JPEG artefacts.

Interesting detail

Quantisation tables are stored in the JPEG file, so the decoder needs no knowledge of the quality setting. This makes it possible to estimate how a JPEG was compressed by reading its tables, which forensic tools use to detect images that were saved twice, and which camera makers use to give their files a characteristic table set.

2.4Zigzag, run-length and Huffman coding

After quantisation a block typically holds a non-zero DC value, a handful of non-zero low-frequency values and a long tail of zeros. JPEG reads the 64 values in a zigzag order that runs from the top-left (low frequencies) to the bottom-right (high frequencies), so the zeros cluster at the end. The DC coefficient is coded as the difference from the previous block's DC, because neighbouring block averages are similar. The AC coefficients are coded as pairs of (number of zeros before this value, size of the value), followed by the value's bits, and an end-of-block symbol replaces the trailing zeros. Finally Huffman coding gives the most frequent pairs the shortest bit strings. All of this is lossless: it removes statistical redundancy, nothing else.

A baseline decoder runs the same steps backwards: Huffman decode, undo zigzag, multiply by the table, inverse DCT, upsample the chroma, convert to RGB. Because the costly step on the encoder side (quantisation decisions) is trivial and the transform is symmetric, JPEG encoding and decoding cost about the same, which is not true of video codecs.

2.5Measured results, lossless options and successors

On frame 0 of the test sequence (2.76 MB as raw RGB), PNG, which is lossless (prediction from neighbours plus DEFLATE), reached 0.98 MB, a ratio of 2.8:1. JPEG at quality 95 produced 211 kB (13:1) with a luma PSNR of 46.0 dB; quality 75 63 kB (44:1, 40.1 dB); quality 50 43 kB (65:1, 37.7 dB); quality 20 28 kB (100:1, 34.9 dB). The jump from lossless to quality 95 is a factor of almost five for an error that nobody can see; the jump from 95 to 75 is another factor of three and still hard to see. Below about quality 50 the gains get small and the artefacts large: that is the typical shape of every rate-distortion curve.

The test sequence is synthetic; Figure 2.3 shows the same quality settings on a real road frame (fog, a street lamp and a sign against a grey sky, 1164 × 620 pixels). At quality 80 the file is 39 kB and nothing is visible. At quality 20 (17 kB) the flat sky still looks clean, but faint ripples follow the thin lamp arms: ringing, the high-frequency coefficients of a sharp edge quantised away, so the edge is rebuilt from too few cosines. At quality 5 (13 kB) the block grid itself appears: each 8 × 8 luma block, and each 16 × 16 area of the subsampled chroma, is reduced to little more than its average, so the smooth sky turns into flat tiles and colour bleeds across the lamp post.

Several formats improve on JPEG. JPEG 2000 (2000) replaced the block DCT with a wavelet transform, which avoids blocking and offers progressive and region-of-interest decoding; it is used in digital cinema and medical imaging but never took over the web. WebP (from VP8 intra coding), HEIC/HEIF (HEVC intra frames in a container, the default on iPhones since 2017) and AVIF (AV1 intra frames) reuse video-codec intra tools and typically save 30–50 % over JPEG at equal quality. JPEG XL (ISO/IEC 18181, 2022) is both a better lossy codec and a strong lossless one, and can losslessly recompress existing JPEG files about 20 % smaller. For lossless camera data, PNG, JPEG-LS, lossless JPEG XL and, for video, FFV1 are the usual choices; for raw Bayer data, vendors use lightweight lossless or near-lossless schemes that fit in hardware.

In practice

MJPEG (Motion JPEG, every frame a separate JPEG) is still common in USB webcams, machine-vision cameras and some recorders, because it is cheap, every frame decodes independently and editing is trivial. The price is visible in Figure 4.1: on the test sequence MJPEG needed 9 Mbit/s for the 40 dB that H.264 reached at 1.05 Mbit/s, about 8.5 times more, because it cannot use the similarity between frames.

Pitfall

Saving an image repeatedly as JPEG, or decoding and re-encoding a video, adds new quantisation error each time (generation loss). Training data, calibration images and anything used as ground truth should be kept lossless or in the camera's original compressed form, never re-encoded along the way.

3How video codecs work

All mainstream video codecs since MPEG-1, from H.264 to AV1 and VVC, share one architecture: the block-based hybrid coder. "Hybrid" because it combines prediction (from earlier frames or from neighbouring pixels) with transform coding of what the prediction got wrong. The codecs differ in the details of each tool, not in the plan.

3.1The hybrid coding loop

The encoder cuts the frame into blocks (16 × 16 macroblocks in H.264, coding tree units of up to 64 × 64 in H.265 and 128 × 128 in AV1 and VVC, recursively split into smaller blocks where the content needs it). For each block it builds a prediction: either from already decoded pixels of the same frame (intra prediction, extrapolating the neighbouring edge pixels in one of many directions) or from a block in an earlier decoded frame, shifted by a motion vector (inter prediction). It subtracts the prediction from the real block, and only this residual goes on: transform, quantisation, entropy coding, exactly as in JPEG. Next to the residual the bitstream carries the side information the decoder needs to rebuild the same prediction: block sizes, modes, motion vectors, reference indices.

The essential and slightly surprising part is that the encoder contains a complete decoder. After quantising a block it immediately dequantises and inverse-transforms it, adds the prediction back and stores the result, after in-loop filtering, in the decoded picture buffer. Future predictions are made from these reconstructed frames, not from the original camera frames. If the encoder predicted from the originals, the decoder (which only has reconstructed frames) would predict from slightly different pixels, and the difference would grow with every frame: drift. Predicting from what the decoder will actually have keeps the two in lockstep, which is why every codec standard specifies the decoder bit-exactly and leaves the encoder free.

For each block the encoder tries intra modes (extrapolate from the already coded neighbours above and to the left) and inter modes (search earlier reconstructed frames for a matching block, Section 3.2), and picks the one that minimises a cost of the form distortion + λ × bits. This rate-distortion optimisation (RDO) is where most encoder time goes.

The residual (block minus prediction) is transformed with an integer DCT-like transform (sometimes a DST for small intra blocks), and the coefficients are divided by a step size set by the quantisation parameter QP. In H.264 and H.265 the step size doubles every 6 QP; the deadzone and rounding decisions (trellis quantisation) are themselves optimised for rate and distortion.

Quantised coefficients, modes and motion vectors become bits with context-adaptive entropy coding (Section 3.5). Motion vectors are coded as differences from a prediction made from neighbouring blocks' vectors, which are usually similar.

The encoder dequantises, inverse-transforms, adds the prediction, runs the in-loop filters (deblocking, and in newer codecs SAO, CDEF or ALF) and stores the result as a reference. The decoder executes exactly this path, so both hold identical reference frames.

3.2Motion estimation and compensation

Temporal prediction is where video codecs earn most of their advantage over JPEG. For a block in the current frame, motion estimation searches a window in a reference frame for the block that matches best, typically by the sum of absolute differences, refined with the actual rate-distortion cost. The offset is the motion vector. Motion compensation is the decoder-side half: copy the referenced block, shifted by the vector, and add the residual. In a scene where the camera moves forward, most blocks find a good match a few pixels away, and the residual is small: mostly noise and the parts of the scene that changed shape.

Real motion is not aligned to the pixel grid, so codecs use fractional vectors: quarter-pixel in H.264 and H.265 (with interpolation filters of 6 to 8 taps to compute in-between pixels), down to 1/8 or 1/16 in AV1 and VVC for some modes. Motion is also not purely translational: AV1 and VVC add affine or warped motion models for zoom and rotation, which suits a forward-driving camera where the scene expands away from the vanishing point. Exhaustive search is far too slow (a ±64 pixel window at quarter-pel precision is over 260 000 candidates per block), so encoders use fast patterns (diamond, hexagon, multi-hypothesis searches seeded with the neighbours' vectors), and hardware encoders often use a hierarchical search on downscaled frames.

Why it matters

Motion vectors are a by-product of encoding that perception people sometimes reuse: they are a cheap, coarse optical flow. They are optimised for bits, not for truth, so they jump to wherever the residual is cheapest (in flat areas, often to a wrong place), but for activity detection in surveillance cameras or for selecting interesting clips they are free.

3.3Frame types and the group of pictures

Frames come in three kinds. An I frame (intra) uses no other frame; it is a JPEG-like picture coded with intra prediction, and a decoder can start there. An IDR frame (instantaneous decoder refresh) is an I frame that additionally forbids any later frame from referencing anything before it, so it is a clean entry point for seeking or for joining a live stream. A P frame (predicted) uses earlier frames as references. A B frame (bi-predicted) may use references from both the past and the future, and may average two predictions, which is especially efficient for content that appears or is uncovered. The sequence from one key frame to the next is the group of pictures (GOP).

B frames create a reordering: a B frame that refers to a later P frame cannot be decoded until that P frame has arrived, so the encoder sends frames in decode order, not display order, and every frame carries two time stamps, the decode time (DTS) and the presentation time (PTS). Figure 3.4 shows what x264 actually produced with a GOP of 30 and three B frames: display order I0 B1 B2 B3 P4…, but the packets left the encoder as I0, P4, B2, B1, B3, P8, B6… The middle B frame (B2) is itself used as a reference by B1 and B3, a "B-pyramid", which is why it is larger than its neighbours.

The ratio between frame types depends on content: with little motion an I frame can be ten times the size of a P frame, with heavy motion only two or three times. That ratio matters a lot for streaming, because the network has to carry the I frame's burst (Section 5). GOP length is the other key knob. Long GOPs (several seconds, as in streaming services: x264's default is 250 frames) save bits because I frames are expensive. Short GOPs (0.5–1 s) let a viewer join or seek quickly and limit how long an error can propagate. Recorders, broadcast and live streaming typically use 1–2 s.

Pitfall

B frames add latency that no network fix can remove: with three B frames the encoder must wait for frame 4 before it can send frame 1, so at 30 fps at least 100 ms are lost before any packet leaves. Low-latency profiles (and H.264 Baseline, which has no B frames at all) avoid them.

3.4Transforms, QP and in-loop filters

The residual transform in H.264 is a 4 × 4 (and, in the High profile, 8 × 8) integer approximation of the DCT that can be computed with additions and shifts, bit-exact on any platform. H.265 allows 4 × 4 up to 32 × 32 and uses a discrete sine transform for 4 × 4 intra luma, which fits the residual of an extrapolated block better. AV1 offers 16 combinations of DCT, ADST, flipped ADST and identity transforms horizontally and vertically, and rectangular sizes up to 64 × 64. VVC adds multiple transform selection and a secondary low-frequency transform. All of this serves the same purpose as JPEG's DCT: make the residual's energy compact so that quantisation zeroes most of it.

The quantisation parameter (QP) sets the step size. In H.264 and H.265 (8-bit) QP runs from 0 to 51 and the step doubles every 6 QP, so the bitrate roughly halves every 5–6 QP steps; on the test sequence going from CRF 18 to CRF 23 cut x264's bitrate from 3.3 to 1.7 Mbit/s. AV1 uses a 0–255 quantiser index with a similar logarithmic effect. Encoders vary QP between frames (B frames get a higher QP than the references they are predicted from, because fewer frames depend on them) and between blocks (adaptive quantisation spends more bits on flat areas, where blocking is visible, and fewer on busy texture, where it hides).

After reconstruction, in-loop filters clean up the frame before it becomes a reference. The deblocking filter (since H.264) smooths block boundaries where the quantisation of neighbouring blocks differed, but only where the step across the edge is small enough to be an artefact rather than a real edge. H.265 adds sample adaptive offset (SAO), which corrects banding and ringing with small per-category offsets. AV1 adds CDEF (a directional de-ringing filter) and loop restoration (Wiener and self-guided filters); VVC adds an adaptive loop filter (ALF) with coefficients chosen by the encoder. Being "in-loop" matters: the filtered frame is what later frames are predicted from, so the filter improves not only what you see but also the prediction.

Figure 3.5 shows the deblocking filter at work on the road frame of Figure 2.3, encoded at a very low quality (QP 44, about 130 kbit/s for 1164 × 620 at 20 frames/s). Without the filter the sky and the trees break into visible squares; with it the same quantised data decodes to a soft but continuous picture. The filter also saved bits (4 % at the same QP) and raised PSNR slightly, because the smoother reference frames predict the next frames better.

3.5Entropy coding: CAVLC and CABAC

The last stage turns symbols into bits. H.264 offers two methods. CAVLC (context-adaptive variable-length coding) uses Huffman-like tables chosen by context, for example by how many non-zero coefficients the neighbouring blocks had; it is cheap and is the only option in the Baseline profile. CABAC (context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding) first turns every symbol into binary decisions, then codes each decision with an arithmetic coder whose probability model is selected by context and updated after every bin. Arithmetic coding can spend fractions of a bit per decision (a decision that is 95 % predictable costs about 0.07 bits), and the adaptive probabilities learn the statistics of this particular video. CABAC typically saves 10–15 % of the bitrate over CAVLC at equal quality, at the price of a strictly serial decoding loop that is hard to parallelise; this is why H.264 decoders specify throughput limits per level, and why H.265 dropped CAVLC entirely and spent effort making CABAC more parallel-friendly. AV1 uses a non-binary adaptive arithmetic coder with up to 16 symbols per decision.

3.6The bitstream: NAL units, parameter sets and slices

An H.264 or H.265 bitstream is a sequence of NAL units (network abstraction layer units), each with a small header that says what it contains. The sequence parameter set (SPS) holds stream-wide settings (profile, level, resolution, reference frame count), the picture parameter set (PPS) per-picture settings such as the entropy coder; H.265 adds a video parameter set (VPS). Pictures are coded as one or more slices, each of which can be decoded independently of the other slices of the same picture (prediction does not cross slice boundaries). Slices exist for two reasons: parallel encoding and decoding, and error resilience, because a lost packet then destroys one slice instead of the whole frame. SEI messages carry optional metadata (time codes, HDR information, user data such as a capture time stamp), and an access unit delimiter marks the start of a new frame.

There are two ways to put NAL units in a file or stream, and mixing them up is one of the most common integration bugs. In the Annex B byte-stream format each NAL unit is preceded by a start code 00 00 01 (or 00 00 00 01), and SPS and PPS are repeated in the stream; raw .h264 files, MPEG-TS and most hardware encoders use it. In the AVCC (length-prefixed) format used inside MP4 and MKV each NAL unit is preceded by its length, and SPS/PPS are stored once in the file's header (the avcC box). FFmpeg's h264_mp4toannexb bitstream filter and GStreamer's h264parse convert between the two.

Pitfall

A receiver that joins a live stream needs an SPS and a PPS before it can decode anything. If the encoder sends them only once at the start, late joiners show a black screen until someone restarts the sender. Configure the encoder or payloader to repeat them with every key frame (GStreamer config-interval=-1 on rtph264pay or h264parse, x264 repeat-headers), or signal them out of band in the SDP.

4Codecs compared: H.264, H.265, AV1, VVC

Each generation of codecs has bought roughly a 30–50 % bitrate reduction at equal quality with several times the encoder complexity. Which one a product uses depends less on that headline number than on hardware support, licensing and what the receiving side can decode.

4.1Two families and four generations

Video coding standards come from two families. The ITU-T and ISO/IEC MPEG joint teams produced MPEG-2 (1995, DVD and broadcast), H.264/AVC (2003), H.265/HEVC (2013) and H.266/VVC (2020). Each has two names because it is published by both bodies (H.264 is also MPEG-4 Part 10). The second family is royalty-free by intention: Google's VP8 (2010) and VP9 (2013), then AV1 (2018) from the Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia), whose members include Google, Netflix, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Intel and NVIDIA. AOMedia announced its successor AV2 in 2025, claiming about 30 % lower bitrate than AV1 in its own tests, published a draft specification in early 2026 and released version 1.0 in 2026; software implementations come first and hardware support does not exist yet.

H.264 is the universal one: every phone, browser, GPU, camera SoC and automotive processor decodes it, most encode it, and it is the safe default when the receiver is unknown. H.265 roughly halves the bitrate of H.264 for high-resolution content and is standard in phones (iPhone video since 2017), broadcast 4K, IP cameras and many automotive SoCs, but its patent situation (several competing licence pools plus individual licensors) kept it out of browsers for years. AV1 is now widely decoded in hardware (GPUs since about 2020, phones since about 2023) and is used heavily by YouTube and Netflix and in WebRTC, with hardware encoders in recent PC GPUs and some SoCs. VVC is the most efficient standard on paper but has little hardware support so far.

4.2What actually gets better from one generation to the next

No generation introduced a new principle. Each one made every tool of the hybrid coder more flexible and gave the encoder more choices: bigger blocks for smooth areas at high resolution (a 4K sky in 16 × 16 macroblocks wastes thousands of block headers), smaller and non-square blocks along edges, more intra directions, more and better interpolation filters, more reference frames, better motion vector prediction (merge modes that copy a neighbour's motion), more transform types, more in-loop filters, and better entropy coding contexts. The decoder has to support all of it, which raises its complexity two to three times per generation; the encoder has to search all of it, which raises its complexity five to ten times if it wants the full gain.

That last point explains why codec comparisons often disagree. A fast hardware H.265 encoder that only tries a fraction of the modes may be barely better than a good software H.264 encoder, and x264 at its slowest preset beats many hardware encoders of newer codecs. Comparing codecs really means comparing encoders at a given speed.

4.3Measured: four codecs on the test sequence

Figure 4.1 shows real rate-distortion curves. The 180-frame 720p test sequence was encoded with MJPEG (FFmpeg, quality 2–31), H.264 (x264 medium preset, CRF 18–38), H.265 (x265 medium, CRF 18–38) and AV1 (SVT-AV1 preset 6, CRF 25–55), and each encode was compared with the lossless original using FFmpeg's PSNR and SSIM filters. At 40 dB PSNR-Y, H.264 needed 1053 kbit/s, H.265 619 kbit/s (41 % less), AV1 309 kbit/s (71 % less) and MJPEG 8959 kbit/s (8.5 times more). At 42 dB the savings were 36 % for H.265 and 65 % for AV1.

These AV1 gains are larger than the 30–50 % usually reported, for reasons worth understanding because they apply to every codec test. The sequence is synthetic: smooth procedural textures, clean geometry and mild Gaussian noise, with large areas where long-range motion prediction and big blocks shine. SVT-AV1 also used a longer prediction structure (mini-GOPs of 32 frames with hierarchical references) than x264's default three B frames. On real camera footage with fine texture, sensor noise and rain, the curves move closer together. Treat the figure as an illustration of the shape and order of the curves, not as a benchmark of the codecs.

Pitfall

The first run of this experiment reported every codec flat at 31 dB, even at the highest quality. The cause was frame alignment: the lossless reference stored millisecond time stamps (33 and 34 ms alternately) while the MP4 files used exact 1/30 s steps, so FFmpeg's metric filter paired frame n with frame n + 1 every so often, and PSNR measured motion instead of coding error. Re-stamping both inputs (setpts=N/30/TB) gave the correct 43–44 dB. Always sanity-check a metric on a known case before trusting it.

4.4Profiles, levels and hardware support

A profile says which tools a stream may use; a level says how much work it may demand from the decoder. H.264 Baseline has no B frames and no CABAC (video calls and cheap devices); Main adds both; High adds the 8 × 8 transform and is what nearly everything uses today; there are 10-bit and 4:2:2 profiles for professional and HDR use. Levels cap the macroblock rate, frame size, bitrate and reference buffer: H.264 level 4.1, for example, covers 1080p30 at up to 50 Mbit/s (Main/High). A decoder that claims "High profile level 4.1" must decode any stream within those limits, so the pair is the contract between encoder and decoder. H.265 has Main, Main 10 and range-extension profiles plus tiers (Main and High) for bitrate; AV1 has Main (4:2:0, 8/10-bit), High (4:4:4) and Professional.

In a product the question is usually not which codec is best but which codec the hardware on both ends can encode and decode at the needed resolution, frame rate and number of streams. Camera and automotive SoCs carry fixed-function encoders that typically support H.264 and H.265 (NVIDIA's Jetson AGX Orin, for example, has hardware H.264/H.265 encoders, while the smaller Orin Nano has none and must encode on the CPU); AV1 encode is common in PC GPUs and arriving in SoCs. Phones and browsers decide what a remote viewer can play: H.264 everywhere, AV1 and VP9 in modern browsers, H.265 in Safari and, more recently, in Chrome where the hardware supports it.

Talk track

"H.264 is the compatibility choice: every device decodes it. H.265 saves roughly 40 % and is in most camera and automotive SoCs, but its licensing is messy. AV1 saves more and is royalty-free; decode is everywhere now, encode is still arriving in embedded hardware. VVC is the most efficient on paper but has little silicon. So we choose by the encoder we have on the SoC and the decoders on the receiving side, then tune the encoder."

5Rate control, latency and error resilience

A codec defines what the bitstream may contain; rate control decides how many bits each frame and each block actually gets. That decision fixes the bitrate, the quality and, through the buffers it needs, the latency. Error resilience decides what happens when part of the stream does not arrive.

5.1Rate control modes and the leaky bucket

The simplest mode is constant QP (CQP): every frame uses the same quantiser. Quality is roughly constant and the bitrate follows the content wildly. CRF (constant rate factor, x264/x265; "CQ" or "CRF" in other encoders) targets constant perceived quality: it varies QP with motion and complexity, because errors in fast-moving areas are less visible, and is the right mode for files and archives. ABR/VBR target an average bitrate over the whole stream, often with two passes so the encoder knows in advance where the hard scenes are. CBR targets a constant rate over short windows, as needed by a fixed-capacity channel such as a broadcast multiplex or a reserved link. Capped CRF (CRF plus a maximum rate) gives constant quality except where that would exceed the channel, and is what most live streaming and recording should use.

What "constant over short windows" means is defined by a leaky-bucket model: the video buffering verifier (VBV) in x264 terms, the hypothetical reference decoder (HRD) in the standards. Picture a buffer between encoder and channel. Each coded frame is poured in at once; the channel drains it at the fixed rate R. The buffer size B is the burst the channel can absorb, and the encoder must choose frame sizes so the buffer never overflows. The same buffer appears, mirrored, in the decoder, which must wait until enough data has arrived. So the buffer size is not an abstract parameter: B / R is latency. A 1.5 Mbit/s stream with a 100 kbit buffer has at most 67 ms of buffering; with a 3 Mbit buffer it has two seconds.

The model makes the key-frame problem obvious. At 4 Mbit/s and 30 fps each frame may average 16.7 kB. With a GOP of 30 and an I frame five times the size of a P frame, the I frame is 73 kB, which takes 147 ms to transmit. Every IDR therefore causes a burst of nearly 150 ms of latency, and a low-latency system must either size its buffers for it (accepting 150 ms on every frame, since the receiver cannot know when the burst comes) or keep I frames small (and ugly). Intra refresh is the third way: instead of one I frame, a column (or band) of intra-coded blocks sweeps across the picture over the GOP, so every frame carries 1/N of a key frame and has the same size. In the model the worst-case buffer delay drops from 147 ms to one frame time, 33 ms.

5.2Measured: what the modes do around a scene cut

To see this in a real encoder, the test sequence was given a hard scene cut at frame 90 (the second half is a mirrored, darker view, like entering a tunnel) and encoded three ways with x264: CRF 23 with a key frame every 30 frames; CBR at 1.5 Mbit/s with a 100 kbit buffer (67 ms) and IDR frames; and the same CBR with intra refresh. Figure 5.2 shows the per-frame sizes and quality.

CRF averaged 1.79 Mbit/s with frames from 4 to 33 kB: fine for a file, impossible for a 67 ms buffer. The CBR encode with IDR frames held the rate exactly, but its key frames were capped at about 9 kB, so their quality fell by about 3 dB (from 37.7 to 34.6 dB at frame 30), and the following P frames inherited the worse reference and needed a second or so to recover: a visible "breathing" every second. With intra refresh every frame was 6.25 kB and there were no periodic dips; the mean PSNR was about the same (37.4 versus 37.3 dB). After the cut both CBR variants dropped to about 34 dB and climbed back over a second, because the encoder had no bits to spare to paint a whole new scene. A CRF encoder simply spent more bits there.

In practice

For recording, use capped CRF with a generous buffer (one to two seconds). For interactive or remote-control links, use CBR or capped VBR with a buffer of one to a few frame times, no B frames, no look-ahead, and intra refresh or small I frames; accept lower quality after scene changes. The x264 options that implement this are bundled as tune=zerolatency; hardware encoders have a "low latency" or "ultra low latency" rate-control preset.

5.3Where latency comes from

"Glass-to-glass" latency runs from light hitting the camera to light leaving the display, and every stage adds to it. The sensor exposes and reads out a frame (up to one frame time, 33 ms at 30 fps, unless the pipeline processes lines as they arrive). The ISP adds a few milliseconds to a frame. The encoder adds its look-ahead and reordering delay plus its processing time. The packetiser and network add serialisation time (an I frame at a low bitrate takes long to transmit, as in Section 5.1) and propagation and queueing delay. The receiver's jitter buffer adds a safety margin against variable arrival times. The decoder adds a frame or less. The display adds its own buffering and refresh (on average half a refresh interval, often a full frame in a compositor).

The encoder's contribution is the one people underestimate. Measured with GStreamer's latency tracer on 720p30 input, x264 with its default settings (medium preset, 40-frame rate-control look-ahead, B frames, macroblock-tree) held each frame for 1.39 seconds on average. With tune=zerolatency (no look-ahead, no B frames, sliced threads so that one frame is encoded by several threads in parallel) the same encoder added 7.3 ms, and 4.7 ms at the ultrafast preset. The defaults are tuned for files, where latency does not exist; every live system has to undo them.

Numbers

Rough latency classes: a CMS or surround-view display on a direct link, 50–100 ms; teleoperation and drone links, 100–200 ms; video calls, 150–400 ms mouth to ear; WebRTC-based live streaming, under 0.5 s; SRT contribution links, 0.2–2 s depending on the retransmission budget; low-latency HLS/DASH, 2–5 s; classic HLS/DASH, 10–30 s; broadcast TV, 3–10 s.

5.4Error resilience: when packets go missing

Prediction makes compression efficient and errors persistent. A lost packet destroys the slice it carried; the decoder conceals it (usually by copying the co-located area from the previous frame, perhaps with neighbouring motion vectors); and every later frame that predicts from the damaged area copies the error forward, smeared along the motion vectors, until an intra-coded block replaces it. Error resilience is the set of tools that limit this damage. Slices confine a loss to part of a frame; with a maximum slice size equal to the packet payload, one lost packet costs exactly one slice. Periodic IDR frames or intra refresh bound how long an error lives. Feedback lets the receiver ask for repair: RTCP NACK requests retransmission of a specific packet (useful when the round trip is short compared with the latency budget), and PLI or FIR asks the encoder for a new key frame. Forward error correction (FEC) sends redundant parity packets so the receiver can rebuild a lost one without a round trip, at a fixed bitrate overhead. Encoders can also use reference picture selection: after a loss report, predict only from frames the receiver is known to have, which repairs the stream without a key frame.

Figure 5.4 measures the basic trade-off. The test sequence was encoded at 2 Mbit/s with slices of at most 1200 bytes (about six per frame) and 1 % of the slices were removed with a fixed random seed, as if one packet in a hundred had been lost; FFmpeg's decoder concealed the losses. With an IDR every 60 frames, mean PSNR fell from 39.6 to 34.0 dB and 166 of 180 frames were more than 1 dB worse than the clean stream: the losses came faster than the key frames could repair them. An IDR every 15 frames cut the damage to 72 frames (mean 35.9 dB) but cost bitrate and still showed deep dips. Intra refresh over 15 frames also limited the damage to 72 frames, gave the best mean (37.3 dB) and the shallowest worst case (31.5 dB). With intra refresh over 60 frames, the loss affected 176 frames: refresh helps only if its period is shorter than the typical gap between losses.

Pitfall

At 1 % packet loss and six packets per frame, about 6 % of frames are damaged, one every 17 frames. A design with "an IDR every two seconds" therefore shows damaged video almost all the time. Either the loss rate must be brought down (retransmission, FEC, a better link) or the refresh period must be well below the loss interval; usually both.

Talk track

"Prediction means a lost packet doesn't just break one frame, it breaks everything that references it until the next intra refresh. So for a lossy link we make slices fit packets, keep the refresh short with intra refresh rather than big I frames so the bitrate stays flat, and add a repair path: NACK retransmission if the round trip is short, a key-frame request or FEC if it isn't."

6Hardware encoders and media pipelines

On a camera, a vehicle computer or a phone, video is encoded by a fixed-function block on the SoC, not by x264. The software around it is a pipeline framework (GStreamer, FFmpeg, or a vendor SDK on top of V4L2) whose main job is to move buffers between hardware blocks without copying them.

6.1Why encoding is done in hardware

Software encoders on general-purpose CPUs are flexible and give the best quality per bit, but they are expensive: x264 at the medium preset used all four cores of the test machine for 54 fps at 720p, which extrapolates to roughly one core per 1080p30 stream at a faster preset, tens of watts for a handful of cameras. A hardware encoder (NVIDIA NVENC, Intel Quick Sync, the video processing units in Qualcomm, TI, Renesas, NXP, Ambarella and Rockchip SoCs) is a dedicated pipeline of motion search, transform, quantisation and entropy-coding engines that encodes several 4K streams at a fraction of a watt and leaves the CPU and GPU free for perception.

The price is flexibility and some compression efficiency. Hardware encoders search fewer modes and smaller motion ranges, often have no or limited B frames and look-ahead, and expose only the knobs the driver offers: bitrate, rate-control mode, GOP length, profile and level, QP limits, intra refresh, slice size, and a "force key frame" command. For live and low-latency use these limits hardly matter, because a low-latency software encoder gives up the same tools; for archive-quality encoding a slow software encoder can be 20–40 % more efficient. Most hardware encoders also report per-frame statistics (sizes, average QP, sometimes motion vectors) and accept per-block QP maps for region-of-interest coding (Section 9.3).

Interesting detail

Encoding is far more expensive than decoding, because only the encoder searches. A decoder just follows the instructions in the bitstream. This asymmetry is deliberate: one encoder serves millions of viewers. It also means that "the SoC supports AV1" frequently means decode only.

6.2V4L2 memory-to-memory: the Linux interface to codec hardware

On embedded Linux, codec hardware is usually exposed through Video4Linux2 as a memory-to-memory (M2M) device: a /dev/videoN node with two buffer queues (the camera capture side of V4L2 is described in Camera, GPU and display 2.4 and Linux 6.5). The naming is from the application's point of view and confuses everyone once: the OUTPUT queue is where the application outputs data to the device (raw frames, for an encoder), and the CAPTURE queue is where it captures results from the device (the compressed bitstream). The application sets formats on both queues, requests buffers, starts streaming, and then loops: queue a raw frame on OUTPUT, dequeue a finished bitstream buffer from CAPTURE, return the used buffers. Encoder settings are V4L2 controls (V4L2_CID_MPEG_VIDEO_BITRATE, ..._GOP_SIZE, ..._H264_PROFILE, ..._FORCE_KEY_FRAME and so on).

/* Sketch of a V4L2 M2M H.264 encode loop (error handling omitted). */
int fd = open("/dev/video11", O_RDWR | O_NONBLOCK);
struct v4l2_format out = { .type = V4L2_BUF_TYPE_VIDEO_OUTPUT_MPLANE };
out.fmt.pix_mp = (struct v4l2_pix_format_mplane){ .width = 1280, .height = 720,
                  .pixelformat = V4L2_PIX_FMT_NV12, .num_planes = 1 };
ioctl(fd, VIDIOC_S_FMT, &out);                         /* raw frames in            */
struct v4l2_format cap = { .type = V4L2_BUF_TYPE_VIDEO_CAPTURE_MPLANE };
cap.fmt.pix_mp.pixelformat = V4L2_PIX_FMT_H264;
ioctl(fd, VIDIOC_S_FMT, &cap);                         /* bitstream out            */
struct v4l2_control br = { V4L2_CID_MPEG_VIDEO_BITRATE, 4000000 };
ioctl(fd, VIDIOC_S_CTRL, &br);
/* VIDIOC_REQBUFS on both queues (OUTPUT with V4L2_MEMORY_DMABUF for zero copy),
   VIDIOC_STREAMON on both, then: */
for (;;) {
    poll(&pfd, 1, -1);
    /* VIDIOC_DQBUF on OUTPUT: a raw buffer the encoder has finished reading -> give back to camera
       VIDIOC_QBUF  on OUTPUT: next camera frame (its dmabuf fd)
       VIDIOC_DQBUF on CAPTURE: one encoded frame; bytesused = its size; flags show key frame
       VIDIOC_QBUF  on CAPTURE: return the empty bitstream buffer */
}

There are two kinds of M2M codec drivers. Stateful codecs run firmware that parses the bitstream and manages reference frames itself; the application only feeds buffers. Stateless codecs (common in newer SoCs, used through the media request API) are bare hardware engines: user space parses the headers, tracks the reference frames and passes per-frame parameters with each request. Stateless designs move complexity into libraries such as GStreamer's v4l2codecs elements, but let one driver serve several codecs. Vendors often wrap all this in their own APIs (NVIDIA's Multimedia API and NvMedia on Jetson and DRIVE, Qualcomm's and TI's OpenMAX or proprietary layers), but the concepts are the same.

6.3GStreamer: pipelines of elements

GStreamer is the most common media framework on embedded Linux and in automotive infotainment and camera software. An application builds a pipeline of elements (sources, filters, encoders, payloaders, sinks) linked through pads. Before data flows, neighbouring pads negotiate caps (capabilities: format, resolution, frame rate, memory type), so a pipeline automatically agrees on NV12 in DMABUF memory if both sides support it. A queue element decouples its two sides into separate threads; a tee fans one stream out to several branches; a leaky queue drops buffers instead of blocking when a branch falls behind, which is what you want in front of a slow consumer so it cannot stall the camera. Timing is handled by a pipeline clock and per-buffer time stamps, and the framework propagates latency queries so that sinks know how much delay upstream adds.

6.4FFmpeg: the Swiss army knife

FFmpeg is two things: a set of libraries (libavcodec for codecs, libavformat for containers and protocols, libavfilter for processing, libswscale for scaling and colour conversion) used inside countless products, including GStreamer's av* elements, browsers and media players; and the ffmpeg and ffprobe command-line tools that every video engineer uses daily for conversion, analysis and testing. Hardware encoders appear in FFmpeg as separate encoders (h264_nvenc, h264_vaapi, h264_qsv, h264_v4l2m2m), so the same command line can test software and hardware encoding. Where GStreamer is a framework for long-running pipelines with dynamic behaviour, FFmpeg is the tool for batch processing, measurement and quick experiments; many products use both.

6.5Zero-copy buffers

A 1080p NV12 frame is 3.1 MB; copying it costs about as much memory bandwidth as reading and writing it once more, and on an SoC with several cameras, an ISP, a GPU, an NPU and an encoder all sharing DRAM, bandwidth runs out before compute does (Embedded compute 4.4). The rule in a video pipeline is therefore that pixels are written once by the producer and read in place by every consumer. On Linux the currency for this is the dma-buf file descriptor (Camera, GPU and display 4.4): the camera driver exports its buffers as dma-bufs, the encoder imports them on its OUTPUT queue, the GPU imports them as textures and the NPU as input tensors (Edge AI 7.3). The practical obstacles are formats and alignment: an encoder may need a 64-byte stride or a 16-line height alignment, a tiled or compressed layout from one block may not be readable by another, and if the formats do not match, the framework silently inserts a conversion, which is a copy.

Pitfall

A GStreamer pipeline that "works" can be quietly copying every frame: a videoconvert negotiated into the path, system memory instead of DMABUF caps, or a software scaler. Check the negotiated caps with gst-launch-1.0 -v (look for memory:DMABuf or the vendor memory type), and watch CPU load and DRAM bandwidth per camera added.

7Containers and transport

An encoder produces a sequence of compressed frames. To store them you need a container that adds time stamps, an index and the other tracks; to send them you need a transport that splits them into packets, recovers timing at the receiver and copes with loss and jitter. The choice depends on whether the receiver is a disk, a browser, a broadcast chain or a remote operator.

7.1Codec versus container: MP4, MKV and MPEG-TS

A codec defines the compressed frames; a container defines the file around them: which tracks exist (video, audio, metadata, subtitles), which codec each uses with its configuration (for H.264, the SPS/PPS), the time stamp of every frame, and an index for seeking. The same H.264 stream can live in MP4, MKV or MPEG-TS, and changing container ("remuxing") costs no quality and almost no CPU.

MP4 (ISO base media file format, ISO/IEC 14496-12) is a tree of boxes. In the classic layout the samples sit in one big mdat box and the index (moov) is written at the end, because the recorder only knows the sizes and positions when it has finished. That has a consequence every camera product meets eventually: if power is lost during recording, the moov box is never written and the file is unplayable without repair tools. Fragmented MP4 writes a small index (moof) before every fragment of a second or two, so a crash loses at most the last fragment; it is also the basis of CMAF, the common segment format of HLS and DASH streaming. MKV (Matroska, on the EBML format) is flexible and tolerant of truncation. MPEG-TS (transport stream) is a sequence of fixed 188-byte packets with a program clock reference, designed for broadcast over lossy channels; it has no global index at all, so any truncated file plays, and it is still used for broadcast, SRT contribution links and classic HLS.

Pitfall

Time stamps are part of the container, not of the codec, and they are the most common source of "the video plays too fast", "audio drifts" and "frames are dropped" bugs. Keep the capture time stamp from the camera driver all the way through, use a container time base that represents it exactly (MP4 commonly uses 90 kHz for video), and never regenerate time stamps from a frame counter if the camera can drop or duplicate frames.

7.2RTP and RTSP

The Real-time Transport Protocol (RTP, RFC 3550) is the basis of nearly all low-latency video transport: IP cameras, video calls, WebRTC, broadcast contribution and in-vehicle streaming over Ethernet (where the related IEEE 1722 AVTP is also used, Automotive networks 7.7). RTP runs over UDP, because retransmitting every lost packet in order, as TCP does, would stall everything behind a loss (Networking and security 3.7, 4.1). Each packet carries a 12-byte header: a sequence number that increases by one per packet (so the receiver detects loss and reordering), a timestamp on a media clock (90 kHz for video, so 3000 ticks per frame at 30 fps, identical for all packets of one frame), a marker bit set on the last packet of a frame, a payload type and a stream identifier (SSRC). Its companion RTCP sends periodic sender and receiver reports (packet loss, jitter, and the mapping from RTP time to wall-clock time used to synchronise audio and video) and carries the feedback messages of Section 5.4.

How a codec's frames are cut into packets is defined by a payload format per codec: RFC 6184 for H.264, RFC 7798 for H.265, an AOMedia specification for AV1. For H.264 a small NAL unit is sent whole (single NAL unit mode), several tiny ones can share a packet (STAP-A, for SPS and PPS), and a NAL unit larger than the path MTU is split into FU-A fragments with start and end bits. Figure 7.2 shows a real capture from this chapter's experiments: the first frame of a GStreamer stream was 22 packets and 16.4 kB, with SPS, PPS and an SEI sent first and four IDR slices (one per encoder thread) fragmented into 1200-byte pieces.

RTSP (Real Time Streaming Protocol, RFC 2326 and 7826) is the control protocol that IP cameras, NVRs and many automotive and industrial devices use to set up RTP sessions: the client sends DESCRIBE (and receives an SDP description listing the streams, codecs, payload types and parameters such as the base64-encoded SPS/PPS), SETUP for each stream (choosing UDP ports or RTP interleaved in the RTSP TCP connection), then PLAY, PAUSE and TEARDOWN. ONVIF, the interoperability standard for security cameras, builds on RTSP and RTP.

7.3Jitter buffers and packet loss

Packets leave the sender at a steady rhythm but arrive with variable delay, because they queue behind other traffic, take Wi-Fi retries or cross a cellular scheduler. The receiver cannot decode a frame until all its packets are there, and it must show frames at a steady rate. The jitter buffer solves this by holding packets for a fixed or adaptive delay and releasing them by RTP timestamp: frame n is played out at its send time plus the buffer delay. Packets that arrive within the buffer delay are re-ordered and played on time; a packet that arrives later is useless for playback and counts as lost. The buffer also gives retransmission (NACK) a chance: if a lost packet can be requested and resent within the buffer delay, the loss never becomes visible.

Sizing the buffer is a statistical decision: it should cover the delay variation of almost all packets, plus one retransmission round trip if NACK is used. On a LAN 10–30 ms is enough; on the internet WebRTC's adaptive buffer typically settles at 50–200 ms; over a cellular uplink from a moving vehicle the tail can reach hundreds of milliseconds during handovers. GStreamer's rtpjitterbuffer defaults to 200 ms, which surprises anyone measuring the latency of a LAN stream for the first time.

7.4WebRTC, SRT, RIST, HLS and DASH

Several transport stacks build on these pieces for different jobs. WebRTC is RTP made safe for the open internet and for browsers: ICE, STUN and TURN find a path through NATs and firewalls, DTLS-SRTP encrypts every packet, RTCP feedback provides NACK, PLI and FEC, and congestion control (Google Congestion Control with transport-wide feedback) estimates the available bandwidth continuously and tells the encoder to change its bitrate within a few hundred milliseconds. Video calls, cloud gaming, browser-based remote viewing and many teleoperation systems use it; WHIP and WHEP standardise how to publish to and play from WebRTC servers.

SRT (Secure Reliable Transport, open-sourced by Haivision in 2017) carries any payload, usually MPEG-TS, over UDP with selective retransmission inside a fixed latency window (default 120 ms, typically set to three to four times the round-trip time) and optional AES encryption; it is the standard for broadcast contribution over the public internet, and works well for vehicle-to-cloud live video where WebRTC's browser machinery is not needed. RIST is a similar standard from the broadcast industry. HLS (Apple) and MPEG-DASH do something completely different: they cut the video into segments of a few seconds, encode each at several bitrates (the ladder), and let the player download segments over plain HTTP, picking the bitrate that its measured throughput allows. That scales to millions of viewers through CDNs (Backend and system design 3.4) at the price of seconds of latency; low-latency variants with partial segments bring it to 2–5 s.

7.5How the mainstream does it: streaming services and video calls

A streaming service optimises bits per perceived quality at enormous scale, because every saved kilobit is multiplied by millions of viewers. Netflix's well-known move from a fixed bitrate ladder to per-title encoding (2015) and then to per-shot optimisation picks, for each title or scene, the resolution and bitrate pairs that maximise a quality metric (VMAF, Section 8.3) for each bandwidth; a cartoon gets a much lower ladder than a grainy film. Encoding is slow, multi-pass and done once in the cloud with the best software encoders; decoding happens in the viewer's hardware, which is why codec rollouts follow device support (AV1 streams are served only to devices that decode AV1 in hardware).

A video call optimises latency and robustness on unpredictable networks. Each sender encodes in real time, usually in hardware on a laptop or phone, at a bitrate set second by second by congestion control. Group calls add simulcast (the sender encodes two or three resolutions and a server forwards to each receiver the one it can take) or scalable coding (SVC: one stream with layers that can be dropped; AV1 and VP9 support temporal and spatial layers natively), so that one participant on a poor link does not degrade everyone else. When bandwidth collapses, the encoder lowers resolution and frame rate before it lets quantisation make the picture unwatchable, and audio always has priority over video.

Why it matters

Remote viewing of vehicle cameras, teleoperation and fleet live video combine both worlds: the latency needs of a video call with the variable cellular uplink of a moving vehicle. The tools are the video-call ones (WebRTC or SRT, congestion-controlled bitrate, intra refresh, simulcast or layers), and the design question is how to degrade gracefully when the uplink drops from 10 Mbit/s to 1.

8Measuring video quality

"Is this encode good enough?" has to be answered thousands of times per codec decision, so it cannot always be answered by people looking at screens. Objective metrics stand in for viewers; each one approximates human judgement in a different way and fails in its own characteristic places.

8.1PSNR: simple, honest and blind to perception

Peak signal-to-noise ratio compares each pixel of the decoded frame with the original: \( \mathrm{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{255^2}{\mathrm{MSE}} \) for 8-bit video, where MSE is the mean squared error. It is usually computed on luma (PSNR-Y) per frame and averaged. Rules of thumb for 8-bit content: below 30 dB artefacts are obvious, 35–40 dB is acceptable to good streaming quality, above 42–45 dB differences are hard to see. Each 6 dB halves the RMS error. PSNR is easy to compute, exactly reproducible and directly connected to what encoders minimise, which is why codec research still reports it. Its weakness is that it treats every pixel error alike: a small shift of a texture, invisible to a viewer, gives a large MSE, while blocking in a smooth sky, very visible, gives a small one. It also cannot compare across content: 38 dB on a noisy night scene and 38 dB on a cartoon mean different things.

Pitfall

Averaging PSNR in decibels over frames hides bad frames: one frame at 25 dB among 99 at 40 dB barely moves the mean. Report the minimum or a low percentile next to the mean, as Figure 5.4 does, and note whether you averaged dB values or MSE values first (they differ by up to a decibel on variable content).

8.2SSIM and MS-SSIM: structure instead of pixels

The structural similarity index (Wang, Bovik and others, 2004) compares local windows of the two images in terms of three things: mean brightness, contrast (standard deviation) and structure (correlation after removing mean and contrast). It returns 1 for identical images and drops when edges blur, texture disappears or blocks appear. Because it is computed per window and normalised by local contrast, it penalises blur in a flat area and changes of structure more than plain noise. Multi-scale SSIM (MS-SSIM) repeats the computation at several resolutions, which better matches viewing at different distances. On the test sequence SSIM-Y ranged from 0.90 (H.264 at CRF 38) to 0.97 (CRF 18); like PSNR it saturates near the top, so differences of 0.005 can matter, and people often report it as dB, \( -10 \log_{10}(1 - \mathrm{SSIM}) \).

8.3VMAF: a learned fusion of features

Netflix's Video Multimethod Assessment Fusion (VMAF, 2016) is now the de facto industry metric for streaming. It computes several elementary features (visual information fidelity at four scales, a detail-loss measure, and a simple temporal-difference feature) and fuses them with a support-vector regressor trained on subjective scores from viewing tests, giving a score from 0 to 100. Models exist for typical viewing conditions: the default for 1080p on a TV at three screen heights, a 4K model, and a phone model. A VMAF difference of about 6 points is often quoted as one just-noticeable difference. Because it is trained on how people rate typical compression artefacts, it tracks viewers far better than PSNR across content. It also has known weak spots: it was trained on compression and scaling, not on other distortions; it can be inflated by sharpening or contrast enhancement (hence the "NEG" variant, no enhancement gain, used for codec comparisons); and it says nothing about what a machine-vision algorithm needs.

The VMAF library could not be run in this chapter's container (the FFmpeg build there has no libvmaf, and downloading binaries was not possible), so the measured results use PSNR and SSIM. With an FFmpeg built against libvmaf the measurement is one filter: [dist][ref]libvmaf=model=version=vmaf_v0.6.1, with the distorted input first.

8.4BD-rate: one number per codec comparison

Comparing two codecs by eye on two curves like those of Figure 4.1 is fine; comparing hundreds of encoder changes needs one number. The Bjøntegaard delta rate (BD-rate, VCEG-M33, 2001) fits a smooth curve through each codec's four or five rate-quality points, with bitrate on a log scale, and integrates the horizontal gap between the curves over their common quality range. The result is "codec B needs x % less bitrate than codec A for the same quality, on average over this range". It is how every standardisation meeting decides whether a tool goes in, and the "−41 %" and "−71 %" figures in Section 4.3 are a simpler two-point version of it. BD-rate can be computed with PSNR, SSIM or VMAF as the quality axis, and the answers differ, which is why papers report several.

8.5Subjective testing: the ground truth

All objective metrics are trained or validated against subjective tests, in which a panel of viewers rates clips under controlled conditions (display, distance, lighting). ITU-R BT.500 and ITU-T P.910 define the methods: absolute category rating (each clip scored on a five-point scale, averaged into a mean opinion score, MOS), double-stimulus methods where viewers compare reference and test, and pair comparisons for small differences. A careful test needs 15 or more screened viewers, randomised order, training clips and outlier rejection, which is why it is done for codec decisions and metric training rather than for every encode. For automotive displays such as a CMS, the equivalent is not a MOS but a requirement-based test of resolution, contrast, latency and artefacts under defined conditions (Standards and regulations 6.3).

Talk track

"PSNR is pixel error in decibels: reproducible, but blind to perception. SSIM looks at local structure, so it catches blur and blocking better. VMAF is a learned fusion of features trained on subjective scores, which is why streaming services optimise for it. For codec comparisons we report BD-rate over a range of qualities. And if the consumer is a neural network, none of them is the right metric: we measure the task metric on compressed data."

9Compression and machine perception

Codecs were designed and tuned for human viewers. More and more video is consumed by algorithms instead: object detectors on recorded fleet data, visual odometry on streamed frames, analytics on surveillance feeds. What a viewer does not notice can matter a great deal to an algorithm, and the reverse.

9.1What artefacts do to features and networks

Compression artefacts have characteristic shapes. Blocking adds straight horizontal and vertical edges on the block grid. Ringing (mosquito noise) adds ripples next to strong edges. Blur removes fine texture and small, low-contrast objects, because their high-frequency coefficients are the first to be quantised to zero. Temporal artefacts (texture that freezes and then jumps at a key frame, flicker between frames with different QP, smearing along motion vectors after a loss) change from frame to frame. To a detector or feature extractor each of these is either a false structure (blocks, ringing) or missing structure (blur).

A classic study by Dodge and Karam (2016) found image classification networks fairly robust to JPEG compression and much more sensitive to blur and noise; later work on detection and segmentation found the same pattern with an important nuance: small and distant objects, thin structures (lane markings, poles, cables) and low-contrast objects degrade first, well before the overall picture looks bad. That is exactly the content automotive perception cares about at long range.

This chapter's experiment used a classical feature pipeline rather than a neural network, because it can be run without trained models: ORB keypoints were detected and matched between frames 60 and 61 of the test sequence, after H.264 encoding at CRF 18 to 43, and each match was checked against the motion measured on the lossless frames. The keypoint count hardly changed (2532–2656), because the detector happily fires on block edges and ringing. The number of matches that agree with the true motion fell from 1483 at CRF 18 (3.3 Mbit/s) to 1212 at CRF 33 (0.69 Mbit/s), only 18 % fewer for 80 % fewer bits, and then dropped steeply to 456 at CRF 43 (0.30 Mbit/s). PSNR went from 42.6 to 35.0 to 29.1 dB over the same range: a smooth decline that does not show where the cliff in the task is.

Pitfall

Training a network on high-quality images and running it on compressed video (or the reverse: training on compressed fleet recordings and deploying on the raw ISP output in the car) is a domain shift. Either keep the training data in the same form as the deployment input, or include compression as an augmentation (random JPEG or video encoding at the expected quality range) so the network learns to ignore the artefacts (Training neural networks 5.1).

9.2Noise is expensive: denoise before you encode

Sensor noise is random, so a codec can neither predict it from the previous frame nor compress it well; at high quality settings it spends most of its bits reproducing it. The test frames were rendered with Gaussian sensor noise of standard deviation 0, 2 and 5 (in 8-bit levels) and encoded with x264. At CRF 23, where the encoder's quantisation already smooths most noise away, the bitrate rose only from 1.70 to 1.93 Mbit/s. At CRF 18 it rose from 2.8 Mbit/s without noise to 3.4 Mbit/s at σ = 2 and 17.9 Mbit/s at σ = 5: more than six times as many bits, almost all of them spent on noise. A temporal denoiser in front of the encoder (FFmpeg's hqdn3d) brought the σ = 5 stream back to 6.4 Mbit/s at CRF 18 and 1.77 Mbit/s at CRF 23.

This is why camera ISPs run temporal noise reduction before the encoder (ISP engineering 5.3) and why low-light footage from security cameras is so much larger than daylight footage at the same settings. For perception it is a double-edged tool: denoising saves bits and removes noise the network would otherwise have to ignore, but aggressive temporal filtering also smears moving objects, which is exactly where a detector needs detail.

9.3Coding for machines

If the consumer is an algorithm, the encoder should spend bits where the algorithm needs them. The simplest form is region-of-interest (ROI) coding: lower QP inside regions that matter (detected vehicles and pedestrians, the road ahead, a licence plate) and higher QP elsewhere. Hardware encoders accept per-block QP maps for this, and FFmpeg passes ROI side data to x264. In this chapter's experiment, a ROI around the lead car with a QP offset of about −7 and +1.5 elsewhere raised the car region from 33.0 to 41.6 dB at CRF 30, for a 5 % higher bitrate and a 1.7 dB loss outside the region (Figure 9.1). The car region also shows why ROI coding is needed at all: the encoder's psycho-visual tuning had given the high-contrast, uniformly coloured car fewer bits than the textured background, because people do not notice errors there; a detector does.

Standardisation is following. MPEG's Video Coding for Machines (VCM) work develops pixel-domain coding with task-aware tools, and Feature Coding for Machines (FCM) compresses the intermediate features of a neural network instead of pixels, for "split inference" where the first layers run on the device and the rest in the cloud; both were still in development at the end of 2025. JPEG AI (ISO/IEC 6048-1, published 2025) is the first international standard for a learned image codec: a neural encoder produces a compact latent representation that a neural decoder turns back into an image, and the latent can also be fed to vision networks directly. Learned codecs reach better rate-distortion than classical ones on many images, but need neural accelerators on both ends and are not yet in vehicles.

Why it matters

A fleet that uploads clips for training is paying for every bit twice: on the cellular link and in storage for years. Coding for the task (ROI, the right resolution, the right quality range validated on the task metric) decides how many useful clips the same budget buys, and a validation that only reports PSNR or VMAF answers the wrong question.

10Automotive and industrial uses

In a vehicle, video leaves the closed camera-to-ECU path in a few typical ways, each with its own budget for bits, latency and quality. Knowing which path you are designing for settles most of the codec and transport decisions before any experiment.

10.1Development data logging

Perception development needs recorded camera data in a form that can be replayed into the software exactly as the car saw it. That pushes towards raw or lossless recording: raw sensor data (before the ISP) lets the team change ISP tuning later and re-run everything, and lossless storage avoids training on compression artefacts that the car never sees. The cost is the 12.7 TB per hour of the example vehicle, recorded on NVMe arrays or removable data cartridges in a logger in the trunk and shipped to the data centre physically, because no network can upload it. All streams carry hardware time stamps from a common clock (PTP, Calibration and fusion 4.4) so that cameras, lidar, radar and vehicle bus data can be aligned to within a millisecond. Where storage is the bottleneck, teams use visually lossless compression (high-quality H.265 or JPEG XL, or vendor near-lossless schemes for raw data) and verify on the perception metrics that the difference does not matter.

10.2Fleet upload and triggered clips

Series vehicles cannot log everything, so they keep the last few seconds or minutes of video in an encoded ring buffer in RAM or flash and upload a clip only when something interesting happens: a hard braking, a driver takeover, a perception disagreement, a rare object or a request from the development team's data engine (Deep learning and vision 15.1; the backend side is in Software architecture 14.3). The clip is encoded with the SoC's hardware encoder at a quality chosen for the perception tasks it will train (Section 9), typically H.265 at a few Mbit/s per camera, so a 20-second event across several cameras is tens of megabytes. Privacy rules (the GDPR in Europe) usually require faces and licence plates to be blurred before or right after upload, and the data must be encrypted in transit and at rest.

10.3Remote viewing and teleoperation

Remote assistance for automated vehicles, teleoperated driving in yards and mines, and "show me my car" features stream live video over a cellular uplink. The requirements are the video-call requirements, harder: latency well under 200 ms glass to glass for an operator to react, an uplink whose capacity changes from tens of Mbit/s to almost nothing within seconds, and several cameras competing for it. The usual design is a hardware encoder per camera in low-latency mode (no B frames, intra refresh, packet-sized slices), WebRTC or SRT transport with congestion control that adjusts each camera's bitrate, priority for the camera that matters most for the current manoeuvre, and a clear degraded mode (lower resolution, lower frame rate, then a stop) when the link cannot carry the minimum.

10.4Camera monitor systems and surround view

A camera monitor system that replaces a mirror (UN R46 with ISO 16505; see Truck perception 4.3) has the strictest latency budget of all, commonly quoted as at most 200 ms end to end with good systems far below, and must never show a frozen picture as if it were live. Most CMS and surround-view systems therefore do not compress at all: the camera sends uncompressed video over a SerDes link (GMSL, FPD-Link) straight to the ECU that drives the display, and the only processing is ISP, geometric correction and rendering. Where video has to cross an Ethernet backbone to a display ECU, light compression is used: intra-only or low-delay codecs with sub-frame latency (slice- or line-based, such as JPEG XS, a lightweight wavelet codec designed for exactly this), never long-GOP codecs with look-ahead.

10.5Industrial and surveillance cameras

The same principles run the world's IP cameras: H.264 or H.265 from a hardware encoder in the camera SoC, RTSP/RTP to a network video recorder, ONVIF for discovery and control, continuous recording in MPEG-TS or fragmented MP4. Their characteristic tricks are "smart codec" modes that take the static scene into account: very long GOPs with a background reference, dynamic GOP length that stretches when nothing moves, ROI coding on detected motion, and frame-rate reduction at night. They also show the noise effect of Section 9.2 at scale: storage planning for a camera site is dominated by night-time bitrates.

Talk track

"Video leaves the vehicle on three very different budgets. Development logging is raw or lossless, terabytes per hour, shipped on disks. Fleet clips are triggered, encoded with the SoC's H.265 encoder at a quality validated on the perception task, blurred for privacy and uploaded over cellular. Live remote viewing is a video-call problem on a worse network: low-latency hardware encoding, intra refresh, WebRTC or SRT with congestion control. And the digital mirror doesn't compress at all, because 200 ms is the whole budget."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

Why does video need to be compressed, and how much?Basic

Raw video is hundreds of megabits per second (332 Mbit/s for 720p30 YUV 4:2:0, 3 Gbit/s for an 8 MP RAW12 camera), while networks, radio links and storage budgets are one to three orders of magnitude smaller. Codecs reach 100:1 to 1000:1 by removing spatial, temporal and statistical redundancy and by discarding detail the viewer will not notice. Lossless coding only reaches 2–4:1 on camera data because sensor noise is unpredictable.

Walk me through how JPEG compresses an image.Basic

Convert RGB to YCbCr and subsample the chroma (4:2:0). Split each channel into 8 × 8 blocks, level-shift and apply the DCT, which concentrates energy into a few low-frequency coefficients. Divide by a quantisation table and round: this is where loss happens, and most high-frequency coefficients become zero. Read in zigzag order, code DC as a difference, code AC as run-length pairs, and Huffman-code the symbols. Decoding reverses the steps.

What are I, P and B frames, and what is a GOP?Basic

I frames are coded without reference to other frames; IDR frames are I frames that also reset the reference list, so decoding can start there. P frames predict from earlier frames; B frames can predict from earlier and later frames and average two predictions. A GOP is the sequence from one key frame to the next. Long GOPs save bits; short GOPs allow fast joining and seeking and limit error propagation. B frames force decode order to differ from display order and add latency.

Explain motion estimation and motion compensation.Basic

Motion estimation is the encoder searching a reference frame for the block that best matches the current block, giving a motion vector (with sub-pixel precision). Motion compensation is building the prediction by copying the referenced block shifted by the vector; the decoder does this too. Only the vector (predicted from neighbours) and the residual are coded. Estimation is the most expensive part of encoding; compensation is cheap.

Why does an encoder contain a decoder?Intermediate

Predictions must be made from exactly the frames the decoder will have, which are the reconstructed, quantised frames, not the originals. If the encoder predicted from originals, the decoder's predictions would differ slightly and the error would accumulate frame after frame (drift). So the encoder dequantises, inverse-transforms, adds the prediction and runs the in-loop filters, and uses those reconstructions as references. That is also why decoders are specified bit-exactly.

How do H.264, H.265, AV1 and VVC differ?Intermediate

Same hybrid architecture, more flexible tools each generation: larger and more varied block partitions (16 × 16 macroblocks to 64 × 64 and 128 × 128 trees), more intra directions, more transform sizes and types, better motion vector prediction and interpolation, more in-loop filters (SAO, CDEF, ALF), better entropy coding. Each saves roughly 30–50 % over its predecessor at equal quality, at several times the encoder complexity. H.264 is universal; H.265 is in most cameras and SoCs but has messy licensing; AV1 is royalty-free and widely decoded, with encoders arriving in embedded hardware; VVC has little hardware so far.

What is CRF, and when would you use CBR instead?Intermediate

CRF (constant rate factor) targets constant perceived quality and lets the bitrate follow the content; it is right for files and archives. CBR keeps the bitrate constant over a short window defined by a buffer (VBV/HRD), which a fixed-capacity or low-latency channel needs. Capped CRF combines both: constant quality unless it would exceed the channel. In my test, CRF frames ranged from 4 to 33 kB; CBR with a 67 ms buffer kept them near 6.3 kB at the cost of quality dips at key frames.

Where does latency come from in a video streaming system, and how do you reduce it?Intermediate

Capture and readout, ISP, encoder look-ahead and B-frame reordering, encoding time, transmission of large frames through the rate-control buffer, network propagation and queueing, the receiver's jitter buffer, decoding, and display buffering. Reduce it with a zero-latency encoder configuration (no B frames, no look-ahead, sliced threads; x264's default 1.39 s became 7 ms), small rate-control buffers with intra refresh instead of large I frames, sub-frame processing where possible, a jitter buffer sized to the measured jitter, and a display path without extra buffering.

What is intra refresh and why is it used?Intermediate

Instead of a periodic I frame, a band of intra-coded blocks sweeps across the picture over N frames, so the whole picture is refreshed once per period while every frame stays about the same size. It removes the key-frame burst that forces large buffers (worst-case buffer delay of one frame instead of about 150 ms in the leaky-bucket model) and repairs loss damage progressively. The cost is that a receiver joining mid-stream needs a full period before the picture is clean, and there is no single random-access frame for seeking.

How does H.264 travel over RTP?Intermediate

Per RFC 6184: small NAL units go one per packet, several tiny ones (SPS, PPS) can be aggregated with STAP-A, and NAL units larger than the MTU are split into FU-A fragments with start and end bits. All packets of a frame share one 90 kHz timestamp and the last has the marker bit set. The sequence number detects loss. SPS/PPS go in the SDP or are repeated in band before each key frame. In the capture in this chapter, the first key frame was 22 packets: parameter sets, an SEI and four IDR slices fragmented into 1200-byte FU-As.

What does a jitter buffer do and how would you size it?Intermediate

It holds arriving packets for a delay, reorders them and releases each frame at its send time plus that delay, turning variable arrival into steady playout. Packets later than the delay count as lost. Size it to cover the delay variation of almost all packets (a high percentile of measured jitter), plus a retransmission round trip if NACK is used: 10–30 ms on a LAN, 50–200 ms on the internet, adaptive on cellular links. Too small gives losses; too large gives latency.

Compare WebRTC, SRT and HLS for live video.Intermediate

WebRTC: RTP over UDP with NAT traversal, mandatory encryption, NACK/FEC/PLI and congestion-controlled bitrate; 0.15–0.5 s; for calls, browser viewing, teleoperation. SRT: carries MPEG-TS over UDP with retransmission inside a configured latency window and AES encryption; 0.2–2 s; for contribution links and vehicle-to-cloud streams. HLS/DASH: segments over HTTP with a bitrate ladder chosen by the player; scales through CDNs; 2–5 s in low-latency mode, 10–30 s classic.

What is the difference between a codec and a container?Basic

The codec defines the compressed frames (H.264, AV1). The container defines the file or stream around them: tracks, codec configuration, time stamps, index, metadata (MP4, MKV, MPEG-TS). The same H.264 stream can be remuxed between containers without re-encoding. Container choice affects seeking, streaming and crash safety: plain MP4 writes its index at the end and is unplayable after power loss; fragmented MP4, MKV and TS survive it.

What are PSNR, SSIM and VMAF, and when is each misleading?Intermediate

PSNR is 10·log10(peak² / MSE): reproducible but blind to perception and to content differences. SSIM compares local mean, contrast and structure; better for blur and blocking, saturates near 1. VMAF fuses several features with a regressor trained on subjective scores; the industry metric for streaming, but it can be gamed by sharpening (hence VMAF NEG) and knows nothing about machine perception. All three are misleading when the consumer is an algorithm; then measure the task metric.

What is BD-rate?Intermediate

The Bjøntegaard delta rate: fit curves through each codec's rate-quality points (log bitrate), and average the horizontal gap between the curves over their common quality range. It gives one number, "x % less bitrate for the same quality", and is the standard way to compare codecs or encoder changes. The result depends on the quality metric and range used.

Why are hardware encoders used, and what do you give up?Basic

They encode several high-resolution streams at a fraction of a watt without loading the CPU or GPU, which embedded systems need. You give up some compression efficiency (smaller searches, limited B frames and look-ahead, typically 20–40 % worse than a slow software encoder) and flexibility (only the driver's knobs). For live, low-latency use the gap is small because software encoders in low-latency mode give up the same tools.

How does a V4L2 memory-to-memory encoder work?Advanced

It is a /dev/video node with two queues named from the application's view: raw frames are queued on OUTPUT, bitstream buffers are dequeued from CAPTURE. The application sets both formats, requests buffers (DMABUF on OUTPUT for zero copy), sets controls (bitrate, GOP, profile, force key frame), starts streaming and then loops on QBUF/DQBUF with poll. Stateful drivers manage references in firmware; stateless ones use the request API and leave bitstream parsing and reference management to user space.

How do you keep a camera-to-encoder pipeline zero-copy?Advanced

Allocate frames once as dma-bufs (from the camera driver or a shared allocator), and pass the file descriptors to the encoder, GPU and NPU instead of copying pixels. Make sure all consumers accept the same format, stride and alignment (NV12 with the encoder's required alignment), or a framework will insert a conversion that copies. In GStreamer check that the negotiated caps carry DMABuf or vendor memory and that no videoconvert sits in the path; measure CPU and DRAM bandwidth per added camera.

How do compression artefacts affect neural networks?Advanced

Blocking and ringing add false structure; blur removes small and low-contrast objects and thin structures first; temporal artefacts change between frames. Classification is fairly robust to moderate compression; detection of small and distant objects and of thin structures degrades earlier. In this chapter's feature-matching experiment, valid matches fell only 18 % for an 80 % bitrate cut, then 73 % at very low rates: a cliff that PSNR does not show. Mitigations: train with matching compression or compression augmentation, choose quality by the task metric, and use ROI coding.

What does "coding for machines" mean?Advanced

Compressing video for algorithms rather than viewers: allocating bits by task relevance (ROI coding), choosing resolution and quality by task metrics, compressing neural-network features instead of pixels for split inference (MPEG FCM), task-aware pixel coding (MPEG VCM), and learned codecs whose latents can feed vision networks (JPEG AI, ISO/IEC 6048-1, 2025). In the chapter's ROI test, 5 % more bits raised the car region from 33.0 to 41.6 dB.

How would you design remote viewing of vehicle cameras over cellular?Advanced

Hardware encoder per camera in low-latency mode (no B frames, no look-ahead, intra refresh, slices sized to packets), WebRTC or SRT transport with encryption and congestion control that sets each encoder's bitrate, NACK for short round trips and key-frame requests otherwise, priorities between cameras, a degraded mode that lowers resolution and frame rate before quality collapses, and end-to-end latency monitoring with capture time stamps carried in SEI or RTP header extensions. Target well under 200 ms glass to glass.

Why do camera monitor systems usually not compress video?Intermediate

Their latency budget (commonly quoted as at most 200 ms end to end under ISO 16505) and their safety requirements leave no room for codec delay, buffering, loss concealment or frozen frames, and a dedicated SerDes link can carry uncompressed video at several Gbit/s. Where compression is needed over Ethernet, intra-only, sub-frame-latency codecs such as JPEG XS are used, not long-GOP codecs.

How would you record video in a test vehicle?Intermediate

For development replay, raw or lossless (raw sensor data lets ISP tuning change later), with hardware time stamps from a common PTP clock, onto NVMe arrays or removable cartridges, because terabytes per hour cannot be uploaded. If storage forces compression, use visually lossless settings and verify on perception metrics. Use crash-safe containers or short segments. For series vehicles, keep an encoded ring buffer and upload triggered clips with privacy blurring.

What are profiles and levels?Basic

A profile restricts which coding tools a stream uses (H.264 Baseline: no B frames, no CABAC; Main; High with the 8 × 8 transform; 10-bit and 4:2:2 profiles). A level caps the decoder workload: frame size, macroblock rate, bitrate and reference buffer (H.264 level 4.1 covers 1080p30 at up to 50 Mbit/s). Together they form the contract a decoder promises to support.

What is the difference between Annex B and AVCC formats?Intermediate

Annex B separates NAL units with start codes (00 00 01) and carries SPS/PPS in band; used in raw streams, MPEG-TS and by most hardware encoders. AVCC prefixes each NAL unit with its length and stores SPS/PPS once in the container header (avcC); used in MP4 and MKV. Feeding one to a decoder expecting the other usually fails silently. Convert with h264_mp4toannexb or h264parse.

Q.2Why and what if

Why is the DCT used instead of coding pixels directly?Why

Because it compacts the energy of natural image blocks into a few low-frequency coefficients, so after quantisation most coefficients are zero and the remaining ones can be coded cheaply. Quantising pixels directly would add visible noise everywhere; quantising high frequencies hides errors where the eye is least sensitive. The DCT itself is lossless; the gain comes from what it lets quantisation and entropy coding do.

Why does lossless compression of camera images reach only about 3:1?Why

Sensor noise is random and cannot be predicted from neighbours or earlier frames, so every bit of it must be stored. On the test frames with mild noise PNG reached 2.8:1 and FFV1 3.3:1. Lossy codecs reach 100:1 mainly by deciding not to keep the noise and fine detail.

Why do B frames improve compression?Why

They can predict from a future frame, which helps where content is uncovered (it exists in the next frame but not the previous one), and they can average two predictions, which cancels noise in the prediction. Because few or no frames reference them, they can also be quantised more coarsely. In the measurement B frames averaged 5.0 kB against 11.2 kB for P frames.

Why does the bitrate roughly halve every 5–6 QP?Why

The quantiser step doubles every 6 QP, so each coefficient needs about one bit less and many more coefficients become zero. Bits do not scale exactly with the step because side information (modes, vectors) does not shrink and the number of zeros grows non-linearly, but the rule holds within a factor of about two: CRF 18 to 23 cut x264's bitrate from 3.3 to 1.7 Mbit/s.

What if the encoder predicted from the original frames instead of reconstructed ones?What if

The decoder only has reconstructed frames, so its predictions would differ from the encoder's by the quantisation error of the reference. Each frame would add its own error on top of the previous one's: drift, visible as growing smear or noise until the next I frame. Open-loop schemes exist only where drift is bounded, for example with very short GOPs.

What if a stream has I frames ten times the size of P frames and the link is exactly the average bitrate?What if

Each I frame takes roughly ten frame times to transmit, so every frame behind it queues; at 30 fps that is about 300 ms of extra delay, and the receiver must buffer as much to play smoothly. Either the buffer (and latency) grows, or rate control caps the I frame and quality dips every GOP, or intra refresh spreads the cost so every frame is the same size.

What if the receiver joins a live H.264 stream between key frames?What if

It cannot decode P and B frames without their references, so it shows nothing (or garbage) until the next IDR, and only if it has the SPS and PPS by then. With intra refresh it gets a clean picture after one refresh period. Short GOPs, repeated parameter sets and, for interactive systems, a key-frame request (PLI/FIR) on joining fix it.

What if 1 % of packets are lost and the GOP is 2 seconds?What if

With about six packets per frame, a frame is damaged every 17 frames or so, but repairs come only every 60 frames, so the picture is almost always damaged; the measurement with a 60-frame GOP showed 166 of 180 frames degraded. Reduce the loss (NACK, FEC), shorten the refresh period (intra refresh over 15 frames: 72 degraded frames), and ask for key frames on loss.

Why can hardware encoders be worse than x264 at the same codec?Why

Compression efficiency comes from the encoder's search and decisions, not from the standard: a fixed-function block evaluates fewer partitions, modes and motion candidates, with less look-ahead and simpler rate-distortion decisions, to meet its power and throughput budget. The decoder is identical, so the bitstream is valid, just larger for the same quality.

What if the network's PSNR is fine but detection accuracy drops on compressed data?What if

That is expected: PSNR averages over all pixels while detection depends on small, distant or thin objects that lose their high frequencies first, and on not seeing false edges. Measure the task metric directly over the bitrate range, add ROI coding for relevant objects, raise quality or resolution where the cliff is, and train with the same compression.

Why does a noisy night scene need so many more bits?Why

Noise is new, unpredictable content in every frame, so neither intra nor inter prediction removes it, and at high quality the encoder reproduces it. At CRF 18 σ = 5 noise multiplied the bitrate by 6.4 in the experiment. At lower quality the quantiser discards most of it anyway, which is why the effect was only 14 % at CRF 23.

What if you put a temporal denoiser in front of the encoder for a perception stream?What if

You save bits (σ = 5 went from 17.9 to 6.4 Mbit/s at CRF 18) and remove noise the network must otherwise tolerate, but strong temporal filtering smears moving objects and fine detail, which a detector needs. Tune the strength on the task metric, use motion-adaptive filtering, and keep the same processing in training and deployment.

Why does RTP use UDP rather than TCP?Why

TCP retransmits every lost packet and delivers data strictly in order, so one loss stalls everything behind it for at least a round trip, and its congestion control halves the rate on loss. For live video a late packet is worthless; it is better to skip it, conceal and continue. RTP over UDP leaves loss handling to the application, which can choose selective retransmission within a deadline (NACK, SRT), FEC or concealment.

What if the jitter buffer is set to 200 ms on a LAN with 2 ms of jitter?What if

Almost 200 ms of pure added latency with no benefit, which is the default of GStreamer's rtpjitterbuffer and a classic reason why "the stream lags". Set it to a few times the measured jitter (10–30 ms) or use an adaptive buffer.

Why did AV1 beat H.264 by 71 % in this chapter when published gains are 30–50 %?Why

The synthetic sequence has smooth textures, clean geometry and mild noise, which favour large blocks and long-range prediction; SVT-AV1 used a deeper hierarchical prediction structure than x264's default; and the comparison is at one speed setting each. On real camera footage the gap narrows. Codec comparisons are always encoder, preset and content specific.

What if a recording loses power in the middle of a plain MP4 file?What if

The moov index at the end was never written, so players cannot find the frames; the data is there but the file is unplayable without a repair tool that rebuilds the index from the raw NAL units. Recorders should use fragmented MP4, MKV, MPEG-TS or short segments.
