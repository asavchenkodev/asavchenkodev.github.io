Automotive hardware, interfaces and communication

How bits physically travel through a car and why each kind of link exists: the electronic architecture from distributed ECUs to zonal controllers and a central computer; differential signalling, impedance and reflections; CAN with its arbitration, bit timing and fault confinement; CAN FD and CAN XL; LIN and FlexRay; automotive Ethernet from 10BASE-T1S to multi-gigabit links and the TSN mechanisms that make it deterministic; signals, PDUs, services and DDS; cables and connectors; signal integrity, EMC and grounding; the 12 V supply with its crank and load-dump transients, the ECU power path and thermal design; and finally topology, a worked bandwidth budget for a zonal car, latency chains and how networks fail.

1The big picture: a car is a distributed computer

A modern car contains between 70 and 150 electronic control units, three to five kilometres of copper wire and a dozen different kinds of data link, from a 20 kbit/s single wire that reports the position of a mirror to a 12 Gbit/s coaxial cable that carries raw video from an 8-megapixel camera. Each of these links exists because it solves one problem better than the others at an acceptable cost per node, in an environment that is hostile to electronics: −40 to +85 °C or more, a supply voltage that collapses when the engine starts and spikes when a battery cable comes loose, vibration for fifteen years, and a radio receiver a metre away that must not hear any of it. This chapter explains how the bits travel, why each bus looks the way it does, and how the pieces are combined into a vehicle architecture.

1.1Why a car needs a network at all

The first electronic control units in the 1970s and 1980s were islands: an engine controller, an anti-lock braking controller, each with its own sensors wired directly to it. As soon as functions needed each other's information, for example the gearbox needing engine torque or the ABS needing to reduce engine power, the obvious solution was another wire per signal between the two units. With a dozen units exchanging hundreds of signals, point-to-point wiring became heavy, expensive, unreliable at the connectors and impossible to change. The answer, introduced by Bosch with CAN in 1986 and in series production from 1991, was a shared bus: every unit connects to the same pair of wires, each value is sent once as part of a frame, and every unit that needs it simply listens.

Everything since follows the same logic of sharing and specialisation. A shared medium saves copper and connectors but must decide who may talk (arbitration or scheduling), must protect the data against noise (checksums, error handling), and must leave enough capacity for the worst case. Different functions need different trade-offs: a window lifter is happy with a slow and cheap link; a brake controller needs a guaranteed latency of a few milliseconds and fault confinement; a camera needs gigabits per second in one direction and nothing else. So the car ended up with a family of networks, joined by gateways, and the art of vehicle electronics is to put each function on the right one.

Why it matters

For a computer-vision or ADAS engineer the network is not somebody else's problem. Vehicle speed, yaw rate and gear arrive over it, with an age you must account for. Your detections leave over it, through a bandwidth budget someone else defends. Your camera is powered over its video cable, and a marginal link shows up as dropped frames only in a hot car. And every customer discussion about a new platform starts with "which interfaces does it have, and does it fit our E/E architecture?".

1.2The hardware in one page: ECU, MCU, SoC and the boot chain

The embedded-compute chapter explains the processors in depth; the short version is enough here. An ECU (electronic control unit) is a box with a connector, a power supply, one or more processors, memory, transceivers for its buses and drivers for its sensors and actuators. A small ECU, such as a door module, contains one microcontroller (MCU): a single chip with a Cortex-M or Cortex-R core, on-chip flash and SRAM, CAN and LIN controllers, timers and analog inputs, running bare-metal code or AUTOSAR Classic with deterministic timing (Embedded compute 5.1). A large ECU, such as an ADAS controller or a central computer, adds a system on chip (SoC) with application CPUs, a GPU, an NPU and an ISP, external LPDDR memory and flash storage, running Linux, QNX or a hypervisor (Embedded compute 2.1). Almost every such ECU still contains a safety MCU next to the SoC, because the MCU is available within milliseconds of power-on, owns the vehicle bus interfaces and can supervise the SoC (Embedded compute 5.2, Camera ECU 1.2).

Storage follows the same split: the MCU executes from internal NOR flash; the SoC boots from external NOR or eMMC/UFS flash, loads its images into DRAM and runs from there. The boot chain is a sequence of stages, each verifying the next with a signature before starting it: a boot ROM in the chip, a first-stage loader, often a secure monitor and hypervisor, then the operating systems. Its length explains why an MCU talks on CAN after 50 ms while a Linux application on the SoC is ready only after several seconds, and why the rear camera must be handled by an early path (Embedded compute 6.8, Linux 8.1, Camera ECU 8.2). For the network, three consequences matter: the MCU must answer network management and wake-up requests long before the SoC is up; the SoC's Ethernet switch and PHYs need their own early configuration; and an ECU that is "on" from the network's point of view may still be booting internally.

2E/E architecture: distributed, domain and zonal

The electrical and electronic architecture (E/E architecture) of a vehicle decides where computers sit, which functions run on which of them, how they are connected and how power reaches them. It is the most expensive decision in vehicle electronics to change, because it fixes the wiring harness, the supplier structure and the software organisation for a whole vehicle generation. The industry is in the middle of a transition from one ECU per function to a few central computers with zone controllers, and almost every topic in this chapter looks different depending on which side of that transition a project is on.

2.1Why the architecture keeps changing

Three pressures drive the change. The first is complexity: every new function was traditionally bought as a box from a supplier, with its own processor, software, wiring and diagnostic interface. By the late 2010s a premium car had over 100 ECUs from dozens of suppliers, and integrating them, testing their interactions and updating their software had become the bottleneck of vehicle development. The second is the wiring harness, which in such cars weighs 40 to 70 kg, contains more than 1500 wires and several thousand connection points, is assembled largely by hand, and is often quoted as the third heaviest and third most expensive component of the car after the body and the powertrain. The third is software: functions such as automated driving, over-the-air updates and features sold after the car is built need large computers, high bandwidth and a software platform that can be changed without changing hardware, which is what the industry calls the software-defined vehicle.

2.2Three generations, side by side

The three architectures are easiest to compare on the same car. Switch between them below and watch two things: where the computers are, and how far the wires have to run.

In the distributed architecture every function owns its ECU, its sensors and its actuators. Communication is by signals on buses grouped by function: a powertrain CAN, a chassis CAN or FlexRay, a body CAN with LIN sub-buses, an infotainment network. A central gateway connects the buses, routes the signals that cross between them and serves as the entry point for diagnostics. The strength of this design is independence: each function can be developed, validated and certified by its supplier in isolation. Its weakness is everything else: a hundred boxes, a hundred software stacks, a harness that grows with every feature, and a function that needs data from five domains must collect it through the gateway.

The domain architecture, common from about 2015, consolidates functions of one domain onto a powerful domain controller: an ADAS controller runs perception and planning, a cockpit controller runs the cluster and infotainment on one SoC with a hypervisor, a body controller runs lighting, access and comfort. The domain controllers talk to each other over an Ethernet backbone, usually through the central gateway, using services rather than only signals. The number of ECUs falls and software consolidates, but the wiring does not improve much: a radar in the rear bumper is still wired to the ADAS controller behind the glovebox, and a door module still to the body controller.

The zonal architecture separates the two concerns that the earlier designs tie together: where something is and what function it serves. A zone controller in each corner or region of the car connects all sensors, actuators and small ECUs near it, whatever their function, distributes power to them, and forwards their data over the Ethernet backbone. The functions themselves run as software in one or two central computers (high-performance computers, HPCs). The wires become short and local, the harness can be assembled more automatically, and new functions are mostly new software. Tesla's Model 3 (2017) was an early example of zone-like body controllers; most OEMs announced zonal architectures for platforms launching from the mid-2020s, usually as a gradual migration in which a zonal backbone coexists with a set of existing domain ECUs.

2.3What a zone controller actually does

A zone controller is less glamorous than the central computer but has more varied jobs. It is an I/O concentrator: it reads switches, analog sensors and simple digital sensors directly, drives motors, lamps and valves through high-side and low-side drivers, and talks to local small ECUs over CAN FD, LIN or 10BASE-T1S. It is a gateway: it packs the local signals into Ethernet messages towards the central computer, either as AUTOSAR PDUs, as services, or as tunnelled CAN frames (IEEE 1722), and unpacks commands in the other direction. It is an Ethernet switch with ports to the backbone and to local Ethernet devices such as radars. And it is a power distributor: instead of a passive fuse box, it switches and protects each load with electronic fuses that measure current, cut off faults in microseconds, report diagnostics and can switch loads off to save energy while parked (Section 12.5).

Many zone controllers also run some functions locally, which keeps reflexes short and survives a central computer that is rebooting: the brake lamp follows the brake pedal switch within the zone, a window stops on obstruction locally, an exterior light comes on even if the central computer is busy. Deciding which functions stay in the zone and which move to the centre is one of the main design questions of a zonal architecture, and safety requirements push towards keeping reflexes local.

Numbers

Published estimates for zonal architectures suggest a harness shorter by a few hundred metres to over a kilometre and lighter by several kilograms to around 20 kg, and an ECU count falling from about 100 to a few dozen. The exact numbers depend on how much is really moved; the direction is not disputed. The backbone between zones and centre typically runs at 1 Gbit/s today, with 2.5 to 10 Gbit/s planned as sensor data moves onto it.

2.4Trade-offs and the migration in practice

The zonal design moves complexity rather than removing it. The central computer becomes a single point of failure for many functions, so safety-relevant functions need redundancy: two central computers, or a central computer plus local fallbacks in the zones, plus two independent power paths. The network becomes the nervous system of the car, so its latency, bandwidth and fault behaviour must be engineered, not assumed; that is why TSN and careful budgets (Chapter 13) arrive together with zonal designs. The software organisation changes too: instead of buying a function in a box, an OEM integrates software from many suppliers on its own computer, which needs a common platform such as AUTOSAR Adaptive or an in-house middleware, a hypervisor and freedom from interference between functions of different ASILs.

In practice no car is purely one architecture. A typical 2025 platform has a central computer for ADAS and another for cockpit, zone controllers for body and comfort, some domain ECUs kept from the previous generation (brakes, steering, airbag, powertrain), all joined by an Ethernet backbone with CAN FD still dominating at the edges. Brake and steering controllers in particular stay separate for a long time: they are safety-critical, come with their actuators from specialised suppliers, and their certification is expensive to repeat.

Talk track

"Distributed architectures put one ECU per function, which gives independence but a hundred boxes and a heavy harness. Domain architectures consolidate functions per domain onto domain controllers with an Ethernet backbone, but the wiring still follows the function. Zonal architectures separate location from function: zone controllers connect and power everything nearby, the functions run as software in central computers, and the backbone becomes Ethernet with TSN. The price is that the network and the central computer must now be engineered for safety, latency and redundancy."

3How a bit travels on a wire

Before looking at any particular bus it helps to understand what all of them have in common at the physical level: a bit is a voltage change that propagates along a pair of conductors at about two thirds of the speed of light, with the electromagnetic field travelling between and around the wires. Three ideas explain most physical-layer design decisions in a car: differential signalling, the transmission line with its characteristic impedance, and the relation between propagation delay and bit time. They come back in every later chapter, from CAN's bus-length limit to the eye diagram of a camera link.

3.1Differential signalling: why almost every automotive link uses a pair

A single wire referenced to ground is the simplest way to send a bit, and LIN does exactly that. Its problem is that the receiver cannot tell the signal from anything else that changes the voltage between the wire and its own ground: interference picked up along the way, and the difference between the sender's ground and the receiver's ground, which in a car can be a volt or more (Section 11.5). A differential link uses two wires driven in opposite directions and a receiver that only looks at the difference between them. Interference that couples into a tightly twisted pair couples almost equally into both wires, shifting both by the same amount: it appears as a common-mode voltage and cancels in the difference. Conversely, the fields of the two opposite currents cancel each other at a distance, so a twisted pair also emits far less than a single wire. Both properties matter in a car: immunity because ignition, motors and radio transmitters are close, and low emissions because the car's own radio receivers must not hear the network (Chapter 11).

The cancellation is only as good as the balance of the pair. If one wire is longer, closer to the car body or loaded by a different capacitance, part of the common-mode disturbance is converted into a differential signal that the receiver cannot remove. This mode conversion is why twist rate, cable symmetry, connector symmetry and the matching of the two receiver inputs are specified so tightly for automotive Ethernet, and why common-mode chokes sit in front of almost every transceiver.

3.2Transmission lines, impedance and reflections

At low frequencies a wire is just a connection: the voltage is the same everywhere along it. That stops being true once the time a signal edge takes to travel the wire is comparable to the edge's rise time. Signals travel at about 5 ns per metre in a typical cable. A CAN edge rises in tens of nanoseconds and the cable is tens of metres long, so the edge is already "on the way" along the wire; an Ethernet or SerDes symbol lasts a nanosecond or less, so several symbols are on the cable at once. The wire is then a transmission line: a structure with distributed inductance and capacitance whose ratio defines a characteristic impedance \(Z_0\), about 100–120 Ω for twisted pairs and 50 Ω for automotive coax. A wave travelling along the line sees \(Z_0\) as a resistance, whatever the length.

When the wave reaches the end of the line, or any point where the impedance changes, part of it is reflected. The reflection coefficient is \(\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)\): a load equal to \(Z_0\) absorbs everything (\(\Gamma=0\)); an open end reflects everything with the same sign (\(\Gamma=+1\)) and a short with the opposite sign (\(\Gamma=-1\)). Reflected waves travel back, reflect again at the source, and add to the next bits. This is why buses are terminated: CAN with 120 Ω at both physical ends of the bus, Ethernet and SerDes links inside the PHYs at both ends of each point-to-point link.

Every connector, every branch (stub) and every change in cable type is a small impedance discontinuity, and reflections from all of them add up. This is why CAN limits stub lengths to a few tens of centimetres at 500 kbit/s and much less at CAN FD data rates, why star topologies are hard for CAN FD, and why automotive Ethernet channels are specified with a limited number of inline connectors and with return-loss limits for each component.

Pitfall

Putting the CAN termination in the wrong place. Two 120 Ω resistors belong at the two ends of the bus, not in two ECUs that happen to be convenient. A bench setup with a short cable works with almost any termination, which is why the problem appears only in the vehicle harness. Measuring between CAN_H and CAN_L with the power off should give about 60 Ω; 120 Ω means one termination is missing, 40 Ω means there is one too many.

3.3Bit time, propagation delay and line coding

Two numbers decide much of a link's design: the time one bit (or symbol) occupies, and the time a signal needs to cross the cable. If the bit time is much longer than the round trip, all nodes see the same bit at the same time; this is what CAN relies on to arbitrate bit by bit (Section 4.3), and it limits CAN to about 40 m at 1 Mbit/s. If the bit time is much shorter, the link must be point-to-point, terminated at both ends, and the receiver must recover timing from the data itself; this is the world of Ethernet and SerDes.

How bits are mapped to voltages is the line coding, and it is chosen to fit the channel. CAN uses plain NRZ (non-return-to-zero) and inserts stuff bits so that the receiver sees an edge at least every six bits to keep its clock aligned. LIN uses UART characters with start and stop bits. 100BASE-T1 and 1000BASE-T1 use three-level PAM3 symbols, so that each symbol carries 1.5 bits and the cable's bandwidth suffices for the bit rate; 10BASE-T1S uses differential Manchester coding with an edge in every bit; GMSL3 and multi-gigabit Ethernet use four-level PAM4. More levels per symbol mean a lower symbol rate and less loss in the cable, but a smaller voltage step between levels and therefore less noise margin. The eye diagram in Section 11.2 shows what that margin is.

Interesting detail

Automotive Ethernet PHYs send in both directions on the same single pair at the same time. Each receiver hears the remote transmitter plus its own transmitted signal and all its reflections, which can be far stronger than the wanted signal. A hybrid circuit and a digital echo canceller, which learns the reflection pattern of the channel during link training, subtract the known own signal (Section 7.2). This is the same technique telephone modems and gigabit Ethernet over four pairs use, and it is what allows one pair instead of two.

4CAN: the backbone of signals

Controller Area Network is the most widespread vehicle bus and, forty years after its invention, still the default for exchanging state signals between ECUs. Its design is a small masterpiece of trade-offs: two wires, no master, no addresses, a priority mechanism that resolves collisions without losing a single bit, and error handling strong enough that a faulty node removes itself from the bus. Understanding those few mechanisms explains both why CAN is so robust and why its speed is fundamentally limited.

4.1The bus: dominant, recessive and a wired-AND

All nodes connect to one twisted pair, CAN_H and CAN_L, terminated with 120 Ω at each end (Section 3.2). Each node has a CAN controller, the logic that builds and checks frames (usually inside the MCU), and a transceiver, a small chip that converts the controller's logic levels to bus voltages and back. A transceiver can do only two things to the bus: actively drive it to the dominant state, in which CAN_H is pulled up to about 3.5 V and CAN_L down to about 1.5 V, or let go of it, in which case the termination resistors pull both wires to the same voltage of about 2.5 V, the recessive state (Figure 3.1). Dominant represents a logical 0, recessive a logical 1.

The consequence is the key to CAN: if any node drives dominant, the bus is dominant, whatever the others do. The bus behaves as a wired-AND of all transmitted bits. A node that sends a recessive 1 and reads back a dominant 0 therefore knows immediately that another node is transmitting. Every CAN controller reads the bus while it writes, bit by bit, and the whole protocol is built on that feedback: arbitration, acknowledgement and error signalling all use it.

The physical layer for cars is high-speed CAN (ISO 11898-2), used up to 1 Mbit/s and in practice mostly at 500 kbit/s. The receiver's common-mode range of at least −2 to +7 V lets it tolerate ground offsets between ECUs. A fault-tolerant low-speed CAN (ISO 11898-3, up to 125 kbit/s) that keeps working with one wire broken or shorted was used for body networks and is now rare.

4.2The frame

CAN has no node addresses. A frame carries an identifier that names its content ("vehicle speed", "brake request"), and each node decides by the identifier whether to keep the frame; hardware acceptance filters in the controller do this without CPU load. A classic data frame contains up to 8 data bytes; the rest is protocol.

Reading the fields in order explains the protocol. SOF (start of frame) is a single dominant bit that synchronises all receivers to the start of the frame. The identifier of 11 bits (or 29 bits in the extended format, signalled by the IDE bit) is followed by RTR, which distinguishes data frames from the rarely used remote request frames; together they form the arbitration field. IDE and the reserved bit r0 are followed by the four-bit DLC (data length code, 0–8). Then come the data bytes, a 15-bit CRC over everything from SOF to the end of the data, and a recessive CRC delimiter. In the ACK slot the transmitter sends recessive and every node that received the frame correctly overwrites it with dominant: the sender learns that at least one node got the frame, though not which. An ACK delimiter, seven recessive EOF bits and three bits of intermission (IFS) close the frame.

Bit stuffing is what keeps the receivers' clocks aligned. CAN has no separate clock line, so receivers resynchronise on recessive-to-dominant edges. After five consecutive equal bits the transmitter inserts one complementary bit, which receivers remove. Stuffing also turns six equal bits into an error signal: a sequence that can never occur in a valid frame is used deliberately as the error flag (Section 4.5). The cost is a variable frame length: a frame with 8 data bytes has 111 bits without stuffing and up to 135 in the worst case, which timing analysis must assume.

Numbers

Classic frame, 11-bit identifier, \(n\) data bytes: \(8n + 47\) bits including intermission, plus up to \(\lfloor (34 + 8n - 1)/4 \rfloor\) stuff bits. For 8 bytes: 111 to 135 bits, 222 to 270 µs at 500 kbit/s. The payload is at best 64 of 111 bits, 58 % efficiency, and between about 3700 (worst-case stuffing) and 4500 such frames per second fit on a 500 kbit/s bus at 100 % load. With 29-bit identifiers a frame grows by 20 bits.

4.3Arbitration: the lowest identifier wins, and nobody loses a bit

Any node may start a frame when the bus has been idle. If two or more start in the same bit time, they all transmit their identifiers bit by bit, most significant bit first, and read back each bit. A node that sends recessive 1 and reads dominant 0 has lost: it stops transmitting immediately and becomes a receiver of the winner's frame, which continues undisturbed. Arbitration is non-destructive: the winning frame is not delayed by a single bit, so the bus is never wasted on collisions, unlike classic shared Ethernet. The identifier is a priority: designers assign low identifiers to urgent messages such as brake requests, and the worst-case latency of each message can be computed (Section 4.6). And arbitration requires that every node sees every bit within the same bit time, which is the physical reason for CAN's speed limit (Section 4.4).

Pitfall

Two ECUs sending the same identifier, for example a replacement part with a different software variant, or two instances of the same ECU type without configured identifiers. They pass arbitration together and collide in the data field, producing bit errors that both nodes count until one goes error-passive. The symptom is intermittent errors that appear only in certain vehicle variants. Identifier uniqueness is a property of the network database, and checking it is part of every network integration.

4.4Bit timing, the sample point and bus length

Each bit is divided into time quanta of the controller's clock and into segments: a one-quantum synchronisation segment in which an edge is expected, a propagation segment that absorbs the signal's round trip on the bus, and two phase segments around the sample point, where the bit's value is read. Receivers resynchronise on each recessive-to-dominant edge by stretching or shortening the phase segments by up to the synchronisation jump width, which absorbs oscillator tolerances of a few tenths of a percent.

During arbitration and acknowledgement a node's bit can be overwritten by a node at the far end of the bus, so the far node's response must reach it before the sample point: the propagation segment must cover twice the delay of cable and transceivers. That fixes the trade-off between bit rate and bus length. With about 5 ns per metre of cable and roughly 150–250 ns of transceiver loop delay per node, the rules of thumb are about 40 m at 1 Mbit/s, 100 m at 500 kbit/s and 500 m at 125 kbit/s. Cars are much shorter, but stubs, connectors and the requirement for margin make 500 kbit/s the most common choice for classic CAN.

Interesting detail

All nodes on a bus must use the same sample point, or very close to it, for robust operation. A node configured to sample at 70 % on a bus where everyone else samples at 87.5 % works on the bench and fails intermittently in the vehicle, especially when combined with long stubs and ringing. That is why OEMs and the CAN in Automation association publish recommended bit-timing settings, and why the bit timing of every ECU is checked during network integration.

4.5Error detection and fault confinement

CAN detects errors with five mechanisms. A bit error: a transmitter reads back a different value than it sent (outside arbitration and the ACK slot). A stuff error: a receiver sees six equal bits where stuffing should have prevented it. A CRC error: the received CRC does not match. A form error: a fixed-format bit such as a delimiter or EOF bit has the wrong value. An ACK error: the transmitter sees no dominant bit in the ACK slot. Together they make an undetected corruption extremely improbable: the classic analysis by Bosch gives a residual error probability below about 4.7 × 10−11 times the frame error rate.

A node that detects an error destroys the frame for everyone by sending an error flag of six dominant bits, which violates the stuffing rule and so is seen as an error by every other node, who then send their own flags. All nodes discard the frame, and the transmitter retransmits it automatically. This is how CAN guarantees consistency: either all nodes accept a frame or none does, which matters when several ECUs must act on the same command.

Retransmission would let a single faulty node destroy all traffic, so CAN adds fault confinement. Each node counts its transmit errors (TEC) and receive errors (REC) and changes its behaviour according to them.

The asymmetry in the counters is the clever part: a transmit error costs 8 points, a successful frame earns 1. A node with a broken transceiver or a wrong bit rate sees every one of its own frames fail, so its TEC climbs eight times faster than any healthy node's counter, and it reaches error-passive and bus-off long before it can take the rest of the bus down. Healthy nodes that saw the same disturbance recover with each good frame.

Pitfall

Fault confinement does not protect against a babbling idiot: a node whose software sends a valid high-priority frame continuously, or a transceiver stuck dominant. A valid frame produces no errors, so nobody's counters move, and a high-priority babbler starves everyone below it. Protection needs other measures: transceivers with a dominant time-out that release the bus after a few milliseconds, bus guardians, and in safety-relevant designs timeout monitoring of every expected message by the receivers.

Two lessons hide in this table. At low load every message gets through in a few milliseconds, and the lowest priority suffers most. At high load the response time grows non-linearly: DoorStatus jumps from 10 to 15 ms because its waiting time crosses the 10 ms period of the four fast messages, which then interfere a second time. That is why vehicle networks are designed for a maximum bus load of typically 50–70 % even though CAN would technically run at 100 %: the headroom keeps worst-case latencies short, leaves room for retransmissions after errors and for messages added later in the project.

Pitfall

The analysis assumes that each controller always offers its highest-priority pending frame to arbitration. A controller with a single FIFO transmit buffer, or software that queues frames in arrival order, causes priority inversion: a high-priority frame waits behind a low-priority one in the same node, and the computed response times are no longer valid. Check how the CAN driver uses the hardware mailboxes before trusting any timing analysis.

4.7From frames to signals: the network database

Applications never see frames. They see signals, such as a vehicle speed of 30.00 km/h, which are packed into frames according to a database shared by all ECUs on the bus: a DBC file in the classic CAN world, ARXML in AUTOSAR projects. For each frame the database gives the identifier, length, sender and period; for each signal its start bit, length, byte order (Intel little-endian or Motorola big-endian), scale, offset, unit and receivers. The same database generates the communication code in every ECU and configures test tools such as CANoe, which is why it is the single most important integration artefact of a vehicle network (Camera ECU 3.3 follows one signal through AUTOSAR).

4.8What classic CAN cannot do

Classic CAN's limits follow directly from its mechanisms. Its bandwidth is capped near 1 Mbit/s by bitwise arbitration over the whole bus length, and in practice at 500 kbit/s. Its payload of 8 bytes is too small for modern needs: an E2E header takes 2 bytes, a security MAC and freshness value for authenticated messages another 4 to 8, leaving almost nothing for data. Its efficiency is around 50 %. It has no security: any node can send any identifier, and receivers cannot tell who sent a frame. Software download at 500 kbit/s through a gateway takes tens of minutes for a few megabytes. CAN FD and CAN XL address the first three, cryptographic SecOC the fourth, and Ethernet takes over where bandwidth matters most.

Talk track

"CAN is a two-wire differential bus where dominant bits overwrite recessive ones. Frames carry an identifier instead of an address, and when several nodes start together they arbitrate bit by bit: the lowest identifier wins without losing a bit, so the identifier is the priority and worst-case latencies can be computed. Bit stuffing keeps receivers synchronised, five error checks plus error frames give consistent reception, and error counters make a faulty node switch itself off. The price is speed: arbitration needs every node to see each bit within one bit time, which limits CAN to about 1 Mbit/s."

5CAN FD and CAN XL: more bytes, faster bits

CAN FD (flexible data rate), specified by Bosch in 2012 and standardised in ISO 11898-1:2015, keeps everything that makes CAN valuable: the same wires, the same arbitration, the same error handling. It changes two things: a frame may carry up to 64 bytes, and the data part of the frame may be sent at a higher bit rate than the arbitration. CAN XL, standardised in 2024, pushes the same idea to 2048 bytes and 10–20 Mbit/s.

5.1Why the bit rate can switch in the middle of a frame

The insight behind CAN FD is that only some parts of a CAN frame need every node to see every bit within one bit time: arbitration, where several transmitters compete, and acknowledgement, where every receiver writes into the ACK slot. Between them, once arbitration is over, exactly one node is transmitting and everybody else only listens. During that part the signal only needs to travel from one transmitter to each receiver once, not make a round trip, and nothing limits the bit rate except the signal quality of the wires. So a CAN FD frame starts at the nominal rate, typically 500 kbit/s, switches to the data rate, typically 2 or 5 Mbit/s, at the sample point of the BRS (bit rate switch) bit, and switches back at the CRC delimiter, before the ACK.

How much the switch helps depends on the payload and the ratio of the two rates, because the slow part of the frame is a fixed overhead of about 30 bit times at the nominal rate. Try the combinations:

Numbers

8 bytes at 500 kbit/s: classic 222 µs, CAN FD with 2 Mbit/s data phase 108 µs, with 5 Mbit/s 79 µs. 64 bytes: eight classic frames 1776 µs, one FD frame 335 µs (2 Mbit/s), 170 µs (5 Mbit/s), 129 µs (8 Mbit/s). The payload throughput of a 500 kbit/s / 2 Mbit/s bus with 64-byte frames is about 1.5 Mbit/s, roughly five times classic CAN on the same wires.

5.2What else changed in the frame

Beyond the switch, the frame format changed in a few careful ways. A recessive FDF bit (FD format, where classic CAN has a reserved dominant bit) marks the frame as CAN FD; the remote-request bit becomes RRS and is always dominant, because CAN FD has no remote frames. The ESI bit (error state indicator) tells receivers whether the transmitter is error-passive, useful for diagnosing a sick node. The DLC keeps its 4 bits but codes 9–15 now mean 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 48 and 64 bytes. A stuff count field transmits the number of dynamic stuff bits modulo 8 with a parity bit, and the CRC grows to 17 bits for up to 16 bytes and 21 bits above, computed over the stuff bits as well. Both changes close a weakness of classic CAN, in which two bit errors that create or remove a stuff bit could shift the frame and escape the CRC. In the stuff-count and CRC fields, stuff bits are inserted at fixed positions, every fourth bit, instead of dynamically.

A classic CAN controller that sees a CAN FD frame on the bus interprets the recessive FDF bit as a form error and destroys the frame with an error flag. A mixed network therefore needs every node to be at least FD-tolerant; in practice, a bus is either converted entirely to CAN FD or kept classic, and a gateway translates between them. ISO 11898-1:2015 also corrected the original Bosch CAN FD proposal, so "ISO CAN FD" and "non-ISO CAN FD" controllers do not interoperate; all current parts are ISO.

5.3The physical limits at high data rates

At 2 Mbit/s a bit lasts 500 ns; at 5 Mbit/s, 200 ns; at 8 Mbit/s, 125 ns. The ringing caused by stubs and star points (Section 3.2), which takes some hundred nanoseconds to decay, now fills a significant part of the bit. Two techniques keep the data phase working. Transmitter delay compensation (TDC) handles the fact that the transmitter's own bit returns from its transceiver with a loop delay of 100–250 ns, comparable to the whole bit: the controller measures that delay and checks its own bits at a secondary sample point shifted by it. Signal improvement capability (SIC) transceivers, specified in CiA 601-4 and adopted into ISO 11898-2:2024, actively drive the recessive edge for a short time to absorb the reflections that ring after it, which makes 5 and 8 Mbit/s usable in real harnesses with stubs.

In practice most CAN FD networks in production run at 500 kbit/s nominal and 2 Mbit/s data, a growing number at 5 Mbit/s, and networks with SIC transceivers at up to 8 Mbit/s. The topology matters more than in classic CAN: linear buses with short stubs, few nodes per segment, and sometimes several smaller buses instead of one big one.

5.4CAN XL: CAN with Ethernet-sized frames

CAN XL (ISO 11898-1:2024, CiA 610) keeps CAN arbitration with an 11-bit priority identifier but redefines almost everything else. Payloads range from 1 to 2048 bytes, enough to carry a complete Ethernet frame. Data phases run at up to 10 Mbit/s with SIC transceivers and up to about 20 Mbit/s with CAN SIC XL transceivers that switch to a dedicated fast mode in the data phase. An 8-bit SDU type says what the payload is, for example a classic CAN or CAN FD frame, an IP packet or an Ethernet frame, so CAN XL can tunnel other protocols; an 8-bit virtual CAN network ID lets several logical networks share one physical bus; a separate 32-bit acceptance field carries addressing information; and a header CRC plus a 32-bit frame CRC protect the frame.

CAN XL competes with 10BASE-T1S (Section 7.3) for the edge of zonal architectures: the links between a zone controller and many small nodes. CAN XL offers CAN's priority arbitration, its robustness and a migration path for CAN-based software; 10BASE-T1S offers native Ethernet, the same protocol stack as the backbone and no gateway. Which one wins is open at the time of writing; several OEMs evaluate both, and existing CAN FD will remain for a long time.

Talk track

"CAN FD keeps CAN's arbitration and error handling but switches to a higher bit rate after arbitration, because only one node transmits during the data field and the round-trip constraint no longer applies. It switches back before the acknowledgement. With 64-byte frames and a 2 to 8 Mbit/s data phase it carries several times more payload on the same wires, which is what made E2E protection and authenticated messages practical on CAN. CAN XL extends the idea to 2048-byte frames and up to 20 Mbit/s and can tunnel Ethernet frames."

6LIN, FlexRay and the special-purpose links

Two more vehicle buses complete the classic picture. LIN exists because CAN is too expensive for a mirror motor or a switch panel; FlexRay exists because CAN could not guarantee the timing and redundancy that brake-by-wire and active suspension were expected to need. One is still everywhere, the other has largely been overtaken, and the reasons are instructive.

6.1LIN: the cheapest possible bus

The Local Interconnect Network (LIN, ISO 17987, developed by a consortium of carmakers around 1999) is designed for cost: one signal wire plus ground, 12 V levels, a node that needs only a UART and a simple transceiver, and a node clock that can come from an on-chip RC oscillator instead of a crystal, because every frame starts with a sync pattern that the node measures to adjust its baud rate. Rates are 2.4 to 20 kbit/s, most often 19.2 kbit/s; a bus has up to 16 nodes and 40 m of wire. LIN connects simple nodes to one ECU: window lifters, mirror motors, seat adjusters, switch panels, the rain and light sensor, ambient lighting, the battery sensor.

All the intelligence sits in the commander, usually the body or zone controller, and all timing is decided by its schedule. Responders, formerly called slaves, only answer. Diagnostics use reserved identifiers 0x3C and 0x3D, and responders can be configured at the end of the line or in the workshop through them, including assigning node addresses to identical parts such as the four window-lifter motors. LIN's weaknesses follow from its simplicity: it is slow, has no error handling beyond a checksum and a response-error bit, and depends entirely on the commander.

6.2FlexRay: determinism by time division

FlexRay (ISO 17458) was developed by a consortium of carmakers and suppliers from 2000, for a future of by-wire systems that needed more bandwidth than CAN, timing that does not depend on load, and redundancy. Its answer is time-division multiple access with a global time. All nodes synchronise their clocks to each other to within about a microsecond; communication runs in fixed cycles, typically 1–5 ms; and each cycle starts with a static segment of equal slots, each owned by one node, followed by a dynamic segment for event-driven frames.

A frame carries a 5-byte header with an 11-bit frame ID, the payload length in 16-bit words, a header CRC and the cycle count, up to 254 bytes of payload and a 24-bit CRC. Because each slot is owned, a bus guardian can stop a node that transmits outside its slot, the FlexRay answer to the babbling idiot. FlexRay went into series production in 2006 in BMW's adaptive damping and was later used for chassis and powertrain backbones by several premium carmakers.

FlexRay has largely stopped spreading. Its static schedule must be designed for the whole network in advance, so every change touches every node; the controllers and the tooling are more expensive than CAN's; and the problems it addressed are now solved differently. CAN FD gives enough bandwidth for most control traffic with proven, cheap hardware, and switched Ethernet with TSN gives far more bandwidth with time-aware scheduling where determinism is needed. Many existing vehicle platforms still contain FlexRay, so it is worth recognising, but new architectures rarely choose it.

Interesting detail

FlexRay's static schedule and TSN's time-aware shaper (Section 7.6) are the same idea in different clothes: a global time, a cyclic schedule, and windows reserved for traffic that must not wait. Engineers who designed FlexRay schedules recognise TSN gate control lists immediately.

6.3The special-purpose links

Several narrow links survive because they solve one problem cheaply. SENT (SAE J2716) sends sensor values from a single sensor, such as a pressure or position sensor, to an ECU as pulse lengths on one wire, without the cost of a bus. PSI5 connects airbag crash sensors to the airbag ECU over two wires that carry both power and Manchester-coded current pulses, with very fast and robust timing. A2B (automotive audio bus) carries digital audio, microphone signals for hands-free and noise cancellation, and control over one twisted pair in a daisy chain. MOST, a ring network for infotainment, often optical, was common in European premium cars in the 2000s and has been replaced by Ethernet.

Inside an ECU, chips talk over board-level links that never leave the box: SPI for fast MCU-to-SoC messages and external flash, I²C for configuring sensors, PMICs and serializers (the camera's control channel is I²C tunnelled through the SerDes back channel, Camera pipeline 8), UART for debug consoles, MIPI CSI-2 for camera data into the SoC and PCIe between SoCs, to NVMe storage or to an Ethernet switch chip. They are short, unterminated or terminated on the board, and outside the scope of vehicle network design, but they appear in every ECU block diagram.

7Automotive Ethernet and TSN

Ethernet entered the car through diagnostics and infotainment around 2008 and is now the backbone of every new E/E architecture. The reasons are bandwidth, which scales from 10 Mbit/s to 25 Gbit/s with one familiar protocol; switching, which lets many links run in parallel instead of sharing one bus; and the internet protocol stack, which brings services, software update, security protocols and a huge ecosystem of tools. What the car needed to add was a physical layer that survives the car's environment at low cost, and a set of standards that make a switched network deterministic enough for control traffic.

7.1Why ordinary Ethernet did not fit

Office Ethernet, 100BASE-TX, uses two twisted pairs, one per direction, in a cable with RJ45 connectors, and its signal spectrum and emissions were designed for buildings, not for a metal box full of radio receivers. Its cable is heavy, its connector does not survive vibration and moisture, and it fails automotive emission limits. 100BASE-TX is still used for the diagnostic connector (DoIP through the OBD port), where a workshop tester is plugged in, but inside the car the industry needed something else.

Broadcom's BroadR-Reach, standardised by IEEE in 2015 as 100BASE-T1 (IEEE 802.3bw), solved this with one unshielded twisted pair, full duplex on that pair, a narrower signal spectrum and automotive-grade connectors. The OPEN Alliance, an industry group founded in 2011, wrote the conformance, interoperability and EMC test specifications that made PHYs from different vendors interchangeable. A family followed: 1000BASE-T1 (802.3bp, 2016), 10BASE-T1S (802.3cg, 2019), 2.5/5/10GBASE-T1 (802.3ch, 2020) and 25GBASE-T1 (802.3cy, 2023). An asymmetric variant for cameras and displays, with multi-gigabit in one direction and a slow return channel, is being standardised as IEEE 802.3dm.

7.2One pair, both directions: 100BASE-T1 and 1000BASE-T1

100BASE-T1 sends 100 Mbit/s in each direction over a single pair up to 15 m long. To fit the cable's bandwidth it maps 3 bits onto 2 three-level symbols (PAM3), so the symbol rate is 66.7 MBd and most signal energy stays below about 33 MHz, well under what an unshielded pair can carry with acceptable emissions. Both PHYs transmit at the same time on the same pair; each receiver separates the remote signal from its own with a hybrid and an echo canceller.

1000BASE-T1 uses the same principle at 750 MBd PAM3 with a Reed-Solomon forward error correction, over 15 m of unshielded or 40 m of shielded pair. Multi-gigabit 2.5/5/10GBASE-T1 moves to PAM4 and shielded cable. Two automotive details differ from office Ethernet. The PHYs are configured, not negotiated: master or slave role and speed are fixed in hardware strapping or by software, which avoids negotiation time and ambiguity. And the PHYs report a signal quality indicator (SQI) and can run cable diagnostics by time-domain reflectometry, finding an open or short and roughly where it is, which is valuable in a workshop.

Numbers

100BASE-T1: 66.7 MBd PAM3, 15 m, unshielded. 1000BASE-T1: 750 MBd PAM3 with RS-FEC, 15 m unshielded or 40 m shielded. 2.5/5/10GBASE-T1: PAM4, shielded, 15 m. Sleep and wake-up over the data line are standardised by the OPEN Alliance (TC10), so an ECU can sleep with a few tens of microamperes and be woken by its link partner.

7.310BASE-T1S: Ethernet as a bus

Point-to-point Ethernet needs a switch port and a PHY per node, which is too expensive for small sensors and actuators. 10BASE-T1S brings Ethernet back to a shared medium: one unshielded pair, at least 25 m long, with eight or more nodes connected along it, at 10 Mbit/s half duplex with differential Manchester coding. Classic shared Ethernet resolved collisions by detecting them and backing off for a random time (CSMA/CD), which wastes capacity under load and gives no latency bound. 10BASE-T1S adds PLCA, physical layer collision avoidance: the nodes take turns.

A node with nothing to send costs only a short gap, so a cycle with eight mostly silent nodes is very short and the latency for a single urgent frame is small. With all nodes sending maximum-size frames the worst-case wait is one full cycle of all nodes, which can be computed. 10BASE-T1S makes "Ethernet to the edge" possible: a zone controller with one 10BASE-T1S port can talk to door, seat and light modules using the same IP-based protocols as the backbone, with no CAN gateway in between. Its competitor for this role is CAN XL (Section 5.4).

7.4The frame, the VLAN tag and the priority field

An automotive Ethernet frame is an ordinary IEEE 802.3 frame, and almost always carries an IEEE 802.1Q tag. The tag is what makes Ethernet usable for mixed-criticality traffic in a car: its 12-bit VLAN identifier separates traffic into virtual networks that switches keep apart, and its 3-bit priority code point (PCP) tells every switch which queue the frame belongs to.

The numbers matter for budgets. A 1500-byte payload occupies 1542 byte times on the wire: 123 µs at 100 Mbit/s and 12.3 µs at 1 Gbit/s. A small control message with 8 bytes of data is padded to the 42-byte minimum payload (with a tag), so it costs 84 byte times, and with IP, UDP and a SOME/IP header its efficiency is below 10 %. That is why signals are collected into larger PDUs before they go onto Ethernet (Section 8.1), and why a gateway that forwards each CAN frame as its own Ethernet frame wastes most of the backbone's capacity.

VLANs serve two purposes in a car. They separate traffic classes: camera control, radar data, diagnostics and infotainment can share links but stay in different broadcast domains with different priorities. And they are a first security boundary: a switch drops frames whose VLAN is not configured on the receiving port, so an infotainment unit cannot inject frames into the chassis VLAN even if it is compromised, provided the switch configuration is right and locked.

7.5Switches, queues and where latency comes from

Automotive Ethernet is switched: each link is point-to-point between an ECU and a switch port, and the switch forwards each frame to the port behind which the destination lives. Automotive switches are mostly configured statically: the forwarding tables, VLAN membership and priority mapping are written at start-up from a configuration that was designed for the whole vehicle, rather than learned at run time, because a car's network does not change and because learning would be an attack surface. Many switches sit inside ECUs, as a switch chip or integrated into the SoC, so that a zone controller or central computer is also a network node.

Latency through a switched network has four parts per hop. The frame must be received completely before it is forwarded (store and forward), because the switch checks the FCS; that costs the frame's transmission time at every hop. The switch needs some microseconds of processing. The frame waits in an output queue behind frames of the same or higher priority. And, crucially, even the highest-priority frame must wait for a lower-priority frame that has already started transmitting on the output port, because Ethernet cannot stop a frame halfway. That last term, the blocking by one maximum-size frame per hop, is what makes plain priority queuing insufficient for tight control loops on slow links.

Pitfall

Calculating Ethernet latency from the average load. A link at 20 % average load can still delay a control frame by several maximum-size frames when a burst arrives, for example when a lidar sends a scan in a few milliseconds or a software update starts. Worst-case analysis must consider bursts, the queue depth per priority and the blocking term per hop, not the average utilisation.

7.7Above the link: IP, DoIP, SOME/IP and IEEE 1722

Most automotive Ethernet traffic is IP: IPv4 in most current vehicles, IPv6 increasingly. Above it, UDP carries cyclic data and events where a lost message is better replaced by the next one than retransmitted late; TCP carries diagnostics, software update and large transfers. DoIP (diagnostics over IP, ISO 13400) carries UDS diagnostic requests from a tester through the gateway to every ECU, on TCP and UDP port 13400, which shortens flashing from tens of minutes over CAN to a few minutes (Camera ECU 10.2). SOME/IP carries service-oriented communication (Section 8.2).

Not everything uses IP. IEEE 1722 (AVTP, the audio video transport protocol from AVB) carries streams directly in Ethernet frames with presentation timestamps, used for audio and some camera streams, and its automotive control format (ACF) tunnels CAN, CAN FD, LIN and FlexRay frames over Ethernet with their timing. That is how a zone controller can forward the traffic of its local CAN buses to the central computer without converting every signal (Section 8.5). Security for Ethernet links can be added at layer 2 with MACsec (IEEE 802.1AE), which authenticates and optionally encrypts each frame between two link partners in hardware, or at higher layers with IPsec and TLS.

8Communication models: signals, services and data

The bus decides how bits move; the communication model decides what the bits mean and who decides to send them. A car uses three models at once: signals broadcast cyclically on CAN, services that clients find and call over Ethernet, and data-centric publish-subscribe in automated-driving software. Each matches a different kind of information, and the gateways between them are where much of the integration work and much of the latency hides.

8.1Signals, PDUs and frames

The classic model is signal-oriented. A signal is a single value with a meaning, a unit and a resolution: vehicle speed, gear, door open. The sender broadcasts it, usually cyclically, sometimes on change or both, and does not know or care who receives it. Every ECU knows from the network database where to find each signal it needs. Signals are packed into a PDU (protocol data unit), the AUTOSAR term for a block of bytes that is transported as a unit, and a PDU is carried in a frame. On CAN one PDU usually fills one frame; on Ethernet many PDUs are collected into one frame, using container PDUs, to save the large per-frame overhead.

The model is simple, robust and deterministic: there is no connection to set up, a lost message is replaced by the next one, and timing analysis is straightforward. Its weakness is that everything is designed in advance. Adding a receiver is free, but adding a signal or changing its scaling means changing the database and the software of every affected ECU, and the model has no notion of requests, answers or larger data structures. In AUTOSAR Classic the COM module packs and unpacks signals, the PDU router forwards PDUs between buses, and the RTE delivers signals to software components (Camera ECU 3.3).

8.2Services: SOME/IP in brief

The service-oriented model treats each function as a server that offers an interface: methods that a client calls with arguments and that return a result, events that the server sends to subscribed clients, and fields, values with a getter, a setter and a change notification. Clients find servers at run time through service discovery, subscribe to the events they need, and receive only those. Data can be structured: a list of detected objects with positions and classes is one event rather than hundreds of signals.

In vehicles the dominant protocol for this is SOME/IP (scalable service-oriented middleware over IP), specified by AUTOSAR: a 16-byte header with service and method IDs, a client and session ID and a message type, followed by a serialised payload, sent over UDP or TCP, with service discovery messages on UDP port 30490 by convention. Its structure, discovery and serialisation are explained in Camera ECU 5 and belong to the AUTOSAR chapter. Here only the architectural point matters: services make the network describe interfaces rather than wires, which is what lets software move between computers without changing the network design.

8.3Data-centric publish-subscribe: DDS

DDS (Data Distribution Service, an Object Management Group standard) takes a different view: the network is a shared, typed data space. Applications publish samples to named topics with a defined type, and subscribe to topics; neither side knows the other. DDS discovers participants automatically, matches publishers and subscribers by topic and type, and delivers data according to quality-of-service policies that both sides declare: reliability (best effort or reliable with retransmission), history depth, durability (does a late subscriber get the last values?), deadline (how often must a sample arrive?), liveliness (is the publisher still alive?) and others. The wire protocol, DDS-RTPS, runs over UDP, with discovery traffic on well-known ports and data over unicast or multicast.

DDS came from defence and industrial systems, where many processes on many computers must share sensor data with explicit timing contracts. It reached cars through ROS 2, which uses DDS as its default middleware, and through AUTOSAR Adaptive, whose communication API ara::com can bind to DDS as well as to SOME/IP. Its strengths are the rich QoS, many-to-many data distribution and shared-memory transports for large data such as point clouds and images inside one computer. Its weaknesses in a car are configuration complexity, discovery traffic in large systems, and less established tooling for automotive validation. Newer middlewares such as Zenoh, also supported by ROS 2, aim at the same space with lighter discovery.

In practice the choice is made per boundary. Brake and steering interfaces stay signal-based with E2E protection, because their behaviour must be predictable and certifiable. Interfaces between ECUs and central computers are increasingly services. Inside a perception and planning stack, where dozens of processes exchange large data at high rates, DDS or a similar data-centric middleware with zero-copy transport is common. A camera ECU engineer typically meets all three in one product: CAN signals for vehicle state, SOME/IP for the interface to the rest of the car, and an internal middleware or DDS between perception processes.

8.5Following one signal through the car

The models meet in the gateway, today usually the zone controller. Step through the path of the vehicle speed from the brake controller to the perception software of a central computer, and of a brake request back:

The ESC reads the four wheel-speed sensors, computes the vehicle speed in its 10 ms task and writes the signal VehSpeed into the PDU that AUTOSAR COM sends every 20 ms. The value is already up to one task period old when it is written, because it describes the wheel pulses counted during the last cycle. The ESC knows nothing about who will use it.

The CAN driver offers the frame with identifier 0x0C4 to arbitration. Higher-priority frames, such as the brake request and the wheel speeds, may go first; the worst-case response time from queuing to reception is about 1.6 ms on the example bus of Section 4.6. Every node on the chassis bus receives it; only those whose filters match keep it.

The zone controller's CAN controller receives the frame. Its routing software runs cyclically, for example every millisecond, unpacks VehSpeed, and either repacks it with other signals into an Ethernet PDU (signal gateway), forwards the whole PDU in a container (PDU gateway), forwards the raw CAN frame in an IEEE 1722 tunnel, or updates the field of a SOME/IP service. Each option trades latency, bandwidth and the amount of configuration the gateway needs.

The Ethernet frame leaves with the VLAN and priority of vehicle-state data, crosses the zone's switch port, the 1000BASE-T1 link and the switch in the central computer. Two store-and-forward links at 1 Gbit/s with strict priority add about 30 µs in the worst case: negligible against the cycles before and after.

The middleware in the central computer receives the UDP datagram, deserialises the event and delivers it to the subscribed perception process, ideally together with a timestamp of when the value was valid in the common gPTP time. The perception software reads it at its next processing cycle and uses it, for example, to predict where static objects should appear in the next camera frame.

The emergency-braking function decides to brake and publishes a deceleration request. It travels the same path in reverse, but with the highest priority at every stage: a top VLAN priority on Ethernet, the lowest CAN identifier on the chassis bus, a fast routing path in the zone, and an E2E header with counter and CRC that the ESC checks before acting. Every stage must also be analysed for its worst case, because this message is part of a safety goal with a fault-tolerant time budget.

Pitfall

Gateways that repack signals without their timing information. When a signal crosses from CAN to Ethernet and is delivered as "the current value", the receiver loses the information of when it was measured, and a 10 to 20 ms age variation turns into a position error for anything that uses it with motion (Calibration and fusion 6.1). Carry a validity timestamp, or at least a known worst-case age, across every gateway.

9Camera and board-level links: a recap

Cameras are the largest data sources in a car and use their own links. The camera-pipeline chapter explains them in depth; this chapter needs only the reason they exist and how they fit into the vehicle architecture.

9.1Why cameras are not on the Ethernet backbone

An 8-megapixel front camera at 30 frames per second with 12-bit raw pixels produces 3840 × 2160 × 12 × 30 ≈ 3.0 Gbit/s; a 3-megapixel surround camera about 1.1 Gbit/s. Seven such cameras add up to almost 10 Gbit/s, twenty times the whole Ethernet backbone of the example car in Section 13.2. Sending them over Ethernet would need either multi-gigabit links per camera, with expensive switches and PHYs in a camera module that must stay small, cheap and cool, or compression, which adds latency of a frame or more, power in the camera, and artefacts that perception networks are not trained for. So cameras use dedicated, asymmetric serializer/deserializer links instead: the camera's serializer packs the sensor's MIPI CSI-2 output onto a single coax or shielded pair at 3 to 12 Gbit/s, and a deserializer in the ECU unpacks it again into CSI-2 for the SoC (Camera pipeline 8).

The same cable carries a slow back channel, about 50 to 190 Mbit/s, for I²C register access, frame-sync triggers and GPIOs, and the camera's supply through power over coax (Camera pipeline 8). The result is a camera with almost no intelligence of its own, configured remotely and synchronised by the central ECU. The main families are TI's FPD-Link III and IV, Analog Devices' GMSL2 and GMSL3, and two open standards that compete to replace them: MIPI A-PHY and the Automotive SerDes Alliance's ASA Motion Link. Displays use the same technology in the other direction.

Interesting detail

The camera link is not part of the vehicle network in the usual sense: it has no addresses, no switching and no other traffic. From the network architect's point of view a camera is a peripheral of the ECU it plugs into, which is why cameras can only be "moved" to another computer by re-wiring, and why zonal architectures still run camera cables directly to the central computer. Ethernet cameras exist, for example with compressed video for recording or for cabin monitoring, and the asymmetric Ethernet work in IEEE 802.3dm aims to bring native Ethernet to cameras, but raw-video SerDes links dominate ADAS today.

9.2Links between chips: SPI, PCIe and on-board Ethernet

Inside an ADAS or central ECU the SoC and the safety MCU usually exchange vehicle state, power-mode requests and heartbeats over SPI, because it needs almost no software and works within milliseconds of power-on (Camera ECU 1.3). Two SoCs in one box, or a SoC and an external accelerator or switch, use PCIe (for example Gen4 x4 at about 64 Gbit/s) or on-board Ethernet links without PHYs, connected through standard MAC-to-MAC interfaces such as RGMII or SGMII. These links decide how a multi-SoC computer shares camera data and results internally, and they are a frequent bottleneck that system architects underestimate when they plan to "just move a network to the other SoC".

10Physical connections: cables, connectors and the harness

Every link in this chapter ends in a connector, and the connector is where most field failures of vehicle electronics happen: water ingress, fretting corrosion, a terminal that was not fully pushed into its housing, a cable bent too tightly behind a bumper. Automotive cables and connectors look over-engineered compared to consumer ones because they must survive fifteen years of vibration, temperature cycling and moisture, be assembled by hand on a moving line without mistakes, and still meet the impedance and loss requirements of gigabit links.

10.1Why automotive connectors are different

Three requirements dominate. Environment: a connector in a door, bumper or engine bay must be sealed against water jets and dust (protection classes up to IP6K9K, high-pressure hot-water cleaning), operate from −40 to +105 °C or more and resist salt, fuel and cleaning agents. Mechanics: under vibration and thermal cycling, contacts move against each other by micrometres, which wears through tin plating and forms oxide, a failure called fretting corrosion that shows up as intermittent contact after years; signal contacts therefore use gold or silver plating, higher contact forces or both. Assembly: a harness is plugged together by people, so every connector has a locking latch, often a secondary connector position assurance (CPA) that can only be closed when the connector is fully mated, a terminal position assurance (TPA) that locks each crimped terminal in its cavity, and mechanical coding and colours so that two similar connectors cannot be swapped. In contrast to consumer connectors rated for hundreds or thousands of mating cycles, automotive connectors are often specified for only 10 to 25, because they are mated once in the factory and a few times in service.

10.2Cables: coax, twisted pairs and star quad

Coaxial cable with 50 Ω impedance carries one signal referenced to the shield, which surrounds it completely. Because the field is contained, coax radiates little and picks up little, and because the shield is a good conductor it can also carry the return current of power over coax. Its loss grows with frequency and falls with cable diameter: thin automotive coax loses roughly 0.5 to 1.5 dB per metre at 3 GHz, so a 15 m camera link can lose 10 to 20 dB at the frequencies that matter, which the SerDes receiver must equalise (Section 11.2). Unshielded twisted pairs (UTP) are the cheapest and lightest data cable; the twist keeps the two wires equally exposed, so interference becomes common mode, and their characteristic impedance is about 100 Ω for Ethernet and 120 Ω for CAN. Shielded twisted pairs (STP) add foil and braid for higher frequencies or longer runs. Star quad cables carry two differential pairs in one round cable with good isolation between them.

10.4The channel: loss, return loss and mode conversion

For high-speed links the unit of design is the channel: everything between the two PHYs or SerDes chips, including PCB traces, common-mode chokes, ECU connectors, inline connectors where the harness is split into segments, and the cable segments themselves. The link standards specify the channel, not the parts, with limits on four quantities across frequency. Insertion loss is how much of the signal arrives; it decides how hard the receiver must equalise. Return loss is how much is reflected back by impedance discontinuities; it decides how much the echo canceller must remove and how much ringing remains. Mode conversion (longitudinal conversion loss, LCL, and its transmit counterpart TCL) measures how much common-mode disturbance turns into differential signal, the measure of balance; it decides both immunity and emissions. Crosstalk between pairs in the same cable bundle or connector matters for multi-pair links and dense connectors. 100BASE-T1 channels of 15 m, for example, are specified with up to four inline connectors; more connectors or poor-quality ones eat the budget.

In practice

Most marginal high-speed links are not caused by the chips but by the channel: an inline connector from a different supplier, a cable bent tighter than its minimum radius, a crimp that changed the impedance of a shielded pair, or a PCB transition without a proper ground return. The PHY's signal-quality indicator and the SerDes eye monitor are the first tools to look at; a vector network analyser measurement of the harness segment is the definitive one.

10.5The wiring harness

The harness is the car's largest single electrical component: several kilometres of wire, more than a thousand circuits, dozens of connectors and splices, routed through doors, pillars and the floor. It is usually designed by the OEM and built by a specialised supplier, almost entirely by hand, in many variants because each option package changes it. Its weight and its labour cost are the strongest economic arguments for zonal architectures, Ethernet backbones and fewer, more integrated ECUs. For an electronics engineer the harness matters because it fixes cable lengths, the number of inline connectors in each channel, how close signal cables run to power cables, and where grounds are connected, all of which decide whether a link that works on the bench also works in the car.

11Signal integrity, EMC, shielding and grounding

Two related questions decide whether electronics work in a car. Signal integrity asks whether the bits arrive intact through the channel. Electromagnetic compatibility asks whether the electronics disturb nothing else, above all the radio receivers in the car, and are not disturbed by anything else, from ignition sparks to a transmitter next to the road. Both come down to the same physics: currents flow in loops, every loop is an antenna, and every conductor has inductance.

11.1Why EMC is a release criterion

A vehicle cannot be sold without passing EMC type approval (UN Regulation No. 10), and every ECU must pass the OEM's component EMC tests before it may go into a car. The tests fall into two groups. Emissions: CISPR 25 measures conducted and radiated emissions of components and vehicles against limits designed to protect the car's own receivers, AM, FM and DAB radio, GNSS, mobile telephony and others, across a range from 150 kHz up to several gigahertz. Immunity: the ISO 11452 series exposes components to radiated fields in an absorber-lined chamber (ALSE, part 2) and to currents injected into the harness with a clamp (bulk current injection, part 4), at field strengths of 100 V/m or more; ISO 7637 applies electrical transients on supply and signal lines; ISO 10605 applies electrostatic discharges of up to ±8 kV by contact and ±15 kV or more through air. OEMs add their own, usually stricter, specifications.

EMC failures are expensive because they appear late: the chamber test happens with a near-final design, and a fix may need a new PCB layout, a shielded housing or a different cable. Experienced teams design for EMC from the first schematic and test pre-compliance early.

11.2Signal integrity: loss, intersymbol interference and the eye

A cable attenuates high frequencies more than low ones: the skin effect concentrates current at the conductor surface as frequency rises, and the dielectric absorbs more energy at higher frequencies. A sharp bit edge contains high frequencies, so after a long cable the edges are slow and each bit's energy smears into the following bits. This intersymbol interference means the voltage at the sampling instant depends not only on the current bit but on the previous ones. The standard way to see it is the eye diagram: many bit periods overlaid on each other. The open area in the middle, the eye, is the margin the receiver has for noise, crosstalk and timing jitter.

Real high-speed receivers combine several equalisers: a continuous-time linear equaliser (CTLE) that boosts high frequencies, a feed-forward equaliser (FFE) on the transmitter or receiver side, and a decision-feedback equaliser (DFE) that subtracts the effect of bits already decided. They adapt to the actual channel during link training, which is why a SerDes or Ethernet PHY can work over cables with very different lengths. The adaptation has limits, and loss, reflections and crosstalk together must stay within the channel specification (Section 10.4). SerDes and Ethernet PHYs expose internal eye-opening or signal-quality metrics through registers, which makes them the first diagnostic for "marginal link" problems.

11.3Common mode, chokes and split termination

Most emissions from a differential link are not caused by its differential signal but by a small common-mode current: any imbalance in the drivers, the PCB traces, the connector or the cable converts a little of the signal into a current that flows in the same direction on both wires and returns through the car body. That current loop is a far better antenna than the twisted pair itself. Two components fight it. A common-mode choke, two windings on one ferrite core, presents a high impedance to currents that flow in the same direction on both wires and almost none to the differential signal. A split termination replaces CAN's single 120 Ω resistor with two 60 Ω resistors whose midpoint is connected to ground through a capacitor of a few nanofarads, giving common-mode noise a short path to ground at the ECU instead of through the harness. Both are standard on automotive transceivers' reference designs.

11.4Shielding

A shield works by carrying a current that cancels the field of the disturbance: an external field induces currents in the shield instead of the conductors inside, and a cable's own common-mode current returns on the shield instead of radiating. For that, the shield must be connected with low impedance at high frequency, which means a 360° connection around the cable at the connector. A shield brought out as a wire, a "pigtail" a few centimetres long, has tens of nanohenries of inductance, tens of ohms at 100 MHz, and makes the shield almost useless at the frequencies that matter. For high-frequency links shields are connected at both ends; the old rule of grounding a shield at one end only, to avoid ground loops, applies to low-frequency analog signals, not to data links.

11.5Grounding: why every ECU has a different zero

In a car the body is the return conductor for most loads, and it is far from ideal. Large currents, from the starter motor during cranking, the blower, the heated windscreen or electric power steering, flow through the body and the ground wires and create voltage differences between the ground points of different ECUs. A volt is common; more is possible during cranking or with a corroded ground connection.

This is the deeper reason why almost every automotive data link is differential or coaxial, why CAN receivers must tolerate a common-mode range of at least −2 to +7 V, and why LIN, the one single-ended bus, uses 12 V logic levels with thresholds at about 40 % and 60 % of the battery voltage and stays slow. Inside the ECU the same thinking continues: separate return paths for noisy power stages and sensitive analog or high-speed circuits, joined at one defined point, and a continuous ground plane under every high-speed trace so that its return current flows directly beneath it.

Pitfall

Debugging a sensor problem on a bench where all ECUs share one lab supply and one ground point. The ground offsets of the vehicle do not exist there, so a design that relies on a single-ended signal or on a shared ground between two ECUs passes every bench test and fails in the car, typically only when a large load switches on.

11.6EMC by design

The cheapest EMC fixes are in the design. Switching regulators run at frequencies chosen to keep their harmonics out of the radio bands, often 2.1 MHz to stay above the AM band, or use spread-spectrum modulation that smears the energy of each harmonic. Clocks and fast interfaces use controlled slew rates and spread-spectrum clocking where the protocol allows it. Every cable leaving the ECU is filtered at the connector, so that noise from inside does not use the harness as an antenna. High-speed traces stay over continuous ground planes, and the housing, if metallic, is connected to the cable shields all around. A typical late EMC failure for a camera ECU is a narrow emission peak at a harmonic of a SerDes or DDR clock in an FM or DAB band, caused by a common-mode current on a camera cable; the fix is better balance or filtering at the connector, a change in the clock frequency, or a different cable shield connection.

12Power: voltage levels, transients and thermal design

The 12 V supply of a car is one of the harshest power sources electronics ever meet. It drops to a few volts while the engine starts, rises to tens of volts if a battery terminal comes loose while the alternator is charging, carries spikes of over a hundred volts when inductive loads switch, can be connected the wrong way round by a mechanic, and must not be drained by parked electronics. At the other end of the ECU, a modern SoC needs several precisely sequenced rails below one volt at tens of amperes. Everything between the connector and the chip exists to bridge those two worlds, and the heat that remains decides how much computing a sealed box can do.

12.1The vehicle power nets

The classic 12 V net consists of a lead-acid battery (about 12.6 V when charged and at rest) and an alternator that holds the net at about 13.5–14.8 V while the engine runs. Most ECUs are specified to work fully between about 9 and 16 V and to survive defined disturbances outside that range. Trucks and buses use a 24 V net (covered in the commercial-vehicle chapter). Mild hybrids add a 48 V net for a starter-generator and large loads such as electric compressors and active chassis; 48 V was chosen because for the same power the current is a quarter of 12 V and the resistive losses \(I^2R\) a sixteenth, while staying below the 60 V limit above which protection against electric shock becomes mandatory. Electric vehicles have a high-voltage traction net of 400 or 800 V, from which a DC/DC converter feeds the 12 V (or 48 V) net, so there is no alternator; the low-voltage battery remains for the electronics and for safety.

ECU connectors name their supply terminals with numbers from the German standard DIN 72552 that the whole industry still uses: KL30 (terminal 30) is the permanent battery supply, KL15 the ignition-switched supply, which many ECUs use as a wake-up signal, KL31 ground, and KL50 the starter signal.

12.2What the supply really does

The disturbances are standardised, so every ECU is designed and tested against the same profiles: ISO 16750-2 for slow supply variations and load dump, ISO 7637-2 for fast transients, and the OEMs' own specifications such as the jointly written LV 124 of several German manufacturers.

The other profiles complete the picture. A jump start with a truck or a charger in boost mode applies about 24 V for up to a minute. Reverse polarity of −14 V for a minute must not damage anything. ISO 7637-2 pulses model switching events: pulse 1 is a negative spike of −75 to −150 V lasting about 2 ms when an inductive load is disconnected in parallel with the ECU; pulse 2a a positive spike of tens of volts for 50 µs when current in the wiring harness is interrupted; pulses 3a and 3b bursts of fast spikes of over 100 V lasting nanoseconds, from switching arcs. The ECU must either keep working through each of them, or reset and recover in a defined way; the required behaviour, from "full function" to "no damage", is specified per test as a functional status class.

Start-stop systems made cranking a frequent event rather than a daily one, and the expectation changed with it: an infotainment screen or a camera function must not reboot every time the engine restarts at a traffic light. Warm cranks are milder than cold ones, typically staying above 6 to 8 V, but surviving them without a reset requires either a wide-input regulator that works down to a few volts or a boost stage in front of it.

12.3The ECU power path

Reverse-polarity protection was traditionally a series diode, which wastes 0.5–1 V and several watts at a few amperes; modern ECUs use an ideal-diode controller with a MOSFET that conducts with milliohms in the forward direction and blocks when the battery is reversed. A TVS diode clamps the ISO 7637 pulses and the residual of a centrally clamped load dump. An LC π-filter keeps the switching noise of the regulators inside the ECU, which is what the conducted-emissions test of CISPR 25 measures. Bulk capacitors bridge short dropouts and give the processor time to save state on a power loss.

The pre-regulator is a wide-input synchronous buck converter that accepts roughly 3 to 40 V and produces an intermediate rail of 5 or 3.3 V; automotive parts switch at about 2.1 MHz, above the AM radio band, and many use spread spectrum. The PMIC then generates the SoC's rails, for example a core rail around 0.8 V for the CPU and NPU that may need 20–40 A on a large SoC, memory rails for LPDDR, and I/O rails of 1.8 and 3.3 V, in the order the SoC demands, and signals power-good. Because the SoC's correct operation is part of the safety argument, a separate supervisor watches each rail with independent over- and under-voltage thresholds and a watchdog, and forces a reset or a safe state when anything leaves its window (Functional safety 4.6).

Why it matters

Software engineers meet the power path as behaviour: a reset during cranking that the specification says must not happen, a brown-out that corrupts a file system write, a camera that does not come up because its power-over-coax switch detected a short at start-up, a "voltage out of range" DTC. Knowing what each stage does turns those symptoms into questions with answers: did the pre-regulator drop out, did the supervisor trip, did the PoC switch latch off?

12.4Sleep, wake-up and quiescent current

A parked car must start after weeks. A typical battery of 70 Ah, of which perhaps half can be used before starting becomes doubtful, lasts about 49 days at a total quiescent current of 30 mA. With dozens of ECUs, this leaves each one well below a milliampere in sleep; OEMs typically allow on the order of 100 µA per ECU. So every ECU must really switch off, and the network must agree when to sleep and how to wake up.

Network management (AUTOSAR NM on CAN and Ethernet) coordinates this: each ECU that needs the network sends NM messages; when no ECU has sent one for a configured time, all go to sleep together. Waking up uses the network itself: a CAN transceiver in sleep mode stays powered at microamperes and wakes the ECU on bus activity, and since ISO 11898-2:2016 partial networking transceivers decode frames in sleep and wake their ECU only on a specific wake-up frame, so that a car can, for example, charge or run a remote climate function without waking every ECU on the bus. For automotive Ethernet, the OPEN Alliance TC10 specification defines sleep and wake-up signalling over the data line. KL15, the ignition line, remains a wake-up source for ECUs that should run whenever the car is on.

Pitfall

A single ECU that keeps the network awake. One node that sends NM messages because of a software bug, a diagnostic session left open or a periodic task that never finishes keeps every ECU on its bus awake, and the battery is flat after a few days. Finding the culprit requires logging the NM traffic of a parked car over hours, which is why "who keeps the bus awake?" is a classic integration task.

12.5Power distribution: electronic fuses and power over coax

Traditional power distribution runs from the battery through a fuse box with melting fuses and relays to each load. A melting fuse is slow and coarse, so the wire behind it must be thick enough to survive the current that blows it, and a blown fuse needs a workshop visit. Zonal architectures replace this with electronic fuses (eFuses) and smart high-side switches in the zone controllers: semiconductor switches that measure the current continuously, trip on overcurrent within microseconds, follow a configurable current-time characteristic matched to the wire (so the wire can be thinner), report the load current as diagnostics, and can be reset by software. They also let the zone controller switch off loads that are not needed while parked, which helps the quiescent budget.

Automated driving adds the requirement of redundant power: if braking or steering must keep working after a single electrical fault, the safety-relevant loads need two independent supply paths, for example two batteries or a battery plus a DC/DC converter, separated by switches that isolate a short on one side. That requirement shapes the zonal topology too, with two power feeds into critical zones.

Power over coax is the camera's power distribution: the ECU switches each camera's supply through a protected high-side switch onto the coax centre conductor, and the camera takes typically 1 to 3 W from it. The switch's current measurement doubles as a diagnosis of the cable: no current means an open cable or a missing camera, too much a short (Camera pipeline 8).

12.6Thermal design

Every watt an ECU consumes becomes heat, which must flow from the silicon to the air around the box. The flow is easiest to reason about as a chain of thermal resistances, measured in kelvin per watt: from the junction to the chip's case, through the thermal interface material to the housing or heat sink, and from there to the ambient air. The temperature rise across each is the power times its resistance, exactly like a voltage drop across resistors in series.

The ambient temperature is set by the mounting place, and it is higher than intuition suggests: in the passenger compartment under a dashboard 70–85 °C is a common design point, behind a windscreen in the sun more, and in the engine bay 105–125 °C. Semiconductor qualification follows: AEC-Q100 grade 2 parts are rated for −40 to +105 °C ambient, grade 1 for +125 °C, grade 0 for +150 °C. The design answers are a better heat path (heat spreaders, fins, a housing with more surface), active cooling (fans for cockpit computers, liquid cooling for central computers of 100 W and more), a cooler mounting place, or less power, which in software means a thermal policy that reduces frame rate or network size in a controlled way rather than letting the SoC throttle unpredictably (Embedded compute 14.7, Edge AI).

Temperature also decides lifetime. Electrolytic capacitors, a classic weak point, roughly halve their life for every 10 K hotter, and solder joints fatigue with every temperature cycle. Qualification therefore uses a mission profile: how many hours the ECU spends at which temperature over fifteen years. A classic car runs about 8000 operating hours in its life; an ECU that is active while the car is charging, parked with surveillance cameras on or driving as a robotaxi accumulates many times more, which changes the component choices.

13Topology, bandwidth, latency and fault behaviour

With the links, models, cables and power understood, the last step is to put a network together and to answer the questions every architecture review asks: does the bandwidth fit, with margin; how old is a value when it is used; and what happens when a wire breaks, a switch hangs or a node goes mad?

13.1Topologies

Real vehicles combine them: CAN FD buses at the edges, a switched star inside each zone, a backbone that is a star around the central computer or a ring between the zones, and point-to-point SerDes links for cameras. The choice is driven by cost per node, wire length, the number of hops a time-critical message crosses and which single faults must be survived.

13.2A worked bandwidth budget for a zonal car

Consider a zonal car with an L2+ sensor set: a front 8-megapixel camera and six 3-megapixel surround and side cameras; a front 4D imaging radar that delivers a point cloud and four corner radars that deliver detection lists; one front lidar; twelve ultrasonic sensors; and body and chassis I/O. Four zone controllers (front, rear, left, right) connect to a central computer over 1000BASE-T1. The budget is built from the payload of each source, plus the protocol overhead on Ethernet: 42 bytes of Ethernet framing and 44 bytes of IP, UDP and middleware headers per frame of 1400 payload bytes, about 6 %.

Pitfall

Forgetting the data logger. Development vehicles record all sensor data, often raw camera data too, which can mean 10–40 Gbit/s into a logger. If the logger is connected through the production network, it changes every budget; development cars therefore usually have a separate recording path, and the production network must not be designed around it.

Faster buses do not fix that; timestamps do, because a value that carries the time it was valid can be interpolated or extrapolated to the moment it is used (Calibration and fusion 6). For control loops where age must be bounded rather than compensated, the remedy is to align the cycles, so that producer, gateway and consumer run in a fixed phase relation, which is exactly what time-triggered designs such as FlexRay or a TSN schedule with synchronised tasks achieve.

Numbers

Rules of thumb for one hop: classic CAN frame of 8 bytes at 500 kbit/s 0.22–0.27 ms; CAN FD 64 bytes at 500 kbit/s / 2 Mbit/s 0.34 ms; Ethernet maximum frame 123 µs at 100 Mbit/s and 12 µs at 1 Gbit/s per store-and-forward hop; gateway routing 0.1–1 ms depending on the routing cycle; signal propagation 5 ns/m, negligible in a car. Software cycles of 5–100 ms usually dominate.

13.4How networks fail, and how they are made to tolerate it

The physical faults are few: a wire open, a wire shorted to ground, to battery or to its partner, a connector with intermittent contact, a transceiver or PHY that fails dead or stuck, and a node whose software sends wrong, too many or no messages. Their effects depend on the topology. On a high-speed CAN bus a short between CAN_H and CAN_L, or of either wire to battery, stops all communication on the bus; an open wire splits the bus into parts that may keep working with poor termination. A stuck-dominant transceiver blocks the bus until its dominant time-out releases it. On switched Ethernet a broken link affects only the nodes behind it, but a failed switch takes down everything behind that switch, and a link that drops needs to retrain before it carries data again, which takes much longer than a control cycle.

Tolerance is built in layers. Detection comes first: every receiver monitors the timeouts of the messages it expects and checks E2E counters and CRCs, so it knows within a defined time that its data is missing or wrong (Functional safety 4.4). Containment keeps a fault local: CAN's fault confinement, transceiver time-outs, per-stream policing in switches, separate buses or VLANs for different domains. Redundancy keeps the function alive where needed: two CAN buses to a brake controller, a ring with FRER on the backbone, two power feeds. And degradation defines what each function does without the data: an AEB function without valid vehicle speed switches off and informs the driver; a parking camera without its CAN gear signal still shows the image but without guidelines.

Talk track

"Network faults are physical faults like opens and shorts, failed transceivers or switches, and software faults like a node that floods the bus or goes silent. CAN contains a sick node through error counters and transceiver time-outs; switched Ethernet contains faults per link but makes the switch a single point of failure, so critical paths get redundancy such as FRER rings or a second bus. Every receiver detects missing or corrupted data with timeouts and E2E protection, and every function has a defined degradation when its inputs disappear."

13.5Security at the network level

Classic vehicle buses assume that every node is trustworthy, which stopped being true when cars got cellular connections. The best-known demonstration, the remote attack on a Jeep Cherokee in 2015, went from the cellular modem through the head unit onto the CAN bus and then sent ordinary CAN frames to the steering and braking ECUs. The network-level defences that followed are layered. The central gateway or zone controllers act as firewalls between domains and only forward what is configured. VLANs and static switch configuration keep Ethernet domains apart. SecOC, AUTOSAR's secure onboard communication, adds a truncated message authentication code and a freshness value to safety-relevant PDUs, so a receiver can tell an authentic brake request from an injected one; it complements E2E, which only protects against random faults (Functional safety 9.6). MACsec protects whole Ethernet links in hardware. An intrusion detection system watches traffic patterns for anomalies and reports them to the OEM's security operations centre, which UN R155 effectively requires.

13.6Debugging a vehicle network

Network problems are diagnosed from the bottom up. On CAN, the first checks are electrical: about 60 Ω between CAN_H and CAN_L with power off, the recessive and dominant levels on an oscilloscope, the bit timing of every node. Then the logical layer: a bus analyser such as CANoe, a PCAN interface or SocketCAN's candump shows error frames, bus load and which node is error-passive. On automotive Ethernet a laptop cannot simply be plugged in, because the PHY is single-pair: a media converter or a dedicated TAP that copies the traffic of a T1 link to a standard Ethernet port, or port mirroring in a switch, gives Wireshark access. The PHY's link status, signal quality indicator and cable diagnostics come before any packet capture.

QSelf-check questions

Core questions with short model answers; the chapter sections hold the depth. The second group checks understanding with "why" and "what if" questions rather than definitions.

Q.1Core questions

Explain CAN arbitration.Basic

CAN is a wired-AND bus: a dominant bit (0) driven by any node overwrites recessive (1). All nodes that start together send their identifiers MSB first and read back each bit. A node that sends recessive but reads dominant has lost, stops, and becomes a receiver. The lowest identifier wins without losing a bit, so arbitration is non-destructive and the identifier is the message priority.

Why is CAN limited to about 1 Mbit/s, and how does CAN FD get around it?Basic

During arbitration and acknowledgement a node's bit can be overwritten by a node at the far end, so the signal's round trip through cable and transceivers must fit before the sample point of one bit. That ties bit rate to bus length: about 40 m at 1 Mbit/s. CAN FD keeps the nominal rate for arbitration and ACK but switches to a higher data rate after the BRS bit, because then only one node transmits and only the one-way delay matters.

What is bit stuffing and why does CAN need it?Basic

After five equal bits the transmitter inserts one opposite bit, which receivers remove. CAN has no clock line, so receivers resynchronise on edges; stuffing guarantees an edge at least every six bits. It also makes six equal bits illegal, which CAN uses on purpose as the error flag. The cost is variable frame length, up to about 20 % more bits in the worst case.

Describe CAN error handling and the bus-off state.Intermediate

Five checks: bit, stuff, CRC, form and ACK errors. A node that detects one sends an error flag that destroys the frame for everybody, and the sender retransmits, so reception is consistent. Each node counts transmit errors (+8) and receive errors (+1), minus 1 per success. Above 127 it becomes error-passive and may only send recessive flags; above 255 transmit errors it goes bus-off and stops sending until it sees 128 × 11 recessive bits. A faulty node thus removes itself.

How do you calculate the worst-case latency of a CAN message?Intermediate

With response-time analysis for fixed-priority non-preemptive scheduling: the message waits for one lower-priority frame already on the bus (blocking), plus all higher-priority frames released while it waits, iterated to a fixed point, plus its own worst-case frame time including stuff bits. On a 500 kbit/s bus at 17 % load the lowest of twelve messages saw about 3.2 ms; at 68 % load, 17 ms. It assumes drivers always offer the highest-priority pending frame.

What are the differences between CAN, LIN, FlexRay and automotive Ethernet?Basic

LIN: one wire, up to 20 kbit/s, one commander with a schedule, cheapest, for simple body nodes. CAN/CAN FD: two-wire differential bus, multi-master priority arbitration, up to 1 Mbit/s (FD data phase 2–8 Mbit/s), robust and cheap, for state signals. FlexRay: 10 Mbit/s time-division with global time and two channels, deterministic, now mostly legacy. Ethernet: switched point-to-point links from 10 Mbit/s to 10 Gbit/s on single pairs, IP-based, with TSN for determinism; the backbone of new architectures.

Explain domain versus zonal architecture.Basic

Domain: functions of one domain consolidated on a domain controller, connected over an Ethernet backbone, but sensors and actuators still wired to their domain controller wherever it is. Zonal: zone controllers by location connect, power and gateway everything nearby, while functions run as software in central computers. Zonal shortens the harness and decouples software from hardware, but needs a deterministic, redundant network and central computers engineered for safety.

How does 100BASE-T1 send full duplex over a single pair?Intermediate

Both PHYs transmit simultaneously on the same pair. A hybrid couples transmitter and receiver to the pair, and an adaptive echo canceller subtracts the PHY's own transmitted signal and its reflections from what it receives, leaving the remote signal. PAM3 coding at 66.7 MBd keeps the spectrum low enough for unshielded cable and automotive emission limits. One PHY is master and provides the clock; there is no auto-negotiation.

What does the VLAN tag contain and what is it used for in a car?Basic

Four bytes after the source MAC: TPID 0x8100, a 3-bit priority code point, a drop-eligible bit and a 12-bit VLAN ID. The VID separates traffic classes and security domains, since switches only forward a VLAN to ports that are members; the PCP selects the egress queue in each switch, so control traffic overtakes bulk traffic.

Name the main TSN standards and what each does.Intermediate

802.1AS gPTP: common time. 802.1Qav: credit-based shaper for streams. 802.1Qbv: time-aware shaper with scheduled gates. 802.1Qbu/802.3br: frame preemption to cut blocking by large frames. 802.1Qci: per-stream filtering and policing against babbling nodes. 802.1CB: frame replication and elimination over redundant paths. 802.1DG: the automotive profile that selects among them.

What is 10BASE-T1S and why is it interesting for zonal architectures?Intermediate

10 Mbit/s Ethernet on one unshielded pair shared by eight or more nodes over at least 25 m, with PLCA giving each node a transmit opportunity in turn, so there are no collisions and latency is bounded. It brings native Ethernet to small edge nodes such as door or light modules, so a zone controller needs no CAN gateway and the whole car speaks one protocol family. CAN XL competes for the same role.

Signal-oriented versus service-oriented communication?Basic

Signal-oriented: values broadcast cyclically according to a database fixed at design time; the sender does not know the receivers; simple and deterministic but rigid. Service-oriented: servers offer methods, events and fields, clients discover and subscribe at run time, data can be structured; flexible and suited to Ethernet and software that moves between computers. Cars use both, with gateways translating between them.

What is DDS and how does it differ from SOME/IP?Intermediate

DDS is a data-centric publish-subscribe standard: applications publish and subscribe to typed topics without knowing each other, and declare QoS such as reliability, history, deadline and liveliness that DDS enforces. SOME/IP is service-oriented: explicit service interfaces with methods, events and fields, found by service discovery. DDS is common in ROS 2 and AD stacks and is a binding of AUTOSAR Adaptive; SOME/IP dominates between ECUs.

Why do cameras use SerDes links instead of Ethernet?Basic

Raw video is large: an 8 MP camera at 30 fps with 12-bit pixels is about 3 Gbit/s. SerDes links carry that over one coax with low, fixed latency, plus a back channel for control and power over coax, with a very simple camera. Ethernet would need multi-gigabit PHYs in each camera or compression, which adds latency, power and artefacts. Asymmetric Ethernet is being standardised but not yet dominant.

Why are CAN buses terminated with 120 Ω at both ends?Basic

The twisted pair is a transmission line of about 120 Ω. A matching resistor at each end absorbs arriving edges instead of reflecting them, so the bus does not ring. The termination also defines the recessive state by pulling both wires together. Measured between CAN_H and CAN_L with power off, two terminations in parallel give 60 Ω.

What is a load dump and how is an ECU protected against it?Intermediate

If the battery is disconnected while the alternator delivers a large current, the alternator's voltage rises to up to about 100 V for up to 400 ms before its regulator recovers. Most vehicles clamp it centrally to about 35 V. The ECU protects itself with a TVS clamp and a pre-regulator rated for the remaining voltage, and must survive or keep working according to its specified functional status.

What does an ECU power path look like, from connector to SoC?Intermediate

Reverse-polarity protection (ideal diode MOSFET), transient clamp (TVS), EMC π-filter, bulk capacitance, a wide-input buck pre-regulator to an intermediate rail, then a PMIC that generates and sequences the core, memory and I/O rails. An independent supervisor monitors the rails and a watchdog for safety, and protected switches feed external loads such as cameras over coax.

What is EMC testing and which standards apply?Basic

Emissions: CISPR 25, conducted and radiated, to protect the car's radio receivers. Immunity: ISO 11452 (ALSE radiated fields, bulk current injection), ISO 7637 transients on supply and signal lines, ISO 10605 ESD. Vehicle type approval: UN R10. OEMs add stricter in-house specifications. Failures appear late and are expensive, so EMC is designed in from the start.

How do you size the thermal design of an ECU?Intermediate

Model the heat path as thermal resistances in series, junction to case, interface material, housing to air, and compute \(T_j = T_a + P \sum\theta\) at the worst-case ambient of the mounting place. Compare with the junction limit and the component grades; the allowed power is \((T_{j,max} - T_a)/\sum\theta\). Then decide between better cooling, a different location or a controlled reduction of computing load.

Q.2Why and what if

Why is CAN arbitration called non-destructive, and why does that matter compared to old shared Ethernet?Intermediate

The winning frame continues without losing a single bit; the losers simply stop and retry. Shared CSMA/CD Ethernet destroyed both colliding frames and backed off randomly, wasting capacity and giving no latency bound. CAN therefore uses the bus fully under load and lets high-priority messages have computable worst-case latencies.

What happens if two ECUs send frames with the same identifier?Intermediate

They both win arbitration together and transmit the identical identifier, then their data fields differ: the node sending a recessive bit reads dominant and detects a bit error, sends an error flag, and both retransmit, colliding again. Error counters rise until one goes error-passive. The result is intermittent errors and delays, which is why identifiers must be unique in the network database.

Why can't you just raise the bit rate of a CAN bus that is too full?Intermediate

Because of the round-trip constraint: every node must see each arbitration bit within the bit time. Higher rates shorten the allowed bus length and stub lengths, and all nodes must support the new timing. The usual remedies are moving to CAN FD (faster data phase, bigger payloads), splitting the bus, or moving traffic to Ethernet.

Why does CAN FD switch back to the slow rate for the ACK slot?Advanced

In the ACK slot every receiver writes a dominant bit at the same time, and the transmitter must see it within the bit. That is again a round trip between the farthest nodes, the same constraint as arbitration, so it needs the nominal bit time.

What if a CAN transceiver fails stuck dominant?Intermediate

The bus is held dominant, so no other node can send. Error counters do not help, because the faulty node does not see its own frames fail in the usual way. Modern transceivers have a dominant time-out that releases the bus after a few milliseconds of continuous dominant transmission; receivers detect the missing messages by timeout monitoring and enter degraded modes.

A control frame has the highest VLAN priority. Why can it still be delayed by a software download?Intermediate

Priority only decides which queue is served next. If a 1500-byte download frame has just started on the output port, the control frame must wait for it to finish: 123 µs at 100 Mbit/s per hop. Across several hops that adds up. Frame preemption cuts the large frame; a time-aware shaper closes the bulk gate before the control window; or a faster link shrinks the blocking.

If you send every CAN frame over Ethernet as its own frame, what happens to bandwidth?Intermediate

Each small payload is padded to the Ethernet minimum and carries 42 bytes of framing plus IP, UDP or IEEE 1722 headers, so efficiency falls below 10–20 %. A busy CAN bus can then cost several times its own bit rate on the backbone. Gateways therefore collect PDUs into containers or batch tunnelled frames within a time window.

Why do zonal architectures make the network a safety topic?Advanced

Because functions are no longer local: a brake request or a steering signal now crosses zone controllers, switches and the central computer. Latency, availability and integrity of that path become part of safety goals with fault-tolerant time intervals, so the network needs worst-case analysis, E2E protection, policing against babbling nodes, redundancy such as FRER or dual links, and defined degradation when a zone or the centre fails.

Why is almost every automotive data link differential or coaxial?Basic

Grounds differ between ECUs by a volt or more and change with load currents, and the car is full of interference. A differential receiver rejects both as common mode, and balanced pairs or a shield also keep emissions low so the car's radios are not disturbed. Coax contains the field completely. Single-ended links survive only where they are slow with large thresholds, like LIN.

A camera link works on the bench but drops frames in the car when it is hot. Where do you look?Intermediate

At the channel and the power: longer production cable and inline connectors increase loss beyond what the bench had; temperature changes cable loss and the inductors of the power-over-coax filter, which can lose impedance; the vehicle's EMC environment adds disturbance. Read the deserializer's link-margin or eye metrics and error counters at temperature, check the PoC current, and compare with the channel specification.

Why does the value a perception algorithm reads from the network have an age of tens of milliseconds, and how do you deal with it?Advanced

The network adds a few milliseconds, but the producer's cycle (value computed from the last period), gateway routing cycles and the consumer's own processing cycle add up to tens of milliseconds, and the age varies. Carry the measurement timestamp in a common time base and interpolate or extrapolate to the time of use; for tight control loops, align the task schedules so the age is bounded.

Why did the industry move from 12 V to adding 48 V instead of switching everything to 48 V?Intermediate

48 V cuts current by four and resistive losses by sixteen for high-power loads such as starter-generators and electric compressors, while staying under the 60 V shock-protection limit. But thousands of 12 V components, lamps, motors and ECUs exist and are cheap, so most cars add a 48 V net with a DC/DC converter instead of converting everything; full 48 V low-voltage nets appear only in some new designs.

What if an ECU in a parked car never goes to sleep?Basic

If it keeps sending network-management messages, every ECU on that network stays awake, and the quiescent current rises from tens of milliamperes to amperes. The battery can be flat in hours to days. Partial networking limits the damage to the ECUs that are really needed; finding the culprit needs logging of NM traffic in a parked car.

Why is a 360° shield connection better than a pigtail?Intermediate

A shield works only if high-frequency current can flow between it and the housing with very low impedance. A few centimetres of pigtail have tens of nanohenries, tens of ohms at 100 MHz, so the shield current, and with it the shielding effect, is blocked exactly where emissions and immunity problems occur. A 360° connection has almost no inductance.

What if the front zone uplink in the budget example needs to carry a second lidar?Advanced

The front uplink would rise from 44 % to about 60 % of 1 Gbit/s, at or above the usual design limit, with no room for bursts, growth or diagnostics. Options: a 2.5 Gbit/s uplink, a second 1 Gbit/s link (with load balancing by VLAN or stream), or connecting the lidars directly to the central computer, bypassing the zone. Each changes cost, harness and the failure behaviour.
