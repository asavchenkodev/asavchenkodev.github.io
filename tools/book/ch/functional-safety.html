Functional safety, SOTIF and cybersecurity in practice

How the safety and security of a camera function is actually engineered, not just what the standards say: one camera–radar emergency-braking function taken through the item definition, hazard analysis, safety goals and timing, functional and technical safety concepts, ASIL decomposition, safety architectures, FMEA, FTA, FMEDA and dependent-failure analysis, fault injection, freedom from interference, safety-related software and traceability, the SOTIF analysis of triggering conditions and its validation, a threat analysis with attack trees, and finally the safety case that argues the remaining risk is acceptable.

1The big picture: three questions for one camera function

A car brakes on its own because a camera saw a pedestrian. Three different things can make that dangerous. A component can break: a bit flips in memory and the software sees a pedestrian that is not there. The design can be insufficient although nothing is broken: the detector, working exactly as built, misses a person in dark clothes in the rain. Or somebody can attack it: a forged brake message is injected through a compromised infotainment unit. Each question has its own standard, its own analysis method and its own evidence, and the engineering work in an automotive project consists largely of answering all three for every function, in a way that another engineer, an assessor and eventually a court can follow.

1.1Three questions, three standards: a one-page recap

The standards themselves are described in the standards chapter; this chapter is about how the work is done. The short version is enough to orient yourself. ISO 26262 (functional safety, second edition 2018, twelve parts) deals with hazards caused by malfunctions of electrical and electronic systems: random hardware faults and systematic faults such as bugs and wrong requirements. Its central idea is that the risk of a hazard sets an ASIL, and the ASIL sets how rigorous the development must be and how well hardware faults must be detected (Standards 2.1). ISO 21448 (SOTIF, 2022) deals with hazards that arise when nothing is broken but the intended functionality is insufficient: perception limits, unforeseen situations and reasonably foreseeable misuse (Standards 3). ISO/PAS 8800 (2024) adds what is specific to machine-learned components: data as specification, data-set properties and AI-specific safety analysis (Standards 4). ISO/SAE 21434 (2021) is the engineering standard for cybersecurity, and the UN regulations R155 and R156 make a certified cybersecurity management system and software-update management system a condition of type approval (Standards 5).

The three disciplines share a shape. Each starts from a description of the thing being built, rates risk by combining how bad an outcome would be with how likely it is, derives top-level goals, refines them into requirements on architecture elements, verifies them, and argues the result in a structured case. They differ in what "likely" means. In functional safety it is exposure to a driving situation and the controllability of the outcome; in SOTIF it is the frequency of triggering conditions and the residual rate of hazardous behaviour, measured statistically; in cybersecurity it is the feasibility of an attack, which depends on the attacker's effort and changes over the product's life as attack techniques become cheaper.

Why it matters

A camera engineer meets these standards not as documents but as questions in reviews: "what is the diagnostic coverage of your freeze detection?", "which triggering conditions did you test this network against?", "what happens if the calibration in flash is corrupted?", "can the extrinsics be written over the diagnostic interface without authentication?". Knowing the method behind the questions turns them from bureaucracy into design input, and it is the difference between a perception module that can be integrated into a safety-relevant function and one that cannot.

1.2The running example: a camera–radar emergency-braking function

The chapter follows one function from its first description to the release argument. The item, in ISO 26262 language the system or combination of systems that implements a function at vehicle level, is an automatic emergency braking function with pedestrian detection, called AEB from now on. Its intended functionality is simple to state: when a collision with a vehicle or a pedestrian ahead is imminent and the driver does not react, warn the driver and then brake automatically, up to full deceleration of about 10 m/s², to avoid the collision or reduce its speed. It is active between 5 and 150 km/h for vehicle targets and up to 80 km/h for pedestrians. The driver remains responsible for driving; the function is an assistance system (SAE level 0, "active safety").

The item consists of a front camera behind the windscreen with an image sensor, a serializer and a coax link to the central ADAS computer; a front radar, a separate ECU in the bumper that delivers an object list over automotive Ethernet; and the ADAS ECU, which contains a high-performance SoC and a safety microcontroller. The SoC runs the ISP, the detection network and the sensor fusion in a partition with a safety-certified real-time operating system, next to a Linux partition for recording, visualisation and connectivity, both under a hypervisor; the safety microcontroller runs AUTOSAR Classic, makes the final braking decision and sends the brake request. Outside the item, but connected to it, are the brake system (ESC), which executes deceleration requests, the instrument cluster for warnings, the gateway that connects the chassis bus to the rest of the car, and of course the driver and the environment.

The item definition written in a project records exactly this: the function and its operating modes, the elements and their interfaces, the legal and functional requirements, known hazards from similar products, and the assumptions about the neighbours. The assumptions matter more than they appear to. If the analysis assumes that the brake system rejects deceleration requests whose E2E protection fails, or that it ramps pressure at a bounded rate, then the safety of the AEB depends on those assumptions being true, and they become requirements on another item in another company. Most integration problems in safety projects are mismatched assumptions that nobody wrote down.

A second, smaller example appears where it teaches something the AEB does not: the rear-view camera (RVC), whose job is to show the driver what is behind the car while reversing. Its danger is a frozen or stale picture that looks plausible, and it is the classic example for display-path safety mechanisms and hardware metrics (Section 5.4).

Interesting detail

An SoC or a perception software module is usually developed before anyone knows the vehicle it will go into. ISO 26262 calls this a safety element out of context (SEooC): the supplier assumes a context ("the perception output is used for an ASIL B safety goal with a fault-tolerant time of 100 ms"), develops to it, and publishes the assumptions in a safety manual. The integrator must check every assumption of use against the real item. "We use an ASIL B certified SoC" means nothing until the integrator shows that the safety manual's conditions, such as running the periodic self-tests or enabling ECC, are actually met.

1.3The work in order: from item definition to release

The work is often drawn as a V-model (Standards 2.2). For understanding how the analyses feed each other, a flow of work products is more useful. Each box is a document or model that someone writes and someone else reviews; each arrow says that the later product depends on the earlier one, so a change upstream invalidates the downstream evidence until it is checked again.

The order in a real project is roughly this. In the concept phase the item is defined, the HARA (Chapter 2) derives safety goals, and the functional safety concept (Section 3.1) states what the architecture must do to meet them. In parallel the TARA (Chapter 9) derives cybersecurity goals from the same item definition, and the SOTIF specification defines the intended functionality, its operational design domain and its acceptance criteria (Chapter 8). In product development the technical safety concept (Section 3.2) allocates requirements to hardware and software, and the architecture (Chapter 4) is designed with safety mechanisms. The safety analyses (Chapter 5) are run on that architecture, not after it: an FMEA or FTA that finds an unprotected failure path produces a new requirement, and the architecture changes. Verification proves the requirements are met (Chapter 7), validation proves the goals are met at vehicle level, and the safety case (Chapter 10) argues it.

The loops are the point. A first HARA is rough; the architecture reveals new failure modes; the SOTIF analysis reveals a triggering condition that needs a functional change; the TARA reveals that a diagnostic service can rewrite the calibration, which is also a safety problem. A healthy project iterates these artefacts several times before the design freezes, and keeps them alive afterwards because every change request, including a retrained network, re-enters the loop through impact analysis.

Pitfall

Doing the analyses after the design to "produce the documents". An FMEA written after the software is finished can only confirm what exists; it cannot change the architecture any more, so findings are rated away instead of fixed. Assessors recognise such analyses quickly: every failure mode has a mitigation that happens to be exactly what was built, and no analysis ever produced a requirement.

1.4Who does what

A functional safety manager plans and coordinates the safety activities, owns the safety plan and is responsible for the safety case. Safety engineers run the HARA and the analyses together with the people who know the design; an FMEA without the developers is fiction, an FMEA without a safety engineer tends to be incomplete. System architects own the safety concepts and the allocation of requirements. Developers and test engineers implement and verify safety requirements and produce most of the evidence. A cybersecurity manager plays the same role for ISO/SAE 21434. Assessors and auditors, with independence that grows with the ASIL, confirm the work. In a supply chain, the OEM and each supplier sign a development interface agreement (DIA) and a cybersecurity interface agreement that say who produces which work product, so that nothing falls between the companies.

For a computer-vision engineer the touch points are concrete. You provide the failure modes of your component for the FMEA (what can your detector output that is wrong, and how would anyone notice). You implement monitors and plausibility checks that become safety mechanisms with claimed diagnostic coverage. You own a large part of the SOTIF evidence: the triggering-condition list, the data coverage of the operational design domain, the per-condition performance. You write safety-related code to the coding guidelines and get it to the required structural coverage. And you are part of the change process: a new network version is a change to a safety-related element and needs an impact analysis before release.

Talk track

"Functional safety asks what happens when something breaks, SOTIF asks whether the function is good enough when nothing is broken, and cybersecurity asks what an attacker can make it do. All three start from the same item definition, rate risk, derive goals and refine them into requirements on the architecture. The analyses are design tools: they are run on the architecture while it can still change, and each finding becomes a requirement or a design change. At the end a safety case argues, with evidence, that the remaining risk is acceptable."

2HARA: from what can go wrong to safety goals

The hazard analysis and risk assessment is where an item's safety story begins and where its ASILs are decided. It looks only at the vehicle level: not why the camera could fail, but what the vehicle could do wrong, in which situations that would hurt someone, and how bad, how frequent and how avoidable the harm would be. Everything later in the project inherits its rigour from this table, so it is worth understanding how the rows are produced and where the judgement calls are.

2.1Malfunctioning behaviour: guidewords on functions

The HARA starts from the functions in the item definition and asks how each can deviate. The systematic way is a function-level HAZOP: apply guidewords to each function and write down the resulting malfunctioning behaviour at the vehicle level. The causes are deliberately ignored at this stage; whether a phantom brake comes from a bit flip, a bug or a corrupted calibration is a question for the later analyses. The HARA also assumes that no safety mechanism of the item exists yet, because the point is to find out how much protection is needed.

The interesting malfunction of an active-safety function is often the commission, not the omission: a vehicle without AEB is legal and drivable, so a missing intervention returns the car to the state of a car without the function, while an unintended full brake at motorway speed creates a hazard that did not exist before. And the malfunctions are described in terms that can later be measured at the vehicle: deceleration, duration, speed. A malfunction such as "wrong object detected" is not a vehicle-level behaviour and does not belong in the HARA.

2.2Situations: where a malfunction becomes a hazardous event

A malfunction is only dangerous in certain situations. An unintended full brake on an empty road is uncomfortable; the same brake on a motorway with a car following at a short distance can cause a rear-end collision. The HARA therefore combines each malfunction with operational situations, drawn from a situation catalogue that describes road type, speed range, traffic, weather and road condition, and manoeuvre. Each combination that can cause harm is a hazardous event, and it is the hazardous event, not the malfunction, that is rated.

Situation catalogues are large, and a full HARA for an ADAS function easily has a few hundred rows. Most of them are quickly rated QM or merged; the work is in the dozen that drive the ASILs. Two rules keep the analysis honest. First, the situations must be described independently of the function's design, from the vehicle's use: the catalogue should be the same whether the AEB is built from cameras or lidars. Second, they must not be over-split. Rating "motorway, 130 km/h, following car, rain, night" separately from "motorway, 130 km/h, following car, dry, day" lowers the exposure of each slice and can lower the ASIL, although the hazard is the same; the standard warns against this, and assessors look for it.

Numbers

Exposure is rated either by the share of operating time spent in the situation or by how often it occurs. By duration: E2 below 1% of the average operating time, E3 between 1% and 10%, E4 above 10%. By frequency: E3 occurs once a month or more often for an average driver, E4 during almost every drive, E2 a few times a year, E1 less often than that. Motorway driving with a following vehicle is E4 by either measure for most vehicle types. Duration is used when the hazard needs the situation to last (the malfunction happens while you are in it); frequency when the situation itself triggers the malfunction, such as a function that only acts while parking.

2.3Rating severity, exposure and controllability

Severity rates the injuries a hazardous event could cause to the people involved, including occupants of other vehicles and pedestrians: S0 no injuries, S1 light and moderate injuries, S2 severe and life-threatening injuries with survival probable, S3 life-threatening injuries with survival uncertain, or fatal. It is based on accident research, typically on the expected collision type and speed difference; the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) gives the vocabulary. A rear-end collision at a moderate speed difference with both occupants belted is usually S1 or S2; a pedestrian struck at 50 km/h is S3.

Exposure rates how often the operational situation occurs (Section 2.2), not how often the malfunction occurs. The probability of the fault itself is deliberately excluded from the HARA; it is handled later by the hardware metrics and by the rigour that the ASIL demands.

Controllability rates whether the persons at risk, usually the driver, but also the driver of the following car or the pedestrian, can avoid the harm by timely reactions: C0 controllable in general, C1 simply controllable (99% or more of drivers manage), C2 normally controllable (90% or more), C3 difficult to control or uncontrollable (fewer than 90%). For a sudden full brake at speed, the hazard is mostly to the follower, who is not warned and has a normal reaction time; for a brake jerk of a few hundred milliseconds, almost everyone copes. Controllability claims of C1 or C2 should be backed by evidence, for example driving-simulator or test-track studies with naive drivers; the RESPONSE 3 code of practice for ADAS describes how such studies are run.

Interesting detail

HE3 shows the most powerful lever in a HARA: limiting the authority of a function. If the AEB may only produce a short brake jerk until a second, independent confirmation is available, the hazardous event "unintended braking" is split by magnitude and duration, and only the full braking keeps a high ASIL. Many production AEB systems use exactly this: a warning jerk or partial braking on lower confidence, full braking only when everything agrees. The authority limits then become part of the safety goal and must themselves be guaranteed with the goal's ASIL.

2.4Safety goals and safe states

The hazardous events are merged into a short list of safety goals, top-level safety requirements at vehicle level, each with the highest ASIL of the events it covers. A good safety goal states what must be avoided, at the vehicle level, with measurable thresholds, and says nothing about how. It also names a safe state, an operating mode without unreasonable risk that the item goes to when it cannot fulfil the goal, and the fault-tolerant time interval within which it must get there.

Three things are deliberately absent. There is no safety goal for missed braking (HE4 is QM): the function's ability to brake for pedestrians is its intended performance, handled by SOTIF and by rating tests, not a functional-safety goal. There is no mention of cameras or radars: the goals are about the vehicle. And the thresholds of HE3 appear inside SG1: deceleration below 4 m/s² for at most 300 ms is permitted even when unjustified, which gives the architecture room for a low-confidence warning brake.

The safe state for SG1 is "do not brake", which is easy to reach: switching off is always possible, and it is safe because the driver is in control. This makes the AEB a fail-safe (more precisely fail-silent) system. An automated driving function at level 3 or higher has no such safe state, because nobody is driving when it switches off; it must be fail-operational long enough to reach a minimal-risk condition, which needs redundancy in sensing, computing, power and actuation. That one difference, whether "off" is safe, explains most of the cost difference between driver assistance and automated driving architectures.

Pitfall

Safety goals written as solutions: "the camera shall detect pedestrians with 99.9% recall" or "the SoC shall have ECC". The first is a SOTIF performance target, the second a technical safety requirement. A goal that names the solution cannot be challenged by the analysis, because it has already decided the architecture, and it cannot be validated at vehicle level. Ask of every goal: could I test this on the vehicle without knowing how it is built?

2.5Timing: FTTI, detection and reaction

A safety mechanism that detects a fault after the car has already braked is useless. ISO 26262 therefore attaches a time budget to each safety goal and to each mechanism. The fault-tolerant time interval (FTTI) is the minimum time from the occurrence of a fault to the possible occurrence of a hazardous event if no safety mechanism acts. Within it, the fault detection time interval (FDTI) is the time a mechanism needs to notice the fault, and the fault reaction time interval (FRTI) is the time to reach the safe state or an emergency operation afterwards. Their sum, the fault handling time interval, must be shorter than the FTTI. For a fail-operational system, an emergency operation tolerance time interval (EOTTI) bounds how long the degraded mode may last before a further fault could become dangerous.

The FTTI is a property of the vehicle and the hazard, not of the electronics, and it is determined by analysis and by vehicle tests: how long can an unintended deceleration of a given magnitude last before a typical follower can no longer avoid the collision? The number here, 300 ms, is the same threshold that appears in SG1, which is no coincidence: the safety goal permits what is controllable, and the FTTI is the time at which it stops being controllable. Once the FTTI is known, the technical safety concept splits it into budgets for detection, communication and reaction, and each mechanism inherits a requirement such as "detect a frozen camera stream within 100 ms". A mechanism that runs every 500 ms cannot meet that requirement however good its coverage; the diagnostic test interval is part of the design.

There is a second, slower clock for faults that are not dangerous on their own. A broken watchdog is harmless until the software it supervises hangs; such latent faults are found by tests at start-up, at shutdown or periodically, and the time between those tests (the multiple-point fault detection interval, often one drive cycle) enters the hardware metrics of Section 5.4.

In practice

Write the timing chain down end to end with real numbers, including the parts nobody owns: sensor frame period, debouncing in the monitor (most mechanisms wait for two or three bad samples to avoid false alarms), task periods on the MCU, bus cycle times, the receiver's E2E timeout, the brake system's pressure release. Debouncing is where budgets usually break: a freeze check that needs three repeated frames at 30 fps already uses 100 ms before anything else has happened.

2.6What goes wrong in HARAs

Assessors see the same weaknesses again and again. Situations are rated with safety mechanisms already in place ("the driver will notice because the system warns"), which double-counts protection. Controllability is claimed at C1 or C2 without evidence. Exposure is lowered by slicing situations. Reasonably foreseeable misuse is ignored: drivers do rely on assistance functions after a few months, which is exactly why the HARA must consider the driver's actual behaviour, not the one in the owner's manual. And the hazardous events of interacting functions are forgotten: the AEB's brake request meets the adaptive cruise control's acceleration request in the brake system's arbitration, and a malfunction in that arbitration is a hazard of neither function alone.

Talk track

"The HARA looks at the vehicle, not the electronics. We list what the function can do wrong, place it in driving situations, and rate severity, exposure of the situation and controllability. For an AEB the dangerous malfunction is the unintended full brake at motorway speed, which typically comes out at ASIL C or D depending on the severity rationale; the missed brake is usually QM because the driver is still responsible, and its performance is handled by SOTIF. Limiting the authority, a short low-deceleration jerk before full braking, is the strongest lever to reduce the ASIL. Each safety goal gets a safe state and a fault-tolerant time interval, here 300 ms, which then becomes the budget for every detection and reaction mechanism."

3Safety concepts: from goals to requirements on hardware and software

A safety goal says what the vehicle must not do. Nobody can implement "avoid unintended deceleration above 4 m/s²" directly; it has to be turned into requirements on the elements of the architecture, first functionally (what must the system do to meet the goal) and then technically (which mechanism, in which hardware or software, with which parameters). This refinement is where the architecture of a safety-relevant function is really decided, and where ASIL decomposition lets a team put the expensive rigour only where it is needed.

3.1The functional safety concept

The functional safety concept (FSC) derives functional safety requirements (FSRs) from each safety goal and allocates them to the elements of a preliminary architecture. It answers, without yet naming technologies: how are faults detected, how is the safe state reached, which degraded modes exist, which warnings does the driver get, and what must the neighbouring items do. Each FSR inherits the ASIL of its goal unless it is decomposed.

The other FSRs are the generic skeleton that almost every safety concept has: detect faults in the inputs, limit what the function can do while unsure, protect the output, and define what happens when protection fails. Writing the assumption A-1 explicitly is what makes the concept checkable: it becomes a line in the DIA with the brake-system supplier, and their safety manual must confirm it.

Pitfall

Requirements that cannot be verified because they promise perfection: "the camera shall never output a phantom object". No test can show that. The verifiable version names a mechanism and a bound: "a camera object shall only be used if its frame counter and timestamp are consistent and its age is below 100 ms". What the camera channel cannot guarantee, the architecture must compensate, which is exactly what FSR-1 does with the second channel.

3.2The technical safety concept

The technical safety concept (TSC) turns each FSR into technical safety requirements (TSRs) on the system architecture, decides which are met in hardware and which in software, specifies the safety mechanisms with their parameters, and splits the timing budgets. It also defines the hardware–software interface: which registers, error flags, interrupts and self-tests the software uses to supervise the hardware. For the AEB, a part of the TSC looks like this:

The perception software itself has almost no technical safety requirement in this concept apart from delivering correctly stamped, protected outputs. That is not because perception does not matter, but because the architecture has been arranged so that a perception fault alone cannot cause the hazard of SG1. The performance of perception, which certainly matters for whether the AEB brakes for real pedestrians, is the SOTIF question of Chapter 8.

3.3ASIL decomposition

Developing everything to ASIL C is expensive, and for a deep neural network on a GPU it is not even clear what that would mean. ASIL decomposition (ISO 26262-9, clause 5) allows a safety requirement to be implemented by two redundant and sufficiently independent requirements of lower ASIL, provided each of them alone is able to prevent the violation of the goal. The allowed splits are fixed:

OriginalAllowed decompositions
ASIL DC(D) + A(D) · B(D) + B(D) · D(D) + QM(D)
ASIL CB(C) + A(C) · C(C) + QM(C)
ASIL BA(B) + A(B) · B(B) + QM(B)
ASIL AA(A) + QM(A)

The letter in brackets records the original ASIL and carries real obligations. Decomposition reduces the rigour of the development of each part (methods, reviews, coverage), but the hardware metrics are still evaluated against the original goal's targets, the integration and the safety mechanisms that combine the parts stay at the original ASIL, and the independence of the two parts must be shown by a dependent failure analysis (Section 5.5). Two "independent" channels that share a power supply, a clock, a memory, the same software library or the same timestamp source are not independent.

The decomposition works for SG1 because of the direction of the hazard. An unintended full brake requires both channels to report a collision-relevant object that does not exist, at the same place and time; each channel alone can veto the brake. For the opposite question, braking when a pedestrian really is there, the AND makes things worse: the AEB now misses the pedestrian whenever either channel misses it. Decomposition is directional. The same two sensors combined with an OR would protect availability and multiply phantom brakes. In a safety concept that is fine, because missed braking is not a functional-safety goal here, but the price is paid in SOTIF performance (Section 8.5 lets you see it).

The choice of channels also follows from what each can be developed to. Radar ECUs with ASIL B object lists are common products, and the safety MCU logic is small enough to develop to ASIL C or D. The camera channel at B(C) means its software (drivers, ISP control, inference runtime, post-processing, fusion interface) is developed with ASIL B methods; the trained network's behaviour is not something ASIL methods can establish, which is acceptable because the analysis shows that the network alone cannot violate SG1.

Interesting detail

A tempting alternative is a QM(C) camera channel plus a C(C) "checker" that validates the camera's objects without a second sensor, for example by checking that a pedestrian's bounding box touches the road plane at a plausible distance. Such checks catch some faults (corrupted object lists, wildly implausible geometry) but cannot tell a real pedestrian from a phantom produced by a faulty network or a corrupted weight: both are geometrically plausible. A checker can only be the second leg of a decomposition if it can, by itself, prevent the violation of the goal. That usually needs independent information about the world, which is another sensor.

3.4Good safety requirements and their traceability

Safety requirements are read by people who were not in the room: testers, assessors, the supplier who inherits them, and the engineer who changes the code three years later. ISO 26262-8 lists the properties they must have: unambiguous and comprehensible, atomic, internally consistent, feasible, verifiable, and each with a unique identifier, a status and an ASIL. In practice that means one requirement per statement, numbers with units, defined terms ("collision-relevant object" must be defined somewhere), and a verification method chosen when the requirement is written, not when the test campaign starts.

Every requirement is linked upward to the requirement it refines and downward to the design elements that implement it and the tests that verify it. Those links are not paperwork: they are how the project answers two questions it will be asked constantly. "Is every safety goal fully refined, implemented and tested?" follows the links downward and looks for gaps. "What does this change affect?" follows them upward from a changed piece of code. Section 7.4 shows a full trace chain for one mechanism; the process side of traceability is covered in the ASPICE chapter (ASPICE 10.3).

"From each safety goal we derive functional safety requirements, which say what the architecture must do, and then technical safety requirements, which say which mechanism in which hardware or software does it, with timing budgets from the fault-tolerant time interval. For the AEB we decompose the ASIL C goal into a camera channel at B(C) and a radar channel at A(C), because an unintended brake needs both to report the same phantom. 4Safety architectures and mechanisms

A safety concept becomes real in the architecture. The same handful of patterns appear in almost every safety-relevant automotive system: something does the work and something simpler checks it, important data travel with their own protection, a watchdog checks that the checker is alive, and the system knows which degraded modes it can fall back to. Recognising the patterns makes any safety architecture readable within minutes.

4.1What an architecture must do with a fault

Every safety mechanism does two things: it detects a fault or its effect, and it controls it by moving the system into a safe state or a degraded mode before the FTTI expires. A system that detects but does not react, for example one that writes a diagnostic trouble code and carries on, has a diagnostic, not a safety mechanism. What the reaction can be depends on the safe state. A fail-safe or fail-silent system, like the AEB, switches the function off. A fail-degraded system continues with reduced capability, such as an AEB that only warns. A fail-operational system continues its full or a minimal function after a fault, which needs redundancy all the way to the actuator and is the domain of automated driving and of steer- and brake-by-wire.

The mechanisms also differ by which faults they address. Random hardware faults are handled by detection mechanisms whose effectiveness is quantified as diagnostic coverage: the share of a component's dangerous failure rate that the mechanism detects. ISO 26262-5 Annex D rates typical mechanisms as low (60%), medium (90%) or high (99%) coverage. Systematic faults, bugs and specification errors, cannot be quantified this way; they are prevented by process and tolerated by diverse redundancy and by monitors that check results rather than implementations.

4.2Doer and checker: the three-level monitoring concept

The most widely used pattern in automotive safety is the separation of a complex doer from a simple checker. It became a de-facto standard through the E-Gas monitoring concept, written by German manufacturers for electronic throttle control in the 1990s and still the template for many functions. It has three levels. Level 1 is the function itself, as complex as it needs to be, developed to QM or a low ASIL. Level 2 is the function monitor: an independent, simplified calculation that checks whether level 1's output is plausible and can force the safe state. It runs in protected memory and is developed to the goal's ASIL. Level 3 is the controller monitor: it checks that the hardware running level 2 still executes correctly, typically by a question–answer exchange with an independent watchdog device that sends questions whose correct answers require the level-2 core to run its instruction tests and program flow correctly. If level 3 fails, the watchdog device can cut the output on its own.

The pattern works because it moves complexity out of the safety-critical path. Level 1 may contain a neural network, millions of lines of Linux and a GPU driver, all of which are impossible to develop to ASIL C. Level 2 contains a few thousand lines of C on a lockstep MCU, which is entirely possible. The price is that the checker must be able to judge the doer's result with less computation, and this is where engineering judgement goes. For throttle control, level 2 recomputes the permissible torque from pedal position with a simplified model. For the AEB, it cannot recompute perception, so it checks the braking decision against an independent sensor and bounds what an unconfirmed decision may do.

Pitfall

A checker that shares the doer's inputs or code. If level 2 takes the camera object list from the same buffer as level 1 and uses the same library to compute time to collision, a corrupted buffer or a library bug fools both. Independence must be designed in: separate input paths, separate memory, preferably a different algorithm or implementation, and an analysis that shows what is still shared.

4.3Redundancy and voting

Redundant channels are described by how many must agree: "M out of N" (MooN). A 1oo1D system is a single channel with diagnostics. In 1oo2 either of two channels can trigger the safety action, which is good for safety and bad for availability, since a spurious trip of either stops the system. In 2oo2 both must agree, the reverse trade-off. 2oo3 majority voting tolerates one faulty channel in both directions and is the classic fail-operational structure, at three times the hardware. The AEB's decision is 2oo2 for "brake" and therefore 1oo2 for "do not brake": either channel can veto. Naming the direction is essential, because the same structure is safe for one hazard and weak for the opposite one.

4.4End-to-end protection of communication

Between the camera, the SoC, the MCU and the brake system, safety-relevant data cross buffers, drivers, network stacks, switches and gateways, none of which are developed to ASIL C. The timing must fit into the FTTI budget: with a 10 ms cycle and a requirement of three consecutive missing messages, the receiver needs 30 ms to declare a timeout.

The data ID is never transmitted. This catches masquerade caused by faults. It does not stop an attacker, who can compute a CRC as easily as the sender; that is why safety-relevant messages that an attacker could reach also carry a cryptographic message authentication code (Section 9.6).

4.6Watchdogs and program-flow monitoring

Software can fail by stopping, by running too slowly, or by running the wrong path. A simple watchdog timer that must be retriggered before it expires catches only the first. A windowed watchdog must be retriggered neither too late nor too early, which also catches a task that runs far too often, for example in a tight error loop. A question–answer watchdog sends a question and expects an answer that the software can only compute by executing a defined sequence of checks, so it also catches a processor that runs but computes wrongly. The watchdog should be outside the supervised processor, typically in the power-management IC, with its own clock and the authority to reset the MCU or disable an output.

On the software side, AUTOSAR's watchdog manager supervises individual functions with three kinds of checks. Alive supervision counts checkpoints of a periodic task and detects tasks that run too rarely or too often. Deadline supervision measures the time between two checkpoints, for example from "object list received" to "decision sent", and detects a computation that takes too long. Logical supervision checks that checkpoints are passed in an allowed order, which detects corrupted control flow. Only if all supervised entities are healthy does the manager trigger the hardware watchdog. In the AEB, deadline supervision on the decision path is what guarantees the detection budget of Figure 2.1; a decision computed correctly but 200 ms late is a fault.

4.7Degradation modes

Switching the whole function off at the first fault is safe but makes the AEB unavailable more than necessary, which has its own safety cost: a car that often has no AEB protects nobody. A designed set of degradation modes keeps as much function as the remaining channels can safely support, and every mode must be consistent with every safety goal.

Two design rules matter. Every downward transition must be fast and certain, because it is the safety reaction; upward transitions should be slow and conservative, because a function that flips between modes confuses the driver and can create its own hazards. And the driver must be told what the car can still do: an AEB that silently runs in camera-only warning mode for months is a reasonably foreseeable misuse waiting to happen, because the driver still believes it will brake.

5Safety analyses: FMEA, FTA, FMEDA, DFA and fault injection

Safety analyses are structured ways of attacking your own design before the world does. An FMEA asks, component by component, "what if this fails?"; a fault tree asks, from the hazard downwards, "how could this happen?"; an FMEDA puts numbers on hardware faults and the mechanisms that catch them; a dependent-failure analysis asks whether the redundancy you rely on is real. Fault injection then checks that the mechanisms do what the analyses claimed. Each one produces requirements, and that is how you know it was done while it still mattered.

5.1Inductive and deductive: two directions of thinking

Inductive analyses go bottom-up: start from the failure modes of each element and follow their effects upward to the vehicle. They are good at completeness over the parts list, because each element is visited, and weak at combinations, because each failure is considered alone. The FMEA is the standard inductive method. Deductive analyses go top-down: start from an undesired event, typically the violation of a safety goal, and decompose it into the combinations of causes that can produce it. They are good at combinations and dependencies, and they show directly which single failures lead to the hazard. The FTA is the standard deductive method. ISO 26262 requires inductive analysis at all ASILs and recommends deductive analysis in addition, highly so for ASIL C and D; the two complement each other, and a mismatch between them is often the most valuable finding.

Both can be qualitative, identifying failure paths and their mitigations, or quantitative, attaching failure rates and computing probabilities. The quantitative versions are only meaningful for random hardware faults, whose rates can be estimated from reliability data; systematic faults have no meaningful rate and are analysed qualitatively. An analysis of software is therefore always qualitative.

5.2FMEA: following failure modes upward

Automotive FMEAs follow the AIAG and VDA handbook (2019) in seven steps: plan the scope, build the structure tree (system, subsystems, components), attach functions to each element, derive failures as a chain of effect, mode and cause across levels, rate the risk, optimise with actions, and document the results. The failure chain is the core idea: a cause at one level ("weight memory bit flip") is a mode of the level below and produces a mode at the level above ("phantom object in the object list"), whose effect at the vehicle is the hazard ("unintended braking"). Each chain is rated for severity of the effect, occurrence of the cause given the prevention measures, and detection of the cause or mode by the planned controls, each from 1 to 10. The handbook's action priority (high, medium, low) is looked up from the three ratings and replaced the older risk priority number, which multiplied them and treated a severity of 10 with good detection like a much lower severity.

Pitfall

Rating detection by the existence of a test in the development process ("we have unit tests, D = 2"). In a design FMEA, detection controls for the product in the field are the safety mechanisms; development tests reduce occurrence of design causes. Mixing the two makes every row look well detected. Be explicit about which control acts where.

5.3FTA: from the hazard down to cut sets

A fault tree starts from one top event, here the violation of SG1: "AEB brake request above 4 m/s² for longer than 300 ms without a collision-relevant object". Below it, gates describe how lower events combine: an OR gate if any input suffices, an AND gate if all inputs must occur together. The leaves are basic events: component failures, software faults, external conditions. The tree is drawn until the basic events are ones for which a failure rate or a mitigation can be stated.

The qualitative result is the list of minimal cut sets: the smallest combinations of basic events that cause the top event. A cut set of size one is a single-point failure and must be protected to the goal's ASIL; cut sets of size two need the independence of their events to be shown, which is the job of the DFA. Quantitatively, an OR gate of rare events adds their rates; an AND gate of two independent faults that are each present for an exposure time \(T\) before being detected or repaired has a rate of approximately \(\lambda_1 \lambda_2 T\). Common-cause failures are often modelled with a beta factor: a fraction \(\beta\) of a channel's failures is assumed to hit both channels at once, so the pair contributes \(\beta\lambda\) instead of a product. What matters is what is left: the common cause (β times the smaller channel's undetected rate, 0.2 FIT) and above all the non-redundant decision element, whose 100 FIT with 99% lockstep coverage leave 1 FIT. The total, about 1.2 FIT, is well inside the target.

The general lesson is one of the most useful in safety engineering: once you have redundancy, the risk is in what the channels share and in the element that combines them. Raising β from 2% to 10% doubles the total; improving both channels' coverage to 99% changes almost nothing. Effort spent on the dependent-failure analysis and on the voter is worth far more than effort spent on making redundant channels individually better.

5.4FMEDA: putting numbers on hardware faults

The FMEDA (failure modes, effects and diagnostic analysis) is the quantitative inductive analysis of the hardware. For each hardware element it takes a failure rate from a reliability database or the semiconductor vendor, splits it into failure modes, decides for each mode whether it can violate the safety goal, and assigns the diagnostic coverage of the safety mechanism that detects it. From this it computes the three hardware metrics of ISO 26262-5, whose targets depend on the ASIL (Standards 2.3): the single-point fault metric (SPFM, the share of faults that are safe or detected), the latent-fault metric (LFM, the share of multiple-point faults that do not stay hidden) and the probabilistic metric for random hardware failures (PMHF, the residual rate of violating the goal).

Failure rates come from databases such as IEC 61709 or SN 29500, from the vendor's own data and, for semiconductors, from the transient soft-error rate, which for large SRAMs and logic often exceeds the permanent rate. ISO 26262-11 explains how to do this for integrated circuits, and SoC vendors deliver an FMEDA for their part that the integrator combines with the board-level one.

5.5Dependent failure analysis

Redundancy and decomposition rest on independence, and the dependent failure analysis (DFA, ISO 26262-9 clause 7) is where independence is argued. It looks for two kinds of dependency. A common-cause failure is a single root cause that makes several elements fail: a supply overvoltage that disturbs both sensing channels. A cascading failure is a failure of one element that causes another to fail: a runaway QM task that overwrites the safety task's memory. The method is to list the coupling factors through which elements can influence each other and check each for each pair of elements that must be independent.

5.6Software safety analysis and fault injection

Software has no failure rate, but its architecture can still be analysed. The software safety analysis of ISO 26262-6 applies guidewords to the data flow and control flow between software components ("value too high", "too late", "never", "corrupted", "wrong order") and asks for each deviation whether it can violate a safety requirement and what detects it. Typical outputs are requirements for range checks on interfaces, for deadline supervision, and, most often, evidence needs for freedom from interference: if a QM component can write into an ASIL component's data, the analysis records it and the architecture must prevent it (Chapter 6).

Every number in the FMEDA and every detection time in the TSC is a claim, and fault injection is how claims become evidence. A fault is introduced deliberately, at the lowest practical level, and the test checks that the intended mechanism detects it within its detection interval, that the system reaches the safe state within the fault-tolerant time, and that no other mechanism fires spuriously. Injection is done at several levels because each has blind spots: in simulation models of the hardware (complete, but slow and only as good as the model), in software (fast, but cannot reach every hardware fault), through the hardware's own injection features (ECC error injection registers, SerDes test modes, lockstep comparator test inputs), and on the HIL bench and in the vehicle (realistic, but limited to what can be provoked from outside).

6Freedom from interference, partitioning and fault containment

Modern ECUs put software of different ASILs on the same chip, often on the same cores: the ASIL B(C) camera channel next to QM recording and connectivity, the ASIL C decision next to a QM diagnostic stack. ISO 26262 allows this only if the lower-integrity software cannot disturb the higher-integrity software. Arguing this freedom from interference is one of the most technical parts of a safety case, because it reaches down to memory controllers and cache policies.

6.1Why it is needed, and the three kinds of interference

The rule in ISO 26262-9 is simple: when elements of different ASILs coexist, either all are developed to the highest ASIL, or it must be shown that the lower ones cannot cause a cascading failure of the higher ones. Developing a Linux-based recording stack to ASIL B is not realistic, so the second route is the normal one, and ISO 26262-6 Annex D lists what must be prevented. Timing and execution: blocking of execution, deadlocks and livelocks, wrong allocation of execution time, wrong synchronisation. Memory: corruption of content, and reading or writing memory allocated to another element. Exchange of information: the communication faults of Section 4.4, now between software components rather than ECUs.

Note the direction. Freedom from interference protects higher-ASIL elements from lower ones. A QM component that is disturbed by an ASIL component is not a safety problem, and a QM component that only reads safety data cannot harm it, unless reading itself has side effects such as locking a shared resource for too long.

6.2Spatial isolation: who may touch which memory

On a microcontroller, the memory protection unit (MPU) defines a handful of regions with access rights, and the AUTOSAR OS reprograms it whenever it switches between OS-applications, so that a QM task trying to write into the decision's data triggers a protection fault, which the OS handles by terminating or restarting the offending application. On application processors the MMU does the same per process with page tables, and a hypervisor adds a second translation stage (stage 2), so that a guest operating system cannot map physical memory it was not given even if its kernel is compromised (Linux 1.2, Embedded compute 6.6).

The gap that is easy to miss is DMA. The ISP, the GPU, the NPU, the Ethernet controller and the video input unit are bus masters that read and write memory on their own, without the CPU's MMU. A misconfigured or faulty QM GPU job can overwrite the camera channel's buffers in physical memory, below every software protection. The SMMU (IOMMU) gives each bus master its own translation and permissions, and interconnect firewalls on safety-oriented SoCs restrict which master may access which address ranges. Without them, spatial freedom from interference on a heterogeneous SoC cannot be argued. ECC on memories completes the picture for random faults: it does not stop a rogue writer, but it stops a bit flip from silently becoming data.

6.3Temporal isolation: who may use how much time

Temporal interference is the harder dimension, and the reason is shared hardware that no partitioning scheme owns. On the MCU it is manageable: the AUTOSAR OS's timing protection monitors each task's execution budget, its minimum inter-arrival time and how long it holds locks or disables interrupts, and stops a task that exceeds them; static schedules with fixed time slots make the timing analysable. On a multicore SoC, two tasks on different cores still share the last-level cache, the interconnect and above all DRAM bandwidth. A QM task streaming large buffers can evict the safety task's working set and saturate the memory controller, and a safety task that meets its deadline comfortably in isolation can become several times slower under such load, although no software ever touched its time budget.

The measures attack each shared resource. Cache partitioning, either by hardware (Arm's memory system resource partitioning and monitoring, MPAM, on newer cores) or by page colouring in the hypervisor, gives each partition its own share of the cache. Memory bandwidth regulation limits how many DRAM accesses a core or a bus master may issue per period, in hardware QoS regulators or by hypervisor-level throttling based on performance counters. Time partitioning in the hypervisor gives each guest fixed CPU windows. And the remaining interference is measured: worst-case execution times of the safety tasks are determined under deliberate worst-case load from the QM side, generated with stress tools on every shared resource, and the budget includes that margin. cgroups and CPU isolation on Linux are useful for performance but do not make this argument on their own (Linux 3.7, Build and containers 10.4).

Pitfall

"The safety task runs on its own core, so there is no interference." Its own core shares the L3 cache, the interconnect, the DRAM controller, the power and clock domains and often interrupts with every other core. A dedicated core removes scheduling interference only. Without cache and bandwidth measures and a measured WCET under load, the claim does not hold on any modern SoC.

6.4Exchange of information and the QM-data problem

Partitions must talk, and every channel between them is a path for interference. Shared memory between guests is set up by the hypervisor with explicit, minimal windows, and the data in it carry E2E protection so that the receiver detects corruption, staleness and repetition just as on a bus. The deeper problem is semantic: when an ASIL component consumes data produced by a QM component, nothing the QM component says can be trusted for safety. The receiver must either treat the data as untrusted input and check it with its own ASIL means (range, plausibility, consistency with independent data), or not use it for a safety decision at all. In the AEB, the safety MCU receives map data and driver-state information from QM sources; it uses them to make braking less likely (suppressing, for example, an intervention while the driver is steering around the obstacle), never to make braking more likely, because a QM fault could then trigger a brake.

6.5Fault containment and the evidence

Containment means that a fault stays in the partition where it occurred and that the system's reaction is proportionate: a crashed QM recording service is restarted while the camera channel continues; a protection fault in the decision partition, by contrast, is a safety event that stops the brake request. The partition design defines these fault containment regions and what happens at their borders, and the safety case argues freedom from interference with three kinds of evidence: an analysis (the software safety analysis and the DFA of shared resources), the hardware and software configuration (MPU and SMMU tables, hypervisor configuration, bandwidth limits, reviewed like code), and tests that try to break it (write attempts across partitions, DMA from QM masters into safety buffers, deliberate overload of every shared resource while measuring the safety tasks' timing).

Talk track

"Mixed criticality on one SoC is allowed if the lower-ASIL software cannot cause a cascading failure in the higher-ASIL software, in three dimensions: memory, timing and communication. Spatially, MPU, MMU and the hypervisor's stage-2 translation, and crucially the SMMU for DMA masters like the GPU and ISP. Temporally, budgets and timing protection on the MCU, and on the SoC cache partitioning, memory-bandwidth regulation and a WCET measured under worst-case QM load, because a dedicated core still shares the cache and DRAM. 7Safety-related software: coding, verification and traceability

Software causes no random faults; every software failure is a systematic fault that was put there by someone and was not found. ISO 26262-6 therefore does not ask for failure rates but for a development process whose rigour grows with the ASIL: how requirements are written, how the architecture is structured, which language subset is used, how units are verified and how thoroughly the tests exercise the code. The engineer's daily experience of functional safety is mostly this chapter.

7.1What ISO 26262-6 asks of software

The software part of the standard follows the right half of the V for software: software safety requirements derived from the technical safety concept, a software architectural design, unit design and implementation, unit verification, integration and verification, and testing of the embedded software on the target. For each step it lists methods in tables, marked per ASIL as highly recommended (++), recommended (+) or without recommendation. A highly recommended method that is not used needs a documented rationale for why the alternative achieves the same; that is what assessors read.

At the architecture level the recommended principles are those of any good design, made mandatory with the ASIL: hierarchical structure, restricted size and complexity of components, restricted size of interfaces, strong cohesion and loose coupling, analysable scheduling, restricted use of interrupts. At the unit level the list becomes concrete: one entry and one exit point per function, no dynamic memory allocation after initialisation (or tested if used), initialised variables, no reuse of names, no unconditional jumps, no recursion, restricted pointers, no implicit type conversions, no hidden data or control flow. Most of this is enforced through a coding guideline for a language subset: MISRA C:2023 for C, and MISRA C++:2023 (which supersedes AUTOSAR C++14) for C++. The rule content belongs to the C++ chapter (C++ 14); here what matters is that the guideline is chosen, deviations are justified one by one in a deviation record, and compliance is checked by tools, not by hope.

Why it matters

The rules exist because each removes a class of faults that testing finds badly: undefined behaviour that works on one compiler and not the next, timing that depends on the heap's state, control flow that no reviewer can follow. A language subset trades expressiveness for analysability, and analysability is what lets static analysis prove, for example, that no division by zero and no out-of-bounds access can occur in the decision code.

7.2Verification: reviews, static analysis and requirements-based tests

Verification of safety-related software combines three kinds of evidence. Reviews (walkthroughs, inspections) check design and code against requirements and guidelines; at higher ASILs they are formal inspections with checklists and recorded findings. Static analysis examines the code without running it: rule checkers for the coding guideline, data- and control-flow analysis, and, for the highest assurance, semantic analysis based on abstract interpretation, which proves the absence of run-time errors such as overflow, division by zero and invalid pointer access for all possible inputs. Dynamic tests run the code: requirements-based tests derived from each software safety requirement, interface tests, fault-injection tests that feed corrupted inputs, resource-usage tests that measure stack, heap and execution time, and back-to-back tests that compare the code with a model or a reference implementation.

Test cases must be derived systematically, not invented: from the requirements, by equivalence classes (one test per class of inputs that the code should treat alike), by boundary values (at and around each threshold, such as an object age of exactly 100 ms), and by error guessing based on experience. A requirement such as "objects older than 100 ms shall be rejected" generates at least tests at 99, 100 and 101 ms, with a stale timestamp, with a timestamp in the future, and with a wrapped clock.

7.3Structural coverage and MC/DC

Requirements-based tests can be incomplete in ways nobody notices: a requirement may be missing, or the code may contain behaviour no requirement asks for. Structural coverage measures which parts of the code the requirements-based tests exercised, and uncovered code is a question: is a requirement missing, is a test missing, or is the code dead? ISO 26262-6 recommends, at unit level, statement coverage (highly recommended for ASIL A and B), branch coverage (highly recommended for B to D) and MC/DC, modified condition/decision coverage (recommended for A to C, highly recommended for D). At the architecture level, function and call coverage show that every function was called and every call was made.

MC/DC requires that every condition in a decision has been shown to independently change the decision's outcome. Take the core of the decision element: brake = confirmed && ttc_low && !override, with three conditions. Branch coverage needs only two tests, one true and one false. Exhaustive testing needs eight. Pitfall

Writing tests to reach coverage instead of deriving them from requirements. Coverage then measures only that the code was executed, not that its behaviour was checked against anything, and the tests faithfully confirm whatever the code does, bugs included. Coverage is an indicator of test completeness for requirements-based tests; on its own it proves nothing.

7.4Traceability: one mechanism from goal to evidence

Bidirectional traceability links every safety requirement to its parent, to the design elements that implement it and to the verification that proves it. Following one mechanism, the camera freeze detection, through the whole chain shows what that means in practice.

Requirement-management tools mark links as suspect when either end changes, and a release is blocked while suspect links on safety requirements exist. The discipline that makes this work is unglamorous: atomic requirements (so a link means something), one owner per requirement, and test results that are linked to the exact software version they were run on. With it, the impact analysis for a change, required by ISO 26262-8 before every change to safety-related elements, becomes a query rather than a meeting.

7.5Tool confidence

A compiler that miscompiles a comparison, a code generator that drops a branch, or a test tool that reports a pass for a failed test can introduce or hide faults in safety-related software. ISO 26262-8 therefore requires a tool confidence level for every software tool used. It is determined from the tool impact (TI1: a malfunction cannot introduce or fail to detect an error; TI2: it can) and the tool error detection (TD1: an error would be detected with high confidence by other measures, TD2 medium, TD3 low). TI1 gives TCL1, which needs nothing further; TI2 with TD1 also gives TCL1; TI2 with TD2 gives TCL2, and TI2 with TD3 gives TCL3. TCL2 and TCL3 tools must be qualified by increased confidence from use, by evaluation of the tool's development process, by validation of the tool, or by development to a safety standard.

The classification is where the thinking is. A compiler has TI2, but if the generated code is tested on the target with requirements-based tests and coverage, many of its errors are detected (TD2 or better). A DNN compiler that quantises and fuses layers has TI2 and, unless the deployed model is compared with the reference model on representative data, poor detection; the comparison of Edge AI 10.5 is what lowers its TCL. The same reasoning applies to AI coding assistants (Generative AI 13.7): treated as a tool, their output's errors are detected by the same reviews, static analysis and tests as human code, which is the argument for using them in a safety project at all.

7.6Machine-learned components: where the method stops

ISO 26262 assumes that a component's behaviour is specified by requirements and realised by design and code, so that reviews, analyses and coverage can establish it. A trained network's behaviour is specified by data and realised by weights. The code around it, from the camera driver to the inference runtime and post-processing, fits ISO 26262 well and is developed to the channel's ASIL; the question "does the network detect pedestrians well enough" does not. The architecture of this chapter is built around that fact: the network sits in a channel that alone cannot violate SG1, random faults in its weights and accelerator are caught by memory protection and by the second channel, and its performance is the subject of SOTIF.

ISO/PAS 8800 fills in what is specific to the AI component (Standards 4, Deep learning 15.3): safety requirements on the model expressed as measurable properties (recall per class and distance band within the ODD, localisation error, calibrated confidence), requirements on the data sets (coverage of the ODD and of the known triggering conditions, label quality, independence of the test set from the training set), an AI-specific safety analysis of the model's failure modes, monitoring in operation, and an assurance argument that ties them together. How training and evaluation are done in practice belongs to the training chapter of the plan; here, what matters is that these properties become the verification evidence for the camera channel's performance requirements in the SOTIF argument.

Talk track

"For software, ISO 26262 asks for rigour, not rates: a coding guideline such as MISRA with justified deviations, design principles like no dynamic allocation and no recursion, static analysis, and requirements-based tests derived by equivalence classes and boundary values. Structural coverage checks the tests are complete, MC/DC for ASIL D, which proves every condition independently affects the decision. Bidirectional traceability turns impact analysis into a query, and every tool gets a confidence level from its impact and how well its errors would be caught. For the neural network, ISO 26262 covers the code around it; its behaviour is handled by the architecture and by SOTIF and ISO/PAS 8800."

8SOTIF in practice: when nothing is broken

Everything so far assumed that the danger comes from a fault. For a perception-based function, most of the real risk comes from somewhere else: a healthy camera, a correct implementation and a well-trained network that still brakes for a bridge shadow or misses a child stepping out between parked cars. ISO 21448 is the method for finding these limitations, reducing them, and showing with statistics that what remains is acceptable. For a computer-vision engineer it is the most important safety standard, because most of its evidence is perception evidence.

8.1The method in one picture

The SOTIF view of the world is the four-area picture of the standards chapter (Standards 3.2): scenarios are known or unknown to the developers, and safe or unsafe for the function. The work moves scenarios out of the unsafe areas: known unsafe scenarios (area 2) become safe through functional modifications, unknown unsafe ones (area 3) are discovered and become known. The clauses of ISO 21448 follow that logic: specify the function and its ODD, identify and evaluate hazards of the intended functionality, identify functional insufficiencies and triggering conditions, modify the function, define a verification and validation strategy, evaluate known and unknown scenarios, argue that the SOTIF is achieved, and keep monitoring in operation.

The vocabulary is precise and worth using correctly. A functional insufficiency is either a specification insufficiency (the specification does not cover a situation, for example it never says what to do with a pedestrian partly hidden by a parked van) or a performance insufficiency (the specification is right, but the technical capability is not, for example the detector's recall at night). A triggering condition is a specific condition of a scenario that activates an insufficiency: low sun, a wet road at night, a mannequin in a shop window. Their combination produces hazardous behaviour of the vehicle, which in a hazardous situation causes harm.

8.2Specification, hazards and acceptance criteria

SOTIF starts from a specification that ISO 26262 does not need in the same detail: what the function is meant to do, in which operational design domain (speed range, road types, weather and lighting in which it is offered), with which sensors and known limits, and what the driver is told about it. The ODD is a design decision with safety consequences: offering pedestrian AEB at night is valuable, and it brings night-time triggering conditions into scope.

The hazard analysis reuses the vehicle-level hazards of the HARA, but asks whether the intended functionality can cause them. Unjustified full braking (the same vehicle behaviour as HE1, now with nothing broken: the detector really "saw" a pedestrian in a shadow and the radar really saw something metal there) is the main SOTIF hazard of an AEB. Missed braking is the second: it is not a malfunction, but drivers who rely on the function will be hurt by its gaps, and reasonably foreseeable misuse is in scope.

For each hazard, the team sets acceptance criteria: quantitative statements of how often the hazardous behaviour may occur. A typical way to derive them compares with the risk that already exists in traffic. Normal traffic produces a rear-end collision roughly every few million kilometres, in the order of \(4\times10^{-7}\) per km. If the team assumes that one unjustified full brake in a hundred causes a rear-end collision, an acceptance criterion of at most one unjustified full brake per \(10^7\) km (AC1) adds about \(10^{-9}\) collisions per km, a fraction of a percent of the existing rear-end risk. For missed braking the criterion is a performance target, here at least 90% of AEB-relevant pedestrian scenarios in the ODD avoided or mitigated (AC2), and it is evaluated per triggering condition, not only on average.

Interesting detail

Customer acceptance often sets a tighter limit than safety. A phantom brake that causes no collision still frightens the driver, makes headlines and leads to the function being switched off. Production AEB systems are therefore usually tuned well below any safety-derived criterion for false activations, and the safety argument inherits that margin.

8.3Triggering-condition analysis

The heart of SOTIF is a systematic search for triggering conditions. Brainstorming alone finds the famous ones and misses the rest, so the analysis walks along the processing chain, sensing, perception, fusion, decision and actuation, and asks at each stage which properties of the environment, the vehicle and the people can degrade it. It is fed from several sources: the physics of each sensor, the literature and the examples in ISO 21448's annexes, the perception team's error analyses, test-drive findings, and the field data of predecessor products. 8.4Functional modifications and their side effects

Once a triggering condition is known to cause unacceptable behaviour, ISO 21448 offers a short list of measures: improve the function (better sensors, better algorithms, more data), restrict the ODD or the function in the affected conditions (no pedestrian AEB above a speed, or only warnings when glare is detected), hand over to the driver with information, or reduce the authority (partial braking first). Every measure has a side effect, and the analysis must look for it, because the classic SOTIF trap is a fix for one hazard that creates another. Raising the confirmation threshold to remove phantom brakes increases missed braking. Lowering it for children increases phantom brakes. 8.5Interactive: the false-activation and miss trade-off

The model is deliberately simple and its numbers illustrative. Every time the AEB evaluates a candidate (a pedestrian near the path, a shadow, a pole), the camera channel produces a score. Over \(10^6\) km the function sees about \(10^5\) candidates that are not collision-relevant, with scores distributed around 0; real collision-relevant pedestrians score around 5.5 by day and around 3.5 at night in rain. The decision brakes when the score exceeds the threshold and, if enabled, the radar confirms. The radar confirms 2% of the irrelevant candidates and 97% (by day) or 95% (at night) of the real ones.

With the defaults (threshold 4, radar confirmation, day), the function meets both criteria: 0.063 unjustified brakes per \(10^6\) km and 9.5% missed. Switch radar confirmation off and the unjustified rate rises fifty-fold to 3.2 per \(10^6\) km, thirty times over AC1; the camera alone would need a threshold of about 4.8 to meet AC1 and would then miss almost a quarter of the pedestrians by day. Switch to night and rain and the miss rate jumps to 71% with the same threshold: no threshold choice can fix a performance insufficiency of this size, because lowering it far enough to catch most pedestrians makes phantom brakes unacceptable. The only real measure is to improve the detector in that condition; the third option assumes that targeted night data have moved the real pedestrians' scores to 5.0, which brings the miss rate down to about 20%, still above AC2. What remains must then be handled by the ODD and the driver information, or argued in the residual-risk evaluation using how often the condition occurs.

Three general lessons come out of this small model. Fusion is the most effective SOTIF measure for false activations, because two independent physical principles rarely share a false alarm (but see the common triggering conditions above). Averages hide the problem: a function that meets AC2 overall can be useless in exactly the conditions where pedestrians are most at risk. And the threshold is a safety parameter: changing it, or retraining the network so that its score distribution shifts, changes the SOTIF evidence and needs the same impact analysis as a code change.

8.6Validation: how much evidence is enough

An acceptance criterion becomes a validation target: the amount and kind of testing needed to show with confidence that the criterion is met. The arithmetic for rare events is sobering. If an event follows a Poisson process and none is observed in \(N\) km, the 95% upper confidence bound on its rate is \(3.0/N\); with one event it is \(4.74/N\), with two \(6.30/N\). Demonstrating AC1, at most 0.1 unjustified brakes per \(10^6\) km, therefore needs 30 million km without a single unjustified full brake, or 47 million km if one occurs. The validation strategy therefore combines sources, each with a different strength. Open-loop replay of recorded data is the workhorse for false activations: the decision of a perception-based AEB on a recorded drive can be evaluated without closing the loop, so millions of kilometres recorded once can be re-run with every software version, and every intervention the new version would have made is reviewed. And shadow mode in a series fleet, where the function computes its decisions without acting and uploads the interesting cases, extends the replay base by orders of magnitude after start of production.

Rare-event statistics also gain from stratification. Instead of driving 30 million km and hoping they contain enough bridges and metal plates, the team estimates how often each triggering condition occurs per km from fleet data, measures the function's unjustified activation probability per occurrence on targeted recordings and simulations, and combines the two. This is a form of importance sampling, and it is only as good as the completeness of the triggering-condition list, which is why the unknown scenarios of area 3 still need undirected exploration and large-scale replay.

8.7After start of production

The SOTIF argument at release rests on known triggering conditions and a statistical extrapolation, and both can be wrong. ISO 21448 therefore requires field monitoring: driver overrides of interventions, interventions without a confirmed object, near misses detected by the fleet, customer complaints and accident data are collected and analysed, and a newly found triggering condition re-enters the analysis like a newly found fault. The fix is usually a new software or model version, which is a software update under UN R156 with its impact assessment and regression evidence (Standards 5.4). A team that has built its replay and evaluation pipeline well can answer "is the new model better and is it no worse anywhere that matters" in days, and that capability is a large part of the SOTIF case.

9Cybersecurity engineering: TARA and its consequences

A fault is indifferent; an attacker is not. An attacker looks for the weakest path, learns from published research, reuses tools, and becomes cheaper and better over the years the car is on the road. That is why cybersecurity has its own analysis, the threat analysis and risk assessment, and its own lifecycle that continues long after release. For a perception function, the analysis also has a pleasant surprise: much of the safety architecture already makes attacks harder.

9.1How it differs from safety, and what is recapped

The legal and organisational frame is in the standards chapter (Standards 5): UN R155 requires the vehicle manufacturer to run a certified cybersecurity management system (CSMS) and to show, per vehicle type, that risks are identified and treated; UN R156 does the same for software updates; ISO/SAE 21434 describes the engineering work that produces the evidence, organised in clauses for management, distributed activities, continual activities, concept, product development, validation, production, operation and maintenance, end of support, and in clause 15 the TARA methods themselves.

Three differences to functional safety shape the method. Impacts are broader: besides safety, a TARA rates financial, operational and privacy damage (the SFOP categories), because stolen models, disabled features and leaked camera recordings are harms too. Likelihood is replaced by attack feasibility, the effort an attacker needs, which is not a property of the design alone but of the state of the art in attacks, and must be re-evaluated as that changes. And the work never finishes: vulnerability monitoring, incident response and updates continue for the support period, which for a car means well over a decade.

9.2TARA step by step

ISO/SAE 21434 clause 15 defines the steps, and they map neatly onto the HARA. Asset identification lists what is worth protecting and which cybersecurity property matters: integrity, authenticity, confidentiality, availability. Damage scenarios describe the harm if a property is violated, at vehicle or stakeholder level. Impact rating rates each damage scenario as severe, major, moderate or negligible in each SFOP category; for safety the ratings correspond to the severity classes S3 to S0. Threat scenarios describe how a property could be violated, often found with STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege) applied to each asset and interface. Attack path analysis works out the concrete steps an attacker would take, usually as an attack tree. Attack feasibility rating rates each path. Risk value determination combines impact and feasibility into a risk value from 1 to 5. Risk treatment decision chooses to avoid, reduce, share or retain each risk, and reductions become cybersecurity goals at concept level and requirements and controls in development; retained or shared risks are recorded as cybersecurity claims.

9.3Attack paths: the attack tree

The damage scenario "the vehicle brakes hard without reason" can be reached in several ways, and an attack tree organises them. Its root is the attacker's goal; OR nodes are alternatives, AND nodes are steps that must all succeed. The leaves are concrete steps whose feasibility can be rated.

The tree makes two things visible. The cheapest-looking paths are often not through the clever part of the system: forging a CAN message from a compromised infotainment unit needs no knowledge of neural networks at all, and historically remote vehicle attacks went exactly that way, through connectivity units and gateways that did not filter. And the safety architecture is a security control: since the decision requires two physically different sensors to agree, an adversarial sticker that fools the camera achieves nothing alone; the attacker must also spoof the radar at the same place and time, which needs bespoke equipment and moves the path from "medium" to "low" feasibility.

9.4Attack feasibility and risk

ISO/SAE 21434 offers three ways to rate feasibility: by attack vector (network, adjacent, local, physical), by CVSS-like metrics, or by attack potential, the most detailed one, adapted from the common-criteria evaluation methodology. Attack potential adds up points for five factors: the elapsed time to identify and perform the attack (up to a day 0, a week 1, a month 4, six months 17, more 19), the attacker's specialist expertise (layman 0, proficient 3, expert 6, multiple experts 8), the required knowledge of the item (public 0, restricted 3, confidential 7, strictly confidential 11), the window of opportunity (unlimited 0, easy 1, moderate 4, difficult 10) and the equipment (standard 0, specialised 4, bespoke 7, multiple bespoke 9). A sum up to 13 means high feasibility, 14 to 19 medium, 20 to 24 low, 25 and more very low. The risk value then comes from a matrix of impact and feasibility that each organisation defines; the example in the standard's annex gives, for a severe impact, risk 5 at high feasibility down to 2 at very low.

Pitfall

Rating feasibility once, at the concept phase, and never again. Attack potential is time-dependent: a technique that needed multiple experts and bespoke equipment five years ago may be a published tool today. ISO/SAE 21434's continual activities require monitoring of vulnerabilities and attack techniques and re-evaluation of the TARA when they change, for the whole support period.

9.5Cybersecurity goals and CAL

Risks that are reduced become cybersecurity goals, for example "CG1: the integrity and authenticity of AEB brake requests shall be protected against manipulation from outside the chassis domain". Each goal can be assigned a cybersecurity assurance level (CAL 1 to 4), which, like an ASIL, scales the rigour of the development and verification work for that goal; the standard's annex gives an example of deriving it from impact and attack vector, with the highest levels for severe impacts reachable over a network. Unlike an ASIL, a CAL is informative and not mandatory, and organisations define their own scheme. The cybersecurity concept then refines the goals into requirements on the architecture, just as the functional safety concept does.

9.6Controls, and how security and safety meet

The controls for the AEB are the standard set of vehicle cybersecurity, each placed on an attack path of Figure 9.1. Domain separation and gateway filtering ensure that a connectivity unit cannot send chassis messages at all. Secure onboard communication (AUTOSAR SecOC) adds a truncated message authentication code computed with a symmetric key and a freshness value (a counter or time) to the brake request, so that a receiver can verify that it came from a key holder and is not a replay. Secure boot and signed software, models and calibration ensure that only authorised code and data run; the mechanics of boot chains, key provisioning and OTA belong to the production integration and ECU lifecycle chapter (in brief: Camera ECU 8.2). Authenticated diagnostics (UDS service 0x29 with certificates, rather than the older seed-and-key of 0x27) protect write services such as calibration (Camera ECU 10.4). Debug interfaces are locked, keys live in a hardware security module, and an intrusion detection component reports security events to the manufacturer's vehicle security operations centre.

Security and safety mechanisms often sit side by side on the same message, and the difference between them is a frequent question. E2E protection detects random faults and some systematic ones; its CRC and data ID are public, so it is no obstacle to an attacker. SecOC detects manipulation by anyone without the key; it is not developed to an ASIL and its failures (a key mismatch after a repair, lost freshness synchronisation) show up as messages that fail verification. The usual combination applies E2E to the safety payload and SecOC around it, and treats a SecOC verification failure like an E2E failure: the message is not used. That direction is safe for the AEB, because "not used" means "no brake". For a function whose safe state needs the message, a security failure becomes an availability problem with a safety consequence, and the two teams must design the reaction together.

Interesting detail

Safety and security can pull in opposite directions. Safety likes diagnostic access for fast fault analysis in the field, and fail-safe defaults that unlock things when in doubt; security wants access authenticated and locks that stay closed when in doubt. Safety likes deterministic, simple software; security adds cryptography, key management and update mechanisms that are anything but simple. ISO/SAE 21434 and ISO 26262 both ask for coordination between the disciplines, and a TARA that produces a threat to a safety goal must feed the HARA's view of that hazard, and vice versa.

9.7Verification, validation and life after release

Cybersecurity requirements are verified like safety requirements, by review and test, with two additions. Fuzz testing sends malformed and unexpected inputs to every interface (CAN, Ethernet services, diagnostic services, file parsers for updates and calibration) to find crashes and unexpected behaviour. Penetration testing by an independent team attacks the ECU or vehicle with the attacker's mindset, starting from the attack paths of the TARA and looking for paths it missed. Third-party software is tracked in a software bill of materials and scanned against vulnerability databases continuously (Build and containers 11.5).

In practice, R155 and R156 turn into processes that suppliers feel directly. The manufacturer's CSMS, certified by an approval authority and renewed every three years, requires suppliers to deliver their part of the TARA and its evidence, to report vulnerabilities in their components for the whole support period, and to support incident response with analysis and fixes. Each vehicle type's approval documents how the threats listed in the regulation's annex were addressed. Under R156, every software change, including a retrained network, is assessed for its impact on type-approved functions, versioned through the RX software identification number (RXSWIN) when it affects them, and delivered through a controlled update process. The interface agreement between the companies says who does which of these activities, exactly as the DIA does for safety.

Talk track

"The TARA mirrors the HARA: assets with the properties that matter, damage scenarios rated for safety, financial, operational and privacy impact, threat scenarios and attack paths, attack feasibility from attack potential, a risk value, and a treatment decision that turns into cybersecurity goals and controls. For a camera–radar AEB the cheap attacks are not the exotic ones but forged CAN messages and unauthenticated diagnostics, so gateway filtering, SecOC, secure boot and certificate-based diagnostics carry most of the weight, and the two-sensor safety architecture already makes adversarial patches expensive. E2E protects against faults and SecOC against attackers, and because feasibility changes over time the TARA is re-evaluated for the whole support period."

10The safety case: argument, evidence and residual risk

At the end of a project, hundreds of work products exist: analyses, specifications, review records, test reports, tool qualifications. None of them alone says the function is safe. The safety case is the document that does: an argument, structured so that someone outside the project can follow it, that the safety goals are met, supported at every leaf by evidence. It is the product of all the work in this chapter, and the thing an assessor actually assesses.

10.1Why an argument and not just a pile of evidence

Evidence does not speak for itself. A test report proves that certain tests passed; whether those tests are the right ones, whether they cover the hazards, and whether the assumptions under which they were run hold in the vehicle are questions the report cannot answer. The safety case makes the reasoning explicit: it states a claim, breaks it into sub-claims by a stated strategy, and supports each sub-claim with evidence, so that a gap in the reasoning is as visible as a missing test. ISO 26262-2 requires a safety case for every item; ISO 21448 asks for an equivalent argument that SOTIF is achieved, and ISO/SAE 21434 for a cybersecurity case. In practice the three are separate documents that reference each other, or one document with three branches.

10.2GSN: drawing the argument

The Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) is the most common way to draw a safety argument. Its elements are few. A goal (rectangle) is a claim. A strategy (parallelogram) says how a goal is broken down, for example "argue over each class of cause". A solution (circle) is a piece of evidence. Context (rounded rectangle) states what a claim refers to, assumptions and justifications (ovals marked A and J) state what is taken as given and why a strategy is valid, and a diamond marks a goal that is not yet developed. Solid arrows mean "is supported by", hollow arrows "in the context of".

Reading the figure top-down is reading the project. The random-hardware branch rests on the FMEDA and FTA of Chapter 5 and on the fault-injection reports that confirm their coverage claims. The systematic branch rests on the traceability report, the verification and coverage reports of Chapter 7 and the tool qualifications. The dependent-failure branch rests on the DFA and the freedom-from-interference evidence of Chapter 6; without it, the decomposition of Section 3.3 is a claim without support. The timing branch rests on the measured detection and reaction times. And the assumption A-1 on the brake system is not evidence at all: it is a dependency on another item's safety case, which must exist and be checked.

Pitfall

Arguments that only list evidence ("SG1 is met: see FMEA, FTA, test report") without saying why that evidence is sufficient. The strategy nodes and the justifications are where the argument lives; an assessor asks for them first. The opposite failure is an argument so abstract that no leaf can be checked: every leaf must be a specific document, version and result.

10.3Confirmation measures and independence

ISO 26262 does not trust a project to check itself. Three confirmation measures provide independent judgement. Confirmation reviews check key work products (the HARA, the safety plan, the safety concepts, the safety case, the integration and test strategy) for compliance and soundness. A functional safety audit checks that the processes in the safety plan were actually followed. A functional safety assessment judges whether the functional safety achieved by the item is adequate, and recommends acceptance, conditional acceptance or rejection. Each needs a degree of independence that grows with the ASIL, from I0 (should be performed, by anyone) through I1 (a different person), I2 (a person independent of the team responsible for the work product) to I3 (a person from an organisationally independent department or organisation); at ASIL D, the assessment requires I3.

10.4Residual risk and the release decision

No function is free of risk, and the safety case must say what remains. For random hardware faults, the residual is quantified by the PMHF and the FTA, here about 1.2 FIT for SG1 against a target below 100. For SOTIF, it is the validated rate of hazardous behaviour per condition with its confidence bounds, against the acceptance criteria, plus an argument about the unknown scenarios: how much undirected exploration, replay and field monitoring has been done, and what has been found. For cybersecurity, it is the list of retained risks with their claims. And for all three, it is the documented known limitations: the ODD, the conditions in which performance is reduced, and what the driver is told in the owner's manual and on the display.

The release for production is a decision, signed by named people, that the residual risk is acceptable. For an active-safety function the decision has a particular shape, sometimes called a positive risk balance: the AEB prevents many more collisions than it causes, so withholding it is also a risk. That argument is legitimate for SOTIF residual risk, but it never excuses an unprotected single-point fault or an untested safety mechanism; functional safety requirements must be met in their own right.

10.5Keeping the case alive

A safety case describes one configuration of one item. Every software release, every new network, every change of a supplier's part and every new field finding changes something, and the impact analysis decides which branches of the argument are affected and which evidence must be renewed. With over-the-air updates this happens many times over the vehicle's life, and the projects that cope are those whose evidence is generated by pipelines rather than by hand: traceability reports, coverage, fault-injection regressions on the HIL and the SOTIF replay results are rebuilt for every release candidate, so the safety case delta for a release is a reviewable diff rather than a new project.

Talk track

"The safety case is an argument, usually drawn in GSN, that the safety goals are met: a claim broken down by explicit strategies, typically over random hardware faults, systematic faults, dependent failures and timing, with evidence at every leaf, such as the FMEDA, the coverage reports, the DFA and the measured fault-injection times. 11Putting it together

The previous chapters took the AEB apart by discipline. This one puts it back together: first one fault followed through every mechanism with its timing, then the whole function on one page from hazard to evidence, then the rear-view camera as a second, simpler case, and finally what all of this means for the people who build perception software.

11.1One fault, step by step

The car approaches a pedestrian crossing at 50 km/h. A pedestrian has just stepped back onto the kerb; the camera's last good frame still shows her in the lane. At that moment the camera serializer locks up after a supply disturbance and starts repeating the last frame. Step through what happens.

Both channels deliver fresh objects every cycle. The camera's frames carry counters 1041, 1042, 1043 in their embedded lines; the radar sees the pedestrian moving back towards the kerb. The decision on the safety MCU sees no collision-relevant object in the path: no request.

The serializer repeats frame 1043. Everything downstream looks healthy: the link is locked, the CRC is correct, the ISP and the network process a perfectly normal image. The detector reports the pedestrian in the lane, 22 m ahead, with high confidence.

The second copy of frame 1043 arrives. The freeze monitor sees the counter has not advanced and counts one repetition, which alone is not yet an error (a single repeat can come from a legitimate frame-rate adaptation). The decision element receives a camera object in the path, but the radar reports nothing collision-relevant at that position: no two-channel confirmation, so no full brake request. At most the authority limiter would allow a short jerk below 4 m/s², which SG1 permits.

The third copy arrives and the monitor has two consecutive repetitions: camera channel invalid. From now on, the SoC sends the camera object list with an invalid flag, and the MCU ignores camera objects. Detection has used 67 ms of the 100 ms budget.

At the next 10 ms decision cycle the MCU's mode manager moves the AEB to radar-only mode: warnings, no camera-dependent braking. Any limited request that might have been active is withdrawn and the brake system releases within its 150 ms. The fault is contained well within the 300 ms FTTI.

The cluster shows "Emergency braking limited" within one second. A diagnostic trouble code with a snapshot (counter values, link status, supply voltage) is stored. The channel stays invalid until the next ignition cycle and a successful start-up check, so that an intermittent fault cannot make the function flicker.

Each step corresponds to an artefact earlier in the chapter. The radar veto is FSR-1 and the decomposition of Section 3.3; the limiter is FSR-2 and the HE3 row of the HARA; the 67 ms come from TSR-11 and were measured by the fault-injection test of Section 5.6; the modes are Figure 4.3; the DTC and the latch are the degradation design rules; and the whole sequence is one branch of the GSN in Figure 10.1. The scenario also shows why the architecture does not rely on one mechanism: the freeze monitor alone would have been fast enough, but the radar veto made the first 67 ms harmless too.

11.3The rear-view camera in brief

The rear-view camera shows how the same method lands on a display function. Its malfunction of interest is a frozen, delayed or misleading image while reversing, which makes the driver believe the space behind is free. The hazardous event "frozen image while reversing towards a child" rates S3 (a child struck by a reversing car), E3 (reversing in places where children may be present happens monthly or more often), C2 (the driver can still check mirrors and over the shoulder, and most do), which gives ASIL B. There is no second sensor to confirm the image and the image itself goes to a human, so the architecture is a single channel with strong diagnostics: frame counters and test rows from the sensor, link CRC, a CRC over a reference overlay at the display output, an age check on the frame actually displayed, and a defined reaction (blank the image and show a warning rather than show a stale one). The FMEDA of Section 5.4 is this architecture. The regulations add timing of their own, such as how quickly the image must appear after selecting reverse (Standards 6.1).

11.4What this means for the perception engineer

The architecture in this chapter was designed so that the neural network could stay outside the hardest requirements, and that is how most production systems handle perception today. It does not make the perception team's work less safety-relevant; it moves it. The team delivers outputs that a safety monitor can check: hardware timestamps carried through every stage, sequence counters, validity flags that mean something, and covariances or confidences that are calibrated rather than decorative. It delivers failure modes for the FMEA in vocabulary the safety engineers can use. It develops the code around the network to the channel's ASIL, including the inference runtime's integration. It owns the triggering-condition list and the per-condition evidence, which are the core of the SOTIF case. And it treats every new model version as a change to a safety-relevant element, with impact analysis, regression evidence and a release record, because under R156 that is what it is.

Every one of those steps traces back to a requirement, an analysis and a measured test. For perception teams, that is the contract: deliver timestamped, counted, protected, honestly calibrated outputs, own the triggering conditions and their evidence, and treat each model update as a safety-relevant change."

QSelf-check questions

Core questions with short model answers; the chapter sections hold the depth. The second group checks understanding with "why" and "what if" questions rather than definitions.

Q.1Core questions

What is the difference between ISO 26262 and ISO 21448?Basic

ISO 26262 covers hazards from malfunctions: random hardware faults and systematic faults such as bugs and wrong requirements, controlled by process rigour scaled by ASIL and by safety mechanisms with diagnostic coverage. ISO 21448 (SOTIF) covers hazards when nothing is broken: insufficiencies of the specification or of the technical performance, activated by triggering conditions such as glare or rain, and reasonably foreseeable misuse. For perception, SOTIF is where most of the real risk and evidence sits.

Walk me through a HARA.Basic

Start from the item definition. Apply guidewords to each function to get vehicle-level malfunctions (unintended, missing, too much, too late, stuck). Combine each with operational situations into hazardous events. Rate severity of the harm, exposure to the situation (not the fault's probability) and controllability by the people involved, without the item's own safety mechanisms. The S/E/C table gives the ASIL. Merge events into safety goals with the highest ASIL, each with a safe state and a fault-tolerant time interval.

How is an ASIL determined, and what does it change?Basic

From severity S1–S3, exposure E1–E4 and controllability C1–C3; the sum reproduces the table (10 is D, 9 C, 8 B, 7 A, less is QM). It changes the rigour: which methods are highly recommended for requirements, design, coding, verification and coverage, the hardware metric targets (SPFM, LFM, PMHF), and the independence of confirmation measures.

What is a safe state, and what is the FTTI?Basic

A safe state is an operating mode without unreasonable risk that the item goes to when it cannot meet its goal, for an AEB "no braking request, driver informed". The fault-tolerant time interval is the minimum time from a fault to a possible hazardous event without safety mechanisms; detection plus reaction must fit inside it, which sets the timing requirements for every mechanism.

Explain ASIL decomposition with an example.Intermediate

A requirement is implemented by two redundant, sufficiently independent requirements of lower ASIL, each able to prevent the violation alone, for example ASIL C as B(C) + A(C). In a camera–radar AEB, "only full-brake when both channels confirm" decomposes into a camera channel at B(C) and a radar channel at A(C). The combining logic stays at C, hardware metrics are still evaluated against C, and a dependent failure analysis must show independence.

What are SPFM, LFM and PMHF?Intermediate

Hardware metrics from the FMEDA. SPFM is the share of safety-related hardware failure rate that is not single-point or residual (safe or detected): at least 90/97/99% for B/C/D. LFM is the share of the remaining multiple-point faults that are not latent, typically through start-up and periodic tests: at least 60/80/90%. PMHF is the residual rate of violating the goal: below 100 FIT for B and C, 10 FIT for D.

FMEA versus FTA?Basic

FMEA is inductive, bottom-up: each element's failure modes are followed to their effects and rated for severity, occurrence and detection, producing actions. FTA is deductive, top-down: from the violated goal down through AND/OR gates to basic events, giving minimal cut sets and, with rates, the probability. FMEA is complete over the parts list, FTA over combinations; ISO 26262 wants both at higher ASILs.

What is a dependent failure analysis?Intermediate

An analysis of common-cause failures (one cause, several elements fail) and cascading failures (one element's failure causes another's) between elements that must be independent, typically for decomposition or freedom from interference. It checks coupling factors: shared power, clock, memory, signals, software, tools, team, environment. Its findings become measures such as separate supplies or E2E with separate data IDs.

What is freedom from interference, and how do you show it on an SoC?Intermediate

The absence of cascading failures from lower-ASIL to higher-ASIL elements, in memory, timing and communication. Memory: MPU or MMU, hypervisor stage-2 translation, SMMU for DMA masters, ECC. Timing: budgets and timing protection, cache partitioning, memory-bandwidth regulation, WCET measured under worst-case QM load. Communication: E2E between partitions. Evidence is analysis, reviewed configuration and tests that try to break the isolation.

What does E2E protection detect?Basic

With a CRC over data and a hidden data ID, a sequence counter and a receiver timeout: corruption, masquerade and wrong addressing, repetition, loss, wrong sequence, insertion of old messages, delay and blocking. The receiver gets a status per message (OK, repeated, wrong sequence, error, no new data) and a state machine decides whether the stream is valid. It does not protect against attackers.

What is the difference between E2E and SecOC?Intermediate

E2E is a safety mechanism against random and some systematic faults, developed to an ASIL, with public CRC and data ID. SecOC is a security mechanism: a truncated MAC with a secret key and a freshness value against manipulation and replay by an attacker. They are often combined on one message; a failed SecOC check is handled like a failed E2E check: the message is not used.

Explain MC/DC.Intermediate

Modified condition/decision coverage requires that each condition in a decision is shown to independently change the decision's outcome, with the others held fixed. For n conditions it needs at least n + 1 tests, far fewer than 2ⁿ. It catches wrongly wired logic such as an OR instead of an AND or a missing negation, which branch coverage misses. ISO 26262 highly recommends it at ASIL D.

What is a triggering condition? Give camera examples.Basic

A specific condition of a scenario that activates a functional insufficiency and leads to hazardous behaviour. For cameras: low sun and glare, tunnel exits, night with rain and spray, lens contamination, bridge shadows, pedestrians printed on billboards, occlusion by parked vans, unusual objects, pitch under hard braking.

How do you validate that phantom braking is rare enough?Intermediate

Set an acceptance criterion, for example at most one unjustified full brake per 10⁷ km, derived from the existing traffic risk. Zero events in N km bound the rate at 3/N with 95% confidence, so 30 million km would be needed by driving alone. Combine open-loop replay of recorded data with every software version, scenario-based simulation, targeted drives in the triggering conditions, stratified statistics per condition and shadow mode in the fleet.

Walk me through a TARA.Basic

Identify assets and their cybersecurity properties; derive damage scenarios and rate their safety, financial, operational and privacy impact; identify threat scenarios (STRIDE helps); analyse attack paths, often as attack trees; rate attack feasibility, for example by attack potential (time, expertise, knowledge, window, equipment); combine into a risk value; decide treatment (avoid, reduce, share, retain); reductions become cybersecurity goals and controls, retained risks become claims.

What do R155 and R156 require in practice?Intermediate

R155: the OEM needs a certified cybersecurity management system (risk management, monitoring and incident response, vulnerability management, supplier management, renewed every three years) and, per vehicle type, evidence that risks including the regulation's threat list are treated. R156: a software update management system, with impact assessment of each update on approved functions, RXSWIN versioning and safe, secure delivery. Suppliers contribute TARAs, evidence and vulnerability handling for the support period.

What is a safety case, and what is GSN?Basic

A safety case is a structured argument, supported by evidence, that the safety goals are met and the residual risk is acceptable. GSN draws it with goals (claims), strategies (how a claim is broken down), solutions (evidence), context, assumptions and justifications, so that both gaps in reasoning and missing evidence are visible.

How does tool qualification work?Intermediate

Each tool gets a tool impact (TI1: cannot introduce or hide errors; TI2: can) and a tool error detection (TD1 high to TD3 low confidence that its errors would be caught). TI1 or TD1 give TCL1, no qualification; TI2 with TD2 or TD3 give TCL2 or TCL3, which need qualification by confidence from use, process evaluation, tool validation or development to a safety standard.

Q.2Why and what if

Why is a missed AEB brake usually QM while a phantom brake is ASIL C or D?Basic

For a driver-assistance function the driver remains responsible; a missed intervention returns the car to the state of a car without AEB, which is controllable in general. An unintended full brake at speed creates a new hazard for the follower, who is not warned. The missed brake is a performance question for SOTIF and rating tests.

What if the HARA rated the motorway phantom brake S3 instead of S2?Intermediate

S3 E4 C3 gives ASIL D. The hardware metric targets tighten (SPFM 99%, LFM 90%, PMHF below 10 FIT), MC/DC becomes highly recommended, decomposition options change (for example B(D) + B(D), so the radar channel would need B), and the assessment needs I3 independence. One severity judgement changes the cost of the architecture, which is why its rationale must be solid.

Why does the safety goal allow a brake jerk below 4 m/s² for 300 ms?Intermediate

Because the HARA showed that such a jerk is simply controllable (HE3 is QM). Writing the limit into the goal gives the architecture room for low-confidence warning brakes without two-channel confirmation, and the authority limiter that enforces it becomes an ASIL C requirement itself.

What if both channels use the same vehicle-speed signal?Intermediate

That is a shared input and a dependent failure. A wrong speed can make a real roadside object appear to be on a collision course to both channels, which then correctly confirm each other, defeating the decomposition. The DFA must find it; measures are an ASIL source with E2E for speed and an independent plausibility check, such as against radar Doppler of stationary targets.

Why does improving both channels' coverage from 90% to 99% barely change the FTA result?Advanced

The independent pair's contribution is a product of two small rates and the exposure time, already negligible. The total is dominated by what is not redundant (the decision element's residual) and by common causes. Effort is better spent on the voter and on reducing β.

Why can a plausibility checker without a second sensor not be the C(C) leg of a decomposition?Advanced

Each leg must be able to prevent the goal violation alone. A geometric or temporal check catches corrupted or implausible objects, but a phantom pedestrian from a faulty network or corrupted weight is geometrically plausible. Without independent information about the world, the checker cannot tell it from a real one.

Why does lockstep not protect against a software bug?Basic

Both cores execute the same instructions on the same data; a bug produces the same wrong result in both and the comparator sees no mismatch. Lockstep detects random hardware faults in one core. Systematic faults need process, diverse redundancy or monitors that check results.

What if the freeze monitor needs three repeated frames instead of two?Intermediate

At 30 fps detection takes 100 ms instead of 67, using the whole detection budget; with any further delay, such as a decision cycle, the 100 ms TSR is violated. The fix is either a faster check, a budget renegotiation with a proof the FTTI still holds, or reliance on the radar veto being argued as the primary measure for SG1 during that time.

Why is a dedicated core not enough for temporal freedom from interference?Intermediate

It removes scheduling interference only. The core still shares the last-level cache, the interconnect, DRAM bandwidth, power and clock domains with the QM cores; memory-heavy QM load can slow a safety task several times. Cache partitioning, bandwidth regulation and WCET measured under worst-case load are needed.

Why may QM map data reduce AEB interventions but not trigger them?Intermediate

A QM source can be wrong without any detection. If its data can only suppress braking, its failure leads at worst to a missed intervention, which is QM; if it could trigger braking, a QM fault could violate SG1, which is ASIL C.

Why does a camera-radar AND gate not protect against a metal plate on the road?Intermediate

The plate is a common triggering condition: it gives the radar a genuine strong reflection and the camera a plausible dark obstacle, so both channels independently report an object and the 2oo2 decision confirms it. Decomposition handles independent faults; scenes that fool both sensors are found and handled in the SOTIF analysis.

What if you lower the camera threshold to catch more pedestrians at night?Intermediate

Unjustified activations rise steeply in every condition, because the threshold applies to all candidates; in the chapter's model a performance gap of this size cannot be closed by threshold at all. The measure is to improve the detector in that condition (data, ISP tuning), possibly with a condition-specific strategy, and to handle the remainder by the ODD and driver information. Any threshold change is a safety-relevant change.

Why is "we drove a million kilometres without a phantom brake" weak evidence?Basic

It bounds the rate only at 3 per million km with 95% confidence, thirty times above a criterion of 0.1 per million. And it says nothing about conditions rare in that million: the evidence must be stratified by triggering condition and extended by replay and simulation.

Why does attack feasibility have to be re-rated over the product's life?Basic

Feasibility depends on the attacker's effort, which falls as techniques are published and tools become available. An attack that needed multiple experts and bespoke equipment at launch may need a laptop five years later; the TARA, the controls and possibly an update must follow.

What if SecOC verification of the brake request fails after a repair because keys were not provisioned?Advanced

The brake system rejects every AEB request, as with an E2E failure: the AEB is unavailable but safe, since its safe state is "no request". It must be detected and shown to the driver, and the service process must provision keys. For a function whose safe state needs the message, such a security failure would be an availability problem with safety consequences and must be designed jointly.

Why is a retrained network a safety-relevant change even if no code changed?Basic

The network's weights are its behaviour. Its score distributions and per-condition performance, which the SOTIF evidence and the acceptance criteria rest on, change. It needs impact analysis, regression evidence on the replay and scenario base, a new release record, and under R156 an update assessment, possibly with an RXSWIN change.

GGlossary

Acceptance criterion
Quantitative statement of how often a hazardous behaviour of the intended function may occur, for example one unjustified full brake per 10⁷ km.
ASIL
Automotive Safety Integrity Level, A (lowest) to D, or QM; set by the HARA from severity, exposure and controllability.
ASIL decomposition
Implementing a safety requirement by two redundant, independent requirements of lower ASIL, written for example B(C) + A(C).
Attack potential
Sum of points for elapsed time, expertise, knowledge of the item, window of opportunity and equipment; maps to attack feasibility.
Common-cause failure
One root cause making several elements fail; often modelled by a beta factor.
CSMS
Cybersecurity management system required by UN R155: the organisation's processes for risk management, monitoring, incident response and suppliers.
CAL
Cybersecurity assurance level 1 to 4 in ISO/SAE 21434: an informative scale of rigour for a cybersecurity goal.
Cut set (minimal)
Smallest set of basic events in a fault tree whose joint occurrence causes the top event.
Degradation mode
Designed operating mode with reduced function that remains consistent with all safety goals after a fault.
DFA
Dependent failure analysis: search for common-cause and cascading failures through coupling factors.
Diagnostic coverage
Share of a hardware element's dangerous failure rate detected or controlled by a safety mechanism.
DIA
Development interface agreement: who produces which safety work product between customer and supplier.
E2E protection
End-to-end protection: CRC with data ID, sequence counter and timeout that detect communication faults across an unprotected channel.
Fault injection
Deliberately introducing faults to verify that safety mechanisms detect and react as claimed, within their time budgets.
FIT
Failures in time: one failure per 10⁹ device hours.
FMEA
Failure mode and effects analysis: inductive, bottom-up analysis of effect–mode–cause chains, rated for severity, occurrence and detection.
FMEDA
Failure modes, effects and diagnostic analysis: quantitative hardware analysis yielding SPFM, LFM and PMHF.
Freedom from interference
Absence of cascading failures from an element to another of higher ASIL, in memory, timing and communication.
Functional safety concept
Functional safety requirements derived from the safety goals and allocated to a preliminary architecture.
Functional insufficiency
Specification or performance insufficiency of the intended function; the SOTIF analogue of a fault.
FTTI
Fault-tolerant time interval: minimum time from a fault to a possible hazardous event without safety mechanisms.
FTA
Fault tree analysis: deductive, top-down analysis from an undesired event through AND/OR gates to basic events.
GSN
Goal Structuring Notation: graphical notation for safety arguments with goals, strategies, solutions, context and assumptions.
Hazardous event
A malfunctioning behaviour combined with an operational situation in which it can cause harm; the unit rated in a HARA.
HAZOP
Hazard and operability study: guidewords (no, more, less, late, unintended…) applied to functions to find deviations.
Item definition
Description of the function, its elements, boundary, interfaces and assumptions about neighbouring items; the input to HARA and TARA.
Latent fault
A multiple-point fault, typically of a safety mechanism, that is neither detected nor perceived and waits for a second fault.
LFM
Latent-fault metric: share of multiple-point faults that are not latent.
Lockstep
Two cores executing the same code with a comparator; detects random faults in one core.
MC/DC
Modified condition/decision coverage: each condition shown to independently change the decision's outcome.
ODD
Operational design domain: the conditions (speed, roads, weather, lighting) in which a function is designed to operate.
PMHF
Probabilistic metric for random hardware failures: residual rate of violating a safety goal, in FIT.
Safe state
Operating mode without unreasonable risk that the item reaches when it cannot fulfil a safety goal.
Safety case
Argument, supported by evidence, that the safety goals are met and the residual risk is acceptable.
Safety goal
Top-level, vehicle-level safety requirement from the HARA, with ASIL, safe state and FTTI.
Safety plan
Plan of the safety activities, work products, responsibilities and confirmation measures of a project.
SecOC
AUTOSAR secure onboard communication: message authentication code with a freshness value against manipulation and replay.
SEooC
Safety element out of context: element developed on assumed requirements and use, documented in a safety manual.
SOTIF
Safety of the intended functionality (ISO 21448): absence of unreasonable risk from functional insufficiencies and foreseeable misuse.
SPFM
Single-point fault metric: share of safety-related hardware failure rate that is not single-point or residual.
SUMS
Software update management system required by UN R156.
TARA
Threat analysis and risk assessment (ISO/SAE 21434 clause 15).
TCL
Tool confidence level 1 to 3, from tool impact and tool error detection; TCL2 and TCL3 need qualification.
Technical safety concept
Technical safety requirements and mechanisms allocated to hardware and software, with parameters and timing.
Triggering condition
Specific condition of a scenario that activates a functional insufficiency, such as glare or a bridge shadow.
Validation target
Amount and kind of evidence needed to show an acceptance criterion is met with stated confidence.
