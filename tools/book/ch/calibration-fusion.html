Multi-sensor calibration, synchronisation and fusion

How a camera and a radar that each see part of the truth, on their own clocks and from their own mounting points, become one track you can brake on: coordinate frames and cross-sensor calibration, timestamps and gPTP, out-of-order data and latency compensation, fusion architectures, EKF, UKF and particle filters, data association, uncertainty and conflicts, and what to do when a sensor goes quiet, late or wrong.

1The big picture: one pedestrian, two sensors, one track

A pedestrian steps off the kerb 34 metres in front of a car. The front camera sees a person-shaped blob and knows very precisely in which direction it is, but only roughly how far away. The radar knows the distance to a few centimetres and how fast it is closing, but is unsure about the direction and cannot say whether the echo is a person or a pole. Neither sensor alone is good enough to decide whether to brake. Fusion is the machinery that turns their two partial, noisy, differently timed views into one estimate that is better than either, together with an honest statement of how good it is.

1.1Why fuse sensors at all

Every automotive sensor is strong where another is weak. A camera measures angles very well, because an angle is just a pixel position, and it recognises what an object is: pedestrian, cyclist, car, traffic sign. It cannot measure distance directly; a single camera infers range from the object's size, from where it touches the road, or from a learned depth network, and all of these get worse with distance and with every bump in the road. It is also blinded by darkness, glare, fog and a dirty windscreen. A radar measures range and radial velocity directly from the echo's time of flight and Doppler shift, works in the dark and in rain, and is cheap. Its angular resolution is coarse, it sees metal far better than people, and it produces ghost targets from reflections off guardrails and tunnel walls. A lidar gives precise 3D points but no velocity and suffers in heavy rain and snow; an IMU and wheel odometry say how the car itself moves but nothing about the world.

Fusion exploits this complementarity in three ways. It improves accuracy: combining a precise angle with a precise range gives a precise position. It improves robustness: an object confirmed by two physically different sensors is unlikely to be a ghost, and when one sensor is blinded the other still delivers something. And it improves coverage and continuity: one sensor fills the gaps in field of view, range or time of the other. A safety argument for automatic emergency braking leans on all three; regulations and consumer tests such as Euro NCAP's night-time pedestrian scenarios are difficult to pass with a single sensor type.

Why it matters

Most field problems of a fused perception system are not in the clever part. They come from a calibration that is half a degree off, a timestamp taken at the wrong moment, a measurement processed in the wrong order or a covariance that claims more certainty than the sensor has. The estimator mathematics is well understood; what makes fusion work in a car is getting space, time and uncertainty right before the filter ever sees the data.

1.2The running example

The chapter follows one scene from start to finish. The ego car drives straight at 8 m/s (29 km/h). A pedestrian, 34 m ahead and 3 m to the right of the car's centre line, walks across the road to the left at 1.4 m/s. Two sensors see the pedestrian.

The front camera sits behind the windscreen, 1.8 m in front of the rear axle. It runs at 30 frames per second with a rolling-shutter sensor, and its frames go over a SerDes link to the central ADAS computer, where the ISP and a detection network run. From each frame it delivers a detection: the azimuth of the pedestrian with a standard deviation of about 0.15°, a monocular range estimate good to about 8% (2.5 m at 32 m), a class and a confidence. From mid-exposure to the moment the detection reaches the fusion takes about 50 ms.

The front radar sits in the bumper, 3.6 m in front of the rear axle. It is a separate ECU with its own processor and its own crystal oscillator, connected to the central computer over automotive Ethernet. Every 50 ms it transmits a frame of chirps, processes it and sends a list of detections: range (0.25 m), azimuth (1°) and range rate (0.1 m/s). Its latency from the middle of the chirp frame to arrival is about 30 ms.

The fusion runs on the central computer. It must place both sensors' detections in one coordinate frame, on one time axis, decide that they belong to the same object, and combine them into a track: position, velocity, their covariance, an existence probability and a class, valid at a stated time. That track goes to the emergency-braking function and to the planner.

The chapter is organised along that chain. Chapters 2 and 3 put both sensors into one coordinate frame. Chapters 4 to 6 put them onto one time axis and deal with data that arrive late or out of order. Chapter 7 asks at which level to combine information. Chapters 8 and 9 are the estimator: filters beyond the linear Kalman filter, and data association. Chapters 10 and 11 deal with uncertainty, conflicts and failing sensors, and Chapter 12 walks the pedestrian through the whole chain with numbers.

1.3What each sensor knows, as a picture

The clearest way to see why fusion helps is to draw each sensor's uncertainty about the pedestrian's position as an ellipse: the region where the true position probably lies. The camera's ellipse is a long needle pointing at the car: narrow across the line of sight (a 0.15° bearing error at 32 m is 8 cm) and long along it (2.5 m). The radar's ellipse is short along the line of sight (25 cm) and wide across it (1° at 30 m is 53 cm).

For two independent Gaussian estimates, the fused information is the sum of the informations: \(P_f^{-1} = P_c^{-1} + P_r^{-1}\). Along the line of sight the radar's information dominates, across it the camera's, so the fused ellipse is about 25 cm by 8 cm, an area ten times smaller than the camera's and almost seven times smaller than the radar's. Velocity follows the same logic: the radar measures the closing speed directly through Doppler but sees almost nothing of the pedestrian's sideways walking, because that motion is perpendicular to the line of sight; the camera sees the sideways motion as a change of bearing. The sideways speed is what decides whether the pedestrian will be in the car's path, so the camera is indispensable for that question, and the radar for the time to collision.

Talk track

"Camera and radar fail differently. The camera gives a precise direction and a class but a poor distance; the radar gives a precise distance and closing speed but a poor direction. Fusing them gives an estimate that is precise in both directions, and an object seen by two independent physical principles is far less likely to be a ghost. Most of the engineering effort goes into making sure both sensors talk about the same place at the same time."

2Coordinate frames: putting every sensor in one space

Every sensor measures in its own coordinate system: the camera in pixels and rays from its optical centre, the radar in range and azimuth from its antenna, the IMU along its own three axes. Before two measurements can be compared they must be expressed in the same frame, and the transforms that do that are the first thing a fusion system needs and the first place it goes wrong.

2.1Why frames are the first source of fusion bugs

A frame is an origin and three axes. Sensors bring their own, and they follow different conventions. The vehicle industry uses the ISO 8855 vehicle frame: x forward, y to the left, z up, usually with the origin at the centre of the rear axle projected onto the ground. Robotics software (ROS, REP 103) uses the same axis directions for bodies. Cameras use the optical frame: z along the optical axis, x to the right in the image and y down. Radars report azimuth with positive angles to the left or to the right depending on the supplier, and IMUs are mounted in whatever orientation the board layout allowed. None of this is difficult, but each convention is a sign or an axis swap waiting to happen, and a sign error in a lateral axis can survive a straight-road test unnoticed.

The quantities themselves are also different. A camera does not measure a 3D point at all; it measures a ray. A radar measures a point in polar coordinates, usually without elevation. A lidar measures 3D points but each one at a slightly different time. Bringing them into one frame therefore involves both a rigid transform and a measurement model that says what each sensor actually observes.

2.2The frames of a vehicle and the transform tree

Fusion software organises frames in a transform tree. At the root is a frame fixed to the ground. An odometry frame is the integral of wheel speeds and IMU rates: it is smooth and continuous, which is exactly what a tracker needs, but it drifts slowly away from the truth. A map frame is corrected by GNSS or map matching: it is globally right but can jump when a correction arrives. The vehicle frame moves through the odometry frame with the ego motion, and every sensor frame is attached to the vehicle frame by a fixed transform, its extrinsics, which is what calibration determines.

The distinction between static and dynamic transforms is the key to the rest of the chapter. A sensor's extrinsics change only when the sensor is recalibrated (or knocked). The vehicle's pose in the odometry frame changes every few milliseconds, so any transform through it must be evaluated at a time: the pedestrian position measured by the camera at 100 ms must use the vehicle pose at 100 ms, not the latest one. Tracking in the odometry frame and using the vehicle frame only for input and output is the most common design, because object motion models (a pedestrian walks in a straight line) are simple in a ground-fixed frame and complicated in the frame of a moving, turning car.

2.3Transforms, notation and composition

A rigid transform maps a point from frame B to frame A with a rotation and a translation, \(p_A = R_{AB}\,p_B + t_{AB}\), or, in homogeneous coordinates, \(p_A = T_{AB}\,p_B\) with

\[ T_{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{AB} & t_{AB} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T_{AC} = T_{AB}\,T_{BC}, \qquad T_{AB}^{-1} = T_{BA} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{AB}^{\top} & -R_{AB}^{\top} t_{AB} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The naming convention \(T_{AB}\) ("B expressed in A", the pose of B in A) makes composition checkable: inner indices must match, as in \(T_{\text{odom},\text{cam}}(t) = T_{\text{odom},\text{veh}}(t)\,T_{\text{veh},\text{cam}}\). \(t_{AB}\) is then the position of B's origin in A, so a calibration file that stores \(T_{\text{veh},\text{cam}}\) contains the camera's mounting position in vehicle coordinates, which can be checked against the CAD drawing with a tape measure. Codebases that do not encode the direction in the name ("cam_extrinsics") are the ones in which half of the integration bugs are an inverted transform.

Rotations are stored as matrices, quaternions or Euler angles. Euler angles are convenient for humans and for small corrections (a calibration tool reports "yaw +0.4°") but come in twelve orders and two interpretations, so a calibration interface must say which. Internally, quaternions or rotation matrices avoid the ambiguities, and small rotation errors are best expressed as a rotation vector, which is what optimisers and filters estimate (Mathematics 6).

Pitfall

Applying the camera's optical-frame rotation twice or not at all. The rotation from vehicle to camera contains both the physical mounting angles (a few degrees of pitch) and the fixed axis permutation from "x forward, z up" to "z forward, y down". If one tool includes the permutation in the extrinsics and another adds it in code, the result is off by 90° and fails immediately. If both include a small pitch convention with opposite signs, the result is off by twice the mounting pitch and fails only at long range.

2.4What a small calibration error costs

A translation error moves every measurement by the same amount, so 2 cm of mounting position error is 2 cm everywhere. A rotation error grows with distance: a yaw error \(\delta\psi\) moves a point at range \(d\) sideways by \(d\tan\delta\psi \approx d\,\delta\psi\). One degree is 1.75 cm per metre of range: 17 cm at 10 m, 52 cm at 30 m, 1.4 m at 80 m. A pedestrian is about 50 cm wide and a lane 3.5 m, so a one-degree yaw error at highway distances moves an object into the neighbouring lane, and at urban distances it moves a pedestrian from "on the kerb" to "in the path".

For a camera, pitch errors are as important for a different reason: a monocular range estimate from the ground contact point depends on the angle below the horizon, and at shallow angles that angle is tiny. With the camera 1.3 m above the road, a point 30 m away is only 2.5° below the horizon, so a pitch error of 0.1° changes its estimated range by \(d^2\,\delta\theta/h \approx 1.2\) m. This is why camera range is so poor at distance, why the car's pitch under braking and load matters, and why online pitch estimation exists (Camera geometry 6.4).

3Calibrating sensors against each other

Calibration determines the numbers in the transform tree: each sensor's internal model (its intrinsics) and its pose relative to the vehicle and to the other sensors (its extrinsics). For a single camera this is covered in the camera geometry chapter; here the question is how to calibrate sensors that measure different things, such as a camera that sees rays and a radar that sees echoes, against each other.

3.1Intrinsics and extrinsics, per sensor

Intrinsics describe how a sensor turns the world into its raw measurement; extrinsics describe where it is mounted. The split matters in practice because the two are calibrated by different people at different times: intrinsics usually by the sensor supplier on the production line, extrinsics by the vehicle manufacturer at the end of the vehicle line, in service, and continuously while driving.

Extrinsics are six numbers per sensor (three rotations, three translations), but not all of them matter equally or are equally observable. For a radar without elevation measurement, pitch and height hardly affect the 2D detections and are set mechanically; yaw matters a lot. For a camera all six matter, with pitch and yaw the most sensitive. For an IMU the rotation matters (it decides which axis measures the vehicle's yaw rate) and the translation hardly does, except for accelerations during fast turns.

3.2Target-based calibration as least squares

The most direct way to calibrate two sensors against each other is to show both of them something they can both measure and that is at a known or common place. For a camera and a radar that is a corner reflector (a trihedral metal corner that returns a strong, point-like radar echo from a wide range of angles) with a visual marker such as a checkerboard or an AprilTag at its centre. The camera measures the marker's pose by PnP (Classic CV 10); the radar measures the reflector's range and azimuth. Put the target in several positions, or several targets in the field of view, and solve for the transform that makes the two agree.

Formally this is a nonlinear least-squares problem, the same machinery as camera calibration (Mathematics 9). With \(c_i\) the marker position in camera coordinates and \(z_i = (r_i, \theta_i)\) the radar detection, the residual of target \(i\) is the radar measurement minus the predicted measurement of the transformed camera point, \(e_i = z_i - h(T_{RC}\,c_i)\), weighted by the inverse of the combined measurement covariance, and the solution minimises \(\sum_i e_i^{\top}\Sigma_i^{-1}e_i\) with Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt. Weighting matters because radar range is much more precise than radar azimuth: an unweighted fit would let the noisy azimuths pull the solution around.

Two practical lessons follow from the formulation. The first is observability: a yaw error and a lateral offset both move targets sideways, and with all targets at the same range they are indistinguishable; only targets at different ranges separate them, because the yaw error grows with range and the offset does not. Likewise a single row of targets straight ahead constrains yaw poorly. The second is that the residuals are the quality report: their spread after the fit should match the sensors' noise, and a fit whose residuals are much larger, or show a pattern over azimuth, has a wrong model (an uncalibrated radar angle curve, a misdetected marker) rather than a wrong transform.

Camera-to-lidar calibration works the same way with a different target. A planar board is detected by the camera (its pose by PnP) and by the lidar (its points fitted to a plane). Each board pose gives the constraint that the lidar points, transformed into the camera frame, must lie on the camera's plane: three constraints per pose, so at least three poses with non-parallel normals are needed, and in practice ten to twenty. Targetless alternatives align lidar depth edges with image edges or maximise mutual information between lidar intensity and image brightness; they are used for checking and refining rather than for the first calibration.

3.3Motion-based calibration: hand-eye and camera–IMU

When two sensors cannot see a common target, or when the calibration must happen without one, their motions can be compared. If the car moves and each sensor estimates its own motion between two times (the camera from visual odometry, the lidar from scan matching, the IMU by integration, the wheels by odometry), then those motions are the same rigid motion seen from two mounting positions. With \(A\) the motion of sensor 1 and \(B\) the motion of sensor 2 over the same interval and \(X\) the unknown transform between them, \(A X = X B\): the classical hand-eye calibration problem from robotics.

Hand-eye calibration needs rotations about at least two non-parallel axes. A car on a flat road rotates almost only about the vertical axis, so the height of a sensor is not observable from motion at all and parts of the rotation are poorly constrained. That is the fundamental reason why motion-based vehicle calibration is combined with the ground plane (which gives roll, pitch and height for a camera or lidar that sees the road) or with targets, and why online calibration estimates only the parameters the driving actually excites.

Camera–IMU calibration is the most demanding case and has a standard solution in tools such as Kalibr: the sensor rig is moved by hand in front of a calibration board with rotations and accelerations in all axes, and an optimiser fits a continuous-time trajectory (a B-spline) that explains both the camera's board observations and the IMU's rates and accelerations. The unknowns are the rotation and translation between camera and IMU, the IMU biases, and the time offset between the two sensors' clocks. That last one is worth remembering: a good calibration estimates time as well as space, because a few milliseconds of offset look exactly like a rotation error during a turn.

3.4Radar alignment: at the end of the line and while driving

Radar alignment is the radar's yaw (and, for radars with elevation, pitch) relative to the vehicle's thrust axis, the direction in which the rear wheels push the car, which can differ from the body's centre line by a few tenths of a degree. At the end of the production line, or in a workshop after a bumper repair, it is set or measured statically: the car stands on an aligned stand, a reflector or a target board is placed at a known position in front of it, and the radar reports the angle it sees. The difference to the expected angle is stored as a correction or, for radars with a mechanical adjustment screw, corrected by turning it.

While driving, radars estimate their own yaw misalignment from stationary objects, and this is one of the most elegant tricks in automotive sensing. A stationary object at true azimuth \(\theta\) in the vehicle frame has a radial velocity of \(-v_{\text{ego}}\cos\theta\) when the car drives straight at speed \(v_{\text{ego}}\). If the radar is mounted with an unknown yaw error \(\psi\), it reports the azimuth \(\theta_m = \theta - \psi\), so every stationary detection satisfies

\[ \dot r = -v_{\text{ego}}\cos(\theta_m + \psi). \]

Collect a few hundred detections of guardrail posts, signs and parked cars while driving straight, fit \(\psi\) by least squares, and the misalignment comes out. The fit needs no target and no map, only the ego speed and the knowledge that most of the world does not move; moving objects are rejected as outliers.

The right-hand plot shows why the method needs a wide field of view. Near boresight the cosine is flat, so a small change of angle changes the expected range rate by almost nothing: the information about \(\psi\) is proportional to \(\sin\theta\). With detections spread over ±60°, the simulation recovers 2.02° ± 0.04°. With only the 43 detections within ±5° of boresight, the same data give 2.75° ± 0.62°: the estimate is unusable, and a forward-looking long-range radar with a narrow field of view needs far more driving to converge than a corner radar.

Interesting detail

The same equation runs backwards in radar odometry: if the alignment is known, the range rates of stationary detections give the ego velocity, including its direction, so a radar can measure the car's sideslip during skidding, where wheel odometry fails. Fitting \(\dot r_i = -v_x\cos\theta_i - v_y\sin\theta_i\) to all stationary detections of one cycle is a two-parameter least-squares problem that runs in microseconds.

3.5Online calibration and calibration monitoring

An end-of-line calibration describes the car on the day it left the factory. Afterwards, loading and tyre pressure change pitch, a parking bump shifts a bumper radar, a windscreen replacement moves the camera bracket, temperature bends brackets slightly. Online calibration estimates the parameters that drift (camera pitch, roll and yaw from lane markings and the ground, radar yaw from Doppler) and applies corrections slowly, with limits, so that a wrong estimate cannot jump the system into a worse state (Camera geometry 6.4).

Fusion adds a second, independent check: cross-sensor residuals. If camera and radar detections associated to the same tracks consistently disagree by a pattern, such as a lateral offset that grows with range, one of the two extrinsics has changed. A monitor that averages the residual between camera bearing and radar-derived bearing over thousands of associated detections can detect a 0.3° change that no single detection would reveal. When the monitor and the sensor's own online calibration disagree, or the correction exceeds its allowed range, the right reaction is a diagnostic trouble code and a degraded mode (Chapter 11), not a silent correction.

In practice

Treat calibration as versioned data with a checksum and a validity state, stored per vehicle and read at boot (Camera ECU 9). Every function that consumes it should know whether it is the end-of-line value, a service recalibration or an online refinement, and when it was last confirmed. "Which calibration was active?" is the first question in every field investigation of a fused perception problem.

Talk track

"Cross-sensor calibration is least squares on correspondences: targets both sensors can see, or motions both sensors measure. The two things to get right are observability, meaning targets spread in range and angle, or motion that excites every axis, and weighting by each sensor's real noise. In the field, radars keep their yaw aligned from the Doppler of stationary objects, cameras their pitch from the road, and the fusion watches cross-sensor residuals to catch a sensor that has moved."

4Time: clocks, timestamps and synchronisation

Space is only half of the alignment. A measurement describes the world at one instant, and the car, the pedestrian and the sensors keep moving. If the fusion believes a measurement was taken 20 ms later than it really was, it places the measurement where the world was not. In a moving car, time errors are position errors.

4.1Why time is a coordinate

At 50 km/h a car moves 14 cm in 10 ms. In a turn at 20°/s, the line of sight to an object 30 m away sweeps sideways by 10 cm in the same 10 ms. A pedestrian walking at 1.4 m/s moves 1.4 cm. These numbers mean that a timestamp error of 10 ms costs about as much as a mediocre extrinsic calibration, and a timestamp error of one camera frame (33 ms) costs more than a bad one. Unlike a calibration error, a time error is invisible when the car stands still and appears only in motion, which is why it survives static end-of-line checks and shows up as "the radar and camera tracks separate in turns".

Getting time right has three parts, which the next sections take in order: each measurement must carry a timestamp that means the right thing (Section 4.2); all timestamps must refer to one clock, which means the clocks of different ECUs must be synchronised (Sections 4.3 to 4.5); and the fusion must process measurements in the order of their timestamps, not of their arrival (Chapter 5).

4.2What a timestamp should mean: event time, hardware and software stamps

The only timestamp a fusion can use is the time of the physical event: when the light that formed the image hit the pixels, when the radar's chirps illuminated the scene. The time a message was received, when a callback ran or when the detection network finished are all later by variable amounts and are useless for fusion except as diagnostics. The rule is to timestamp as close to the event as possible, in hardware, and to carry the timestamp with the data through every later stage without ever replacing it.

A hardware timestamp is latched by a circuit when a signal edge occurs: the camera receiver in the SoC stamps the frame-start packet, an Ethernet MAC stamps the start of a frame on the wire, a GPIO capture unit stamps a sensor's sync pulse. Its uncertainty is nanoseconds to a few microseconds, and, more importantly, it does not depend on load. A software timestamp is a call to the clock in a driver or application; it inherits interrupt latency, scheduling delay and every queue in between, which on a loaded Linux system ranges from microseconds to tens of milliseconds and grows exactly when the system is busiest (Linux 6.5, 9).

A radar's measurement time is the middle of its chirp frame, which only the radar knows; it must report it, in a synchronised clock, rather than the time it sent the message.

Pitfall

Two clocks on the same Linux machine. Camera buffers from V4L2 carry CLOCK_MONOTONIC timestamps, while the network and the PTP stack work in PTP time on the NIC's hardware clock, and phc2sys usually steers CLOCK_REALTIME. Mixing monotonic camera timestamps with PTP radar timestamps without converting gives an offset of seconds to decades, which is caught immediately, but converting once at startup instead of continuously gives an offset that creeps by microseconds per second and is not caught at all.

4.3Clocks drift: offset, rate and jitter

Every ECU counts time with its own quartz oscillator, and no two quartz oscillators run at exactly the same frequency. Automotive crystals are specified to a few tens of parts per million (ppm) over temperature and ageing; two of them can differ by 50 ppm or more. A model of one clock against a reference is \(t_{\text{local}} = (1 + \varepsilon)\,t_{\text{ref}} + \theta + \text{noise}\): an offset \(\theta\) (how far apart they are now), a rate or frequency error \(\varepsilon\) (how fast the offset grows, the drift), and jitter (noise on each reading). The rate itself wanders slowly with temperature.

Twenty ppm sounds negligible, but it is 72 ms per hour. An ECU that synchronised its clock once at boot is a frame off after half an hour of driving, and a recording made with unsynchronised clocks cannot be replayed consistently. Synchronisation protocols therefore do two things: they measure the offset to a reference regularly, and they adjust the local clock's rate as well as its value, with a control loop (a clock servo, typically a PI controller) that slews the clock smoothly rather than stepping it. Steps are poison for fusion: a clock that jumps backwards by 2 ms makes new measurements look older than old ones.

4.4PTP and gPTP: how clocks agree over Ethernet

The Precision Time Protocol (PTP, IEEE 1588) synchronises clocks over a network to well below a microsecond. One clock is the grandmaster; every other clock measures its offset to its master by exchanging timestamped messages. The trick that makes PTP precise is that every timestamp is taken in hardware at the moment the message passes the network interface, so the variable delays of software and queues do not enter the measurement. Step through one exchange:

The master sends a Sync message and its hardware stamps the departure time \(t_1\) in master time. The slave's hardware stamps the arrival \(t_2\) in slave time. The difference \(t_2 - t_1\) contains both the unknown link delay and the unknown offset between the clocks: 250.6 µs here.

The exact departure time is only known after the message has left, so a two-step clock sends it in a separate Follow_Up message. A one-step clock writes it into the Sync message on the fly, in hardware, which needs a more capable PHY or MAC.

To separate delay from offset, the slave sends a message the other way. It stamps the departure \(t_3\) in its own time; the master stamps the arrival \(t_4\) in master time. Now \(t_4 - t_3\) is the delay minus the offset: −249.4 µs.

The master returns \(t_4\) in a Delay_Resp. The slave now holds four timestamps, two from each clock, and two equations with two unknowns.

Adding and subtracting the two differences gives \(\text{offset} = \tfrac{1}{2}[(t_2 - t_1) - (t_4 - t_3)] = 250.0\) µs and \(\text{delay} = \tfrac{1}{2}[(t_2 - t_1) + (t_4 - t_3)] = 0.6\) µs. The slave's servo then slews its clock to remove the offset and adjusts its rate from the change of offset between exchanges.

The derivation assumes the delay is the same in both directions. Any asymmetry \(a\) between the two directions appears as an offset error of \(a/2\) that no amount of averaging removes, which is why PHYs with different transmit and receive latencies need calibrated corrections, and why timestamping in software, where the send and receive paths through the kernel differ, limits accuracy to tens of microseconds.

In a network with switches, plain PTP would see the switch's variable queueing delay. Two kinds of PTP-aware switches remove it: a transparent clock measures how long each PTP message spent inside it (its residence time) and adds that to a correction field in the message; a boundary clock terminates PTP on each port, synchronises itself as a slave and acts as master for the next hop. The Best Master Clock Algorithm (BMCA) lets the clocks of a network elect the best grandmaster automatically from announced quality attributes.

gPTP (generalized PTP, IEEE 802.1AS) is the profile of PTP used in automotive and audio-video Ethernet, part of the Time-Sensitive Networking family. Its main differences: it runs directly on Ethernet frames (layer 2), every device in the path must be time-aware, and each link measures its own delay continuously with a peer-to-peer exchange (Pdelay_Req and Pdelay_Resp between neighbours, giving the link delay \(\tfrac{1}{2}[(t_4 - t_1) - (t_3 - t_2)]\) with each pair of stamps from one clock), so a Sync can be forwarded with the accumulated link delays and residence times already added. It also tracks the frequency ratio to each neighbour, so time is corrected for rate between Sync messages. 802.1AS is designed for synchronisation within about 1 µs over up to seven hops. In vehicles, the Avnu automotive profile and AUTOSAR's EthTSyn module fix the configuration statically: the grandmaster is chosen at design time (often the central computer or a gateway), BMCA is disabled, and synchronisation starts within a fraction of a second after power-up instead of waiting for an election.

Numbers

Default gPTP Sync interval 125 ms, peer delay measurement once per second (automotive configurations often sync faster, e.g. every 31.25 ms). Accuracy with hardware timestamping: tens to hundreds of nanoseconds per hop, within 1 µs end to end. With software timestamps: tens of microseconds. Everything fusion needs (well below 1 ms) is met by any working hardware-timestamped gPTP; the problems are in the endpoints, not the protocol.

4.5Time in the rest of the car

Not every sensor speaks Ethernet. On CAN, AUTOSAR defines a time synchronisation (CanTSyn) that mirrors the two-step idea: the time master sends a SYNC message with the seconds and stamps its actual transmission time, then sends a follow-up (FUP) carrying the precise remainder; receivers stamp the SYNC reception and correct. Accuracy is limited by how precisely the controllers stamp transmission and reception, typically tens of microseconds, which is adequate for radars and ultrasonic sensors on CAN FD. FlexRay has a similar mechanism. Inside each ECU, AUTOSAR's Synchronized Time-Base Manager (StbM) holds the global time base and hands it to applications, along with status bits that say whether the time is currently synchronised.

Cameras behind a SerDes link usually have no clock of their own that matters: the central computer triggers them through the link's back channel and stamps their frames on arrival in its own capture hardware (Camera pipeline 6). The capture hardware's clock must then be tied to the PTP time base, either because the SoC's time-sync hardware disciplines it or by sampling both clocks regularly and converting. Lidars often take a GNSS pulse-per-second (PPS) signal with a time message, or PTP directly. IMUs on SPI are stamped by the host when their data-ready interrupt fires, ideally with a capture unit rather than in the interrupt handler.

The result in a typical vehicle is a small number of time domains (the gPTP domain, the CAN domain, perhaps a GNSS-based domain for logging) connected by gateways that translate between them. The fusion needs every timestamp it uses converted into one of them, and it needs to know whether the source was synchronised when the stamp was taken. AUTOSAR carries this as status flags (synchronised, timeout, sync-to-gateway); Linux stacks must build the equivalent from ptp4l and phc2sys state.

In practice

Define the behaviour for lost synchronisation before it happens. A sensor whose clock lost its master keeps running on its own crystal (holdover) and drifts by its rate error, for example 20 µs per second. For a few seconds that is harmless; after minutes it is not. The fusion should receive a "not synchronised" flag with the data, keep using the sensor while the accumulated uncertainty is small, inflate the measurement noise accordingly (Section 10.2), and degrade the sensor (Chapter 11) when it exceeds a limit.

Talk track

"Every measurement carries the time of the physical event, stamped in hardware and converted into one vehicle time base. The ECUs agree on that time base with gPTP over Ethernet and with AUTOSAR time sync on CAN; hardware timestamping gets us well below a microsecond, so the protocol is not the problem. The problems are timestamps taken at the wrong moment, clocks mixed up inside one machine, and sensors that silently lost synchronisation."

5Triggering, exposure alignment, buffering and out-of-order data

Synchronised clocks let every measurement say when it happened. They do not make the sensors measure at the same moments, they do not make a camera frame a single instant, and they do not make the data arrive in the order they were taken. Those three problems are what this chapter is about.

5.1Triggering: aligning when sensors measure

Some sensors can be told when to measure. Cameras in slave mode start a frame on an external pulse, so several cameras on one computer can expose within microseconds of each other (Camera pipeline 6); surround view and stereo depend on it. Spinning lidars can be phase-locked to the time base so that the laser sweeps across the front camera's field of view exactly while the camera exposes. Radars usually cannot be triggered from outside: their chirp sequences, interference mitigation and processing pipeline run on their own schedule, and at best they can be configured to a fixed cycle relative to the synchronised time.

Triggering does not replace timestamps, and synchronised timestamps do not replace triggering. With triggering but wrong timestamps, the sensors measure together but the fusion places them wrongly relative to the ego motion. With correct timestamps but no triggering, the fusion knows exactly when each measurement happened but must predict across the gaps between them. For early fusion of raw data (projecting lidar points into an image, Chapter 7) triggering is close to essential, because raw data cannot be "predicted" to another time; for detection-level fusion with a tracker, correct timestamps are enough, because the tracker's motion model bridges the gaps.

5.2The timestamp of a camera detection

A camera image is not one instant. Each row integrates light for the exposure time, and in a rolling-shutter sensor each row starts and ends its exposure one line time after the previous one (typically 10–30 µs), so a 1080-row frame spans 10–30 ms from top to bottom (Camera pipeline 3). The best single time for an object is the middle of the exposure of the rows the object occupies. If the capture hardware stamps the frame start \(t_{\text{SOF}}\) when row 0 is read out, the mid-exposure of row \(i\) is approximately

\[ t_i = t_{\text{SOF}} + i\,t_{\text{line}} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,t_{\text{exp}} + t_{\text{fixed}}, \]

where \(t_{\text{fixed}}\) is a sensor-specific constant between readout and the frame-start packet. In the running example the pedestrian's rows are around row 600, the line time is 15 µs and the exposure is 8 ms: the mid-exposure is 9 ms after the frame start minus 4 ms, so 5 ms after the frame-start stamp. Using the frame-start stamp alone would make the camera 5 ms early for this object and up to 16 ms wrong across the image.

The exposure time changes from frame to frame under auto-exposure: a few hundred microseconds in sunlight, up to 10–30 ms at night. A fixed correction calibrated in daylight is therefore wrong at night, which is a classic way for night-time fusion to be worse than daytime fusion for reasons unrelated to image quality. HDR sensors that merge several exposures complicate it further: the long exposure dominates dark regions and the short one bright regions, so different parts of the image have different effective times. Read the per-frame exposure from the embedded data the sensor sends with each frame.

5.3Two sensors on one time axis

Put both sensors of the running example on one time axis and the central problem of this chapter becomes visible.

The camera path is long because it is frame-based: the detector cannot start before the frame is complete in memory, and it may wait behind another network sharing the accelerator. The radar path is short because the radar's DSP is dedicated to it. The result is that the measurement order (camera at 100, radar at 112) and the arrival order (radar at 142, camera at 150) disagree. With several cameras, radars and a lidar, each with its own latency and jitter, almost every cycle contains such inversions.

A Kalman filter must process measurements in time order, because its prediction step only goes forward: it can predict the state from 100 ms to 112 ms, but not back from 112 ms to 100 ms. A measurement older than the filter's current state is an out-of-sequence measurement (OOSM). Ignoring the problem and applying it as if it were current puts it at the wrong ego pose: in a 20°/s turn, 12 ms of mismatch is 0.14 m sideways at 34 m, nearly twice the camera's lateral sigma.

5.4Handling out-of-sequence measurements

There are three families of solutions. Step through them on the example.

At 142 ms the radar detection of 112 ms arrives and is processed: the track now describes the world at 112 ms. At 150 ms the camera detection of 100 ms arrives. It is older than the track, and the filter has no way to go back.

Collect arrivals in a buffer sorted by measurement time, and at each fusion cycle process only what is older than now − L, where \(L\) is at least the worst-case latency of any sensor. Everything is processed in exact time order and the filter stays simple. The price is that the track always describes the world at least \(L\) ago, so the output must be extrapolated further (Chapter 6), and its uncertainty grows with \(L\). Sensors that are late beyond \(L\) are treated as missing.

Process every measurement as soon as it arrives, but keep a short history of filter states and the measurements applied to them. When a late measurement arrives, restore the last state before its time, apply it, and re-apply every newer measurement in order. The result is exactly what in-order processing would have given, with the lowest latency. The price is memory for the history (a few hundred milliseconds) and bursts of CPU when a slow sensor triggers a replay of many updates for many tracks; it also complicates data association, because an earlier association may change on replay.

A measurement older than the history is discarded, and the discard rate is monitored because a rising rate means a sensor is getting slower. Specialised OOSM algorithms (Bar-Shalom's "retrodiction" family) update the current state directly with an old measurement using the cross-covariance between the state at the measurement time and now, without a stored history. They are exact for linear systems with a one-step lag and approximate otherwise, and are used where memory or determinism rule out replays.

Production systems mix these. A common design runs the fusion cyclically (for example every 50 ms), buffers measurements, uses a lag just long enough for the fast sensors, and handles the occasional late camera frame by replay or discards it if it is older than the history. The choice is a latency-versus-complexity trade-off, and it must be made consciously, because the latency it adds is part of the end-to-end budget of the braking function.

Pitfall

Sorting by arrival time hidden in middleware. A publish-subscribe framework that delivers messages from several sensors through one callback queue sorts them by arrival, not by measurement time. Everything works in the lab with small latencies and degrades in the car under load, where the camera path slows down. A fusion that logs, per sensor, the distribution of "arrival time minus measurement time" catches this early.

5.5Buffers, cycles and the output time

A fusion is either event-driven (it updates whenever a measurement arrives) or cyclic (it collects measurements and runs at a fixed rate). Event-driven fusion has the lowest latency but a variable output rate; cyclic fusion fits AUTOSAR scheduling and makes the timing analysable. Either way the buffers between sensors and fusion must be bounded, with a defined policy when they are full: for perception, dropping the oldest data is almost always right, because a stale measurement is worth less than a fresh one (Concurrency 8).

Every output must state the time it is valid for. A track list stamped "112 ms" that is published at 155 ms and consumed by the braking function at 160 ms must be extrapolated by 48 ms by someone, and the design must say who: the fusion (publishing the prediction to the publication time) or the consumer (extrapolating to its own time). Extrapolating twice, or not at all, are both common integration bugs.

6Latency compensation: interpolation and extrapolation

Every measurement describes the past, and every decision is about the present or the future. Latency compensation moves information along the time axis: backwards to the measurement time for the ego pose, forwards to the decision time for the objects, and with honest uncertainty in both directions.

6.1Why everything arrives in the past

In the running example the camera detection is 50 ms old when it arrives, the radar detection 30 ms, and the braking function acts on the fused track perhaps 20 ms after that. In 70 ms at 8 m/s the car moves 56 cm. If nobody compensates, the system reacts to where the pedestrian was relative to where the car was, and every downstream quantity, from time to collision to the decision whether the pedestrian is in the path, is biased in a predictable direction. Latency compensation is the systematic removal of that bias.

There are two different motions to compensate. The ego motion changes where each sensor was when it measured; it is known precisely from odometry and the IMU, so it is interpolated from a history of past poses. The object motion is what the tracker estimates; it is predicted by the motion model, which adds uncertainty. Keeping the two separate is what makes the compensation accurate.

6.2Ego-motion compensation: the pose at the measurement time

The fusion keeps a buffer of ego poses \(T_{\text{odom},\text{veh}}(t)\), typically at 100 Hz from wheel odometry and the IMU, covering the last second or so. A measurement stamped \(t_m\) is transformed into the odometry frame with the pose at exactly \(t_m\), interpolated between the two neighbouring poses: linearly for the translation, by spherical linear interpolation (slerp) for the rotation:

\[ p_{\text{odom}} = T_{\text{odom},\text{veh}}(t_m)\;T_{\text{veh},\text{sensor}}\;p_{\text{sensor}}. \]

Because the pose buffer looks backwards, this is interpolation, not extrapolation, as long as the odometry is faster and less delayed than the sensors, which it usually is. The odometry frame then holds still while the car moves through it, and the tracker sees the pedestrian walking in a straight line instead of a curve distorted by the car's own motion.

For sensors whose measurements span time, ego-motion compensation applies within one measurement. A spinning lidar takes 100 ms per revolution; at 30 m/s the car moves 3 m during one sweep, so the points of one scan must each be transformed with the pose at their own firing time (de-skewing), otherwise a straight wall appears bent and the start and end of the sweep do not meet. The same applies to rolling-shutter cameras when geometry is measured across many rows, for example in visual odometry.

6.3Object motion: predict to the measurement, extrapolate to the output

The tracker's prediction step already contains the object half of latency compensation, as long as it predicts to the measurement time rather than to "now": before an update with a measurement from \(t_m\), every track is predicted from its last time to \(t_m\). Combined with ego-motion compensation and time-ordered processing (Chapter 5), this means measurements from sensors with very different latencies are fused correctly.

At the output, the opposite happens: the track, valid at the time of its last update, is extrapolated to the time its consumer needs, using the same motion model. Its covariance grows during extrapolation: for a constant-velocity model, the position uncertainty grows roughly as \(\sigma_v\,\Delta t\) plus the process noise, so 50 ms of extrapolation with a velocity uncertainty of 0.3 m/s adds about 1.5 cm, harmless, while 300 ms with 1.5 m/s adds 45 cm. The extrapolation horizon, and the velocity uncertainty, therefore decide how much latency a function can tolerate. Extrapolation is only as good as the motion model: a pedestrian who stops or a car that brakes is extrapolated wrongly, which is a further reason to keep latency small rather than compensating ever larger latencies.

Why it matters

The output timestamp is part of the interface. A fused object list without a validity time, or with the time of publication instead of the time the state refers to, makes correct extrapolation impossible downstream. Interfaces for object lists (for example ISO 23150 for sensor-to-fusion data) carry timestamps for exactly this reason.

6.5Finding unknown time offsets from data

Time offsets that are not measured can often be estimated from data, because a time offset produces errors that correlate with motion. Three methods are worth knowing. Cross-correlation of a signal both sensors observe, such as the yaw rate from the IMU and the rotation rate from visual odometry, or the range rate of a tracked object from radar and from differentiated camera range, gives the lag at the correlation peak; with a few minutes of driving with turns it reaches millisecond accuracy. State augmentation adds the time offset to a filter's state and lets the filter estimate it, as in camera–IMU odometry (and in Kalibr's offline version). Residual analysis plots the fusion's normalised innovations against ego yaw rate: if the camera's lateral residuals grow linearly with yaw rate, the camera's timestamps are off, and the slope gives the offset.

Timestamp and calibration errors of the sensor that dominates a direction pass straight into the fused track and the other sensor cannot see them, so we verify time offsets from data, for example by correlating residuals with yaw rate."

7Fusion architectures: where to combine the information

With space and time aligned, the next design question is what to fuse: raw sensor data, intermediate features, detections, or finished tracks. The answer shapes the whole system: the bandwidth between sensors and computer, how precise calibration and synchronisation must be, which supplier delivers what, and how a failure can be explained afterwards.

7.1Fusion levels

In early fusion (raw-data or low-level fusion) the sensors' raw outputs go into one algorithm, typically a neural network that sees camera pixels together with radar returns or lidar points projected into the image or into a bird's-eye grid. Nothing is thrown away before the sensors meet, so the network can use correlations no hand-written rule would find, such as a weak radar return that only becomes credible next to a faint pedestrian silhouette. The price is high bandwidth, strict synchronisation (raw data cannot be predicted to another time) and calibration accuracy at the level of pixels, and a monolithic component that is hard to validate sensor by sensor.

In feature-level fusion each sensor has its own encoder network, and the features are combined in a common space, today usually a bird's-eye-view grid. This is the dominant architecture of learned multi-camera and camera–radar perception (BEVFusion and its relatives), and it belongs to the BEV and 3D perception chapter; for this chapter it matters that the fusion's output is again a set of detections or an occupancy grid that a tracker consumes.

In detection-level fusion, also called central or measurement-level tracking, each sensor runs its own detection and the fusion runs one tracker over all detections, each with its own measurement model and noise. This is the classic architecture of automotive object fusion and the subject of Chapters 8 and 9. It needs good timestamps and calibration but tolerates different rates and latencies, because the tracker's motion model bridges the gaps.

In object-level or late fusion, each sensor delivers tracked objects (the radar's internal tracker, a smart camera's object list), and the fusion combines tracks with tracks. Bandwidth is tiny and the interface between supplier and vehicle manufacturer is simple, which is why many production vehicles of the last decade work this way. The cost is information loss (each sensor's tracker has already smoothed, delayed and filtered) and the correlation problem of Section 7.3.

Interesting detail

Safety and machine learning pull in different directions here. ISO 26262 decomposition likes two independent channels whose failures are unlikely to coincide; early fusion merges them into one channel by design. Some architectures therefore run both: a learned early-fusion path for performance and an object-level path with simpler, separately validated components as a monitor or fallback (Standards 2).

7.3Centralised versus distributed: the correlation trap

Fusing two tracks of the same object looks like fusing two measurements: combine the estimates weighted by their inverse covariances, \(P^{-1} = P_a^{-1} + P_b^{-1}\). That formula assumes the two estimates have independent errors. Tracks violate this in two ways. If both trackers used the same measurements (for example a camera object list fed into both a camera tracker and a fusion tracker), the information is counted twice. And even with disjoint measurements, both trackers predicted the same object with the same kind of motion model, so the unknown accelerations of the object enter both tracks: the common process noise correlates their errors. Fusing as if independent gives a covariance that is too small, a filter that trusts itself too much, gates that become too tight and, eventually, lost tracks.

There are three standard answers. If the cross-covariance between the two tracks can be computed (both trackers on one computer with known models), the Bar-Shalom–Campo formula fuses them correctly. If not, covariance intersection gives a fused estimate that is consistent for any unknown correlation:

\[ P_{CI}^{-1} = \omega P_a^{-1} + (1-\omega)P_b^{-1}, \qquad \hat x_{CI} = P_{CI}\big(\omega P_a^{-1}\hat x_a + (1-\omega)P_b^{-1}\hat x_b\big), \]

with \(\omega \in [0, 1]\) chosen to minimise the trace or determinant of \(P_{CI}\). It is conservative (it never claims more than the better input could justify on its own in the worst case), which is exactly what one wants when the correlation is unknown. The third answer is architectural: avoid track-to-track fusion where possible, and fuse detections centrally, which is a strong argument for detection-level fusion when the sensors allow it.

Pitfall

Feeding the fused output back into a sensor's tracker, for example to "help" the camera tracker with the fused velocity, and then fusing the camera tracks again. Information goes round the loop and is counted many times; the covariance shrinks with every cycle while the error does not. Data flow in a fusion architecture must be a tree or have explicit decorrelation.

7.4What production systems look like

A typical driver-assistance system of the last generation has a front radar and a front camera from suppliers, each delivering an object list over CAN FD or Ethernet, and an object-level fusion in the radar, the camera or a separate ECU. A typical system of the current generation has a central computer with eight to twelve cameras, one to five radars and possibly a lidar; the cameras deliver raw images, the radars detections or radar tensors, and the computer runs learned fusion in a bird's-eye view, followed by a model-based tracker that produces the object list for planning, with occupancy grids for everything that is not a classifiable object. The running example of this chapter sits in the middle: detections from both sensors into one central tracker. The estimator ideas are the same at every level; what changes is where the uncertainty is explicit and where it is learned.

Talk track

"Fusion can happen on raw data, on learned features, on detections or on tracks. Earlier fusion keeps more information but needs more bandwidth, tighter synchronisation and calibration, and is harder to validate per sensor. Late fusion is modular and supplier-friendly but loses information and must handle correlated track errors, for example with covariance intersection. Most new systems fuse cameras and radars in a learned bird's-eye view and still run an explicit tracker on top."

8Bayesian estimation beyond the linear Kalman filter

The linear Kalman filter is the optimal estimator for linear models with Gaussian noise. The running example has neither linear measurements (radar measures range and angle, the camera measures a bearing) nor, at times, Gaussian beliefs. This chapter starts from the linear filter you already know and goes to the extended and unscented Kalman filters and to particle filters, with the running example worked through step by step.

8.1The Bayes filter, and the Kalman filter as its special case

Every filter in this chapter implements the same two-step recursion of beliefs. The prediction pushes the belief about the state through the motion model, \(p(x_k \mid z_{1:k-1}) = \int p(x_k \mid x_{k-1})\,p(x_{k-1} \mid z_{1:k-1})\,dx_{k-1}\), which blurs it. The update multiplies it by the likelihood of the new measurement and renormalises, \(p(x_k \mid z_{1:k}) \propto p(z_k \mid x_k)\,p(x_k \mid z_{1:k-1})\), which sharpens it. The filters differ only in how they represent the belief and how they approximate the two steps.

The linear Kalman filter represents the belief as a Gaussian (mean \(\hat x\), covariance \(P\)) and is exact when the motion \(x_k = F x_{k-1} + w\) and measurement \(z = H x + v\) are linear with Gaussian noise \(w \sim \mathcal N(0, Q)\), \(v \sim \mathcal N(0, R)\). Its equations are recapped in Classic CV 15.2 and Mathematics 8: predict \(\hat x^- = F\hat x\), \(P^- = FPF^{\top} + Q\); innovation \(y = z - H\hat x^-\) with covariance \(S = HP^-H^{\top} + R\); gain \(K = P^-H^{\top}S^{-1}\); update \(\hat x = \hat x^- + Ky\), \(P = (I - KH)P^-\). The gain weighs the measurement against the prediction by their uncertainties, and it does so per direction in state space, which is what makes it a fusion engine: a measurement that is precise in one direction and vague in another corrects the state only in the direction it knows.

8.2The running example as a filter

The fusion tracks the pedestrian in the odometry frame with a constant-velocity state \(x = (p_x, p_y, v_x, v_y)\): position and velocity over ground. Between measurements, the state moves with \(p \leftarrow p + v\,\Delta t\), and the pedestrian's unknown accelerations are modelled as white noise with standard deviation \(\sigma_a = 1\) m/s², which gives per axis the process noise

\[ Q_{\text{axis}} = \sigma_a^2 \begin{bmatrix} \Delta t^4/4 & \Delta t^3/2 \\ \Delta t^3/2 & \Delta t^2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The motion model is linear. The measurement models are not. With \(d = p - s(t)\) the vector from the sensor position \(s(t)\) (known from the ego pose and the extrinsics) to the pedestrian, \(r = \lVert d \rVert\) and \(u = v - v_{\text{ego}}\) the velocity relative to the sensor:

\[ h_{\text{cam}}(x) = \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{atan2}(d_y, d_x) \\ r \end{bmatrix}, \qquad h_{\text{radar}}(x) = \begin{bmatrix} r \\ \operatorname{atan2}(d_y, d_x) \\ (d \cdot u)/r \end{bmatrix}, \]

with \(R_{\text{cam}} = \operatorname{diag}\big((0.15°)^2, (0.08\,r)^2\big)\) and \(R_{\text{radar}} = \operatorname{diag}\big((0.25\text{ m})^2, (1°)^2, (0.1\text{ m/s})^2\big)\). The camera's range noise grows with range, so its \(R\) depends on the state, which is normal in practice. The radar's third measurement, range rate, is the projection of the relative velocity onto the line of sight, which mixes position and velocity nonlinearly.

Modelling the camera in its own measurement space (bearing and range) rather than converting its detections to a Cartesian position first is a deliberate choice. A Cartesian conversion produces a strongly correlated, elongated covariance that changes with the bearing, and it hides the fact that the bearing is precise and the range is not. In measurement space the noise is simple and diagonal, and the filter's linearisation does the conversion where it belongs.

Camera only: after 0.4 s the along-sight uncertainty is still 0.72 m and the closing speed ±1.4 m/s, almost unchanged from the initial guess, because monocular range noise is too large to differentiate. Radar only: the across-sight uncertainty settles at about 0.25 m and the crossing speed is still ±1.06 m/s. Dropout: when the camera disappears at 0.15 s, the across-sight uncertainty grows from 0.07 m to 0.17 m within a quarter of a second, and the crossing-speed estimate stalls. The filter remains consistent (its ellipses grow honestly) but the function relying on it loses exactly the quantity the camera provided.

In practice

The normalised innovation squared (NIS, \(y^{\top}S^{-1}y\)) is shown for each step. For a consistent filter it follows a chi-square distribution with as many degrees of freedom as the measurement has (2 for the camera, 3 for the radar), so it averages 2 or 3 and rarely exceeds 9.2 or 11.3. Plot it per sensor over a recorded drive: a sensor whose NIS averages 10 has a wrong \(R\), a wrong calibration or a wrong timestamp.

8.4The extended Kalman filter, with a worked radar update

The extended Kalman filter (EKF) handles nonlinear models \(x_k = f(x_{k-1}) + w\), \(z = h(x) + v\) by linearising them around the current estimate. The mean is propagated through the true nonlinear functions, the covariance through their Jacobians \(F = \partial f/\partial x\) and \(H = \partial h/\partial x\), evaluated at the current estimate:

\[ \hat x^- = f(\hat x), \quad P^- = FPF^{\top} + Q, \qquad y = z - h(\hat x^-), \quad S = HP^-H^{\top} + R, \quad K = P^-H^{\top}S^{-1}. \]

Everything else is the linear filter. Here is one radar update of the running example in numbers (experiments/ekf_worked.py). To keep the numbers readable, the world origin is placed at the radar at the measurement time, axes aligned with it; the radar moves at \(v_{\text{ego}} = (10, 0)\) m/s.

Why it matters

Reading \(H\) and \(K\) like this is the fastest way to answer "what does this sensor contribute to this state?" in design reviews. A zero or near-zero entry in \(H\) means the sensor cannot see that state component from this geometry, whatever the noise figures in its datasheet say. The radar's blindness to crossing speed is a geometric fact, not a quality problem.

8.5When linearisation fails

The EKF approximates the measurement function by its tangent at the current estimate. That is accurate when the function is nearly linear over the region the uncertainty covers, and wrong when it bends noticeably inside that region. For a polar measurement the bending is governed by the angular uncertainty: converting range and azimuth into Cartesian coordinates bends the uncertainty region into an arc around the sensor. With 1–2° of azimuth noise the arc is indistinguishable from a straight segment; with 15° it is a banana.

At 15° the EKF's mean is 0.67 m too far away and its covariance claims 0.30 m along the line of sight where the samples spread 0.97 m. Its normalised estimation error (NEES, the state-space analogue of NIS) on the samples is 16 instead of the expected 2: the EKF is inconsistent, too sure of a wrong answer. The unscented transform gets the mean right and errs slightly on the safe side (NEES 1.5).

Where does this happen in a car? Rarely in the update of an established track, whose uncertainty is small compared with the curvature of \(h\). It happens at initialisation from a single detection with a large angular error (wide-beam corner radars, ultrasonic sensors with ±30° beams), over long prediction horizons with turning motion models, where an uncertain yaw rate bends the position uncertainty into exactly this banana, and with bearing-only tracking, where the range is unknown. The usual remedies are to initialise tracks with a conservative covariance and only after two or three detections, to re-linearise (the iterated EKF repeats the update with \(H\) evaluated at the new estimate until it converges; in the worked example it changes the result by a tenth of a millimetre, which shows the example is well inside the linear regime), or to use an estimator that does not linearise.

8.6The unscented Kalman filter

The unscented Kalman filter (UKF) replaces the Jacobian by a small set of deterministically chosen sample points. For an \(n\)-dimensional state it picks \(2n+1\) sigma points: the mean and, on each side of it, one point along each column of a matrix square root of the scaled covariance, \(\hat x \pm \big(\sqrt{(n+\lambda)P}\big)_i\). Each sigma point is pushed through the true nonlinear function, and the mean and covariance of the results, with fixed weights, are the predicted mean and covariance. For the update the same is done with \(h\), and the cross-covariance between the state and the measurement sigma points gives the gain.

The result captures the mean correctly to second order (the EKF only to first), needs no derivatives, which removes a classic source of bugs, and costs \(2n+1\) function evaluations: 9 for the pedestrian's 4-dimensional state, comparable to computing a Jacobian. It handles the moderate nonlinearities of radar and camera models and of turning motion models gracefully. It does not fix truly multimodal beliefs, since it still represents the belief as one Gaussian, and its tuning parameters (\(\alpha, \beta, \kappa\), which set the spread of the points and can produce negative weights) need care; the cubature Kalman filter is a closely related variant with a fixed, always positive choice.

Pitfall

Averaging angles. A UKF computes means as weighted sums of sigma points; if one sigma point's heading is +179° and another's −179°, the arithmetic mean is 0°, the opposite direction. Angles in the state or measurement must be averaged as unit vectors (or relative to a reference) and innovations wrapped to \((-\pi, \pi]\). The same applies to the EKF's innovation for azimuth measurements near ±180°.

8.7Particle filters

A particle filter drops the Gaussian assumption entirely and represents the belief by \(N\) weighted samples (particles). Prediction moves each particle through the motion model with randomly drawn noise. The update multiplies each particle's weight by the likelihood of the measurement given that particle's state, and normalises. Over time most weights go to zero; when the effective sample size \(N_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sum w_i^2\) falls below a threshold (often \(N/2\)), the filter resamples: it draws \(N\) new particles with probabilities given by the weights, duplicating good particles and dropping bad ones.

The particle filter can represent anything: two hypotheses at once (the car is in the left or the right lane), a belief bounded by walls or kerbs, a likelihood shaped by a map. That makes it the tool of choice for map-based localisation, which belongs to the localisation chapter, and for track-before-detect of very weak targets. Its cost is the number of particles, which must grow roughly exponentially with the dimension of the state to cover it; a 4-dimensional pedestrian state needs hundreds to thousands of particles where a Kalman filter needs one mean and one matrix. For tracking many objects with well-behaved beliefs, Kalman-type filters remain the norm. Hybrid designs (Rao–Blackwellised particle filters) sample only the awkward part of the state and run Kalman filters for the rest.

The motion model matters as much as the filter. A constant-velocity model with large process noise follows manoeuvres but is noisy when the target moves steadily; small process noise smooths but lags. The interacting multiple model (IMM) filter runs several models in parallel (for a pedestrian: walking, standing; for a car: constant velocity, constant turn, braking), mixes their estimates at each step and weights them by how well each explains the measurements, so the track follows a pedestrian who stops without being jittery while the pedestrian walks (Classic CV 15.2 lists the common vehicle models).

9Data association and multi-object fusion

Everything so far assumed that the fusion knows which measurement belongs to which object. In a real street there are several pedestrians, parked cars, poles and radar ghosts, and every cycle the fusion must decide which detection updates which track, which detection is new, and which is noise. Wrong associations do more damage than any amount of measurement noise, because the filter then confidently fuses information about the wrong object.

9.1Why association is the hard part

The estimator of Chapter 8 is optimal given the right measurement. Association decides what the right measurement is, and it is a combinatorial problem under uncertainty: detections are missing (the radar sees a pedestrian with perhaps 80–90% probability per cycle, the camera misses the pedestrian when occluded), there are false detections (radar multipath, guardrail reflections, camera false positives), and objects are close together (a group of pedestrians, a pedestrian next to a parked car). The classic overview is in Classic CV 15.3; here the focus is on the decisions and their numbers, and on what changes when the detections come from different sensors.

9.2Gating

The first step is to rule out impossible pairs. For each track and each detection, compute the innovation \(y = z - h(\hat x^-)\) and its covariance \(S\), and the squared Mahalanobis distance \(d^2 = y^{\top}S^{-1}y\): the innovation measured in units of its expected spread, in every direction. If the detection really belongs to the track and the filter is consistent, \(d^2\) follows a chi-square distribution with as many degrees of freedom as the measurement has, so a fixed threshold on it is a gate with a known probability of containing the true detection.

Because \(S\) includes the track's own uncertainty, a young, uncertain track has a large gate and a mature one a small gate. That is the right behaviour, and it is also the mechanism by which an overconfident filter destroys itself: if \(P\) is too small, the gate is too small, the true detections fall outside it, the track coasts, and it is deleted while the object is still there. In large scenes, a cheap pre-gate (a fixed distance or a grid lookup) avoids computing \(d^2\) for every pair.

9.3Assignment: GNN, JPDA and MHT

After gating, several detections may fall into one track's gate and one detection into several gates. The figure shows the typical hard case: two pedestrians walking side by side, two detections between them, and a third detection that is a radar ghost.

Global nearest neighbour (GNN) chooses the single one-to-one assignment with the smallest total cost, here \(d^2\) summed over the pairs, with a cost for leaving a track unassigned. The Hungarian algorithm (or the faster auction and Jonker–Volgenant algorithms) solves it in polynomial time. In the example GNN assigns z1 to T1 and z2 to T2 with a total of 3.66, against 7.46 for the swap. GNN is simple, fast and deterministic, and it is the default in most automotive trackers. Its weakness is that it commits: if the swap was the truth, both tracks are updated with the wrong detection and may swap identities.

Joint probabilistic data association (JPDA) does not commit. It enumerates the feasible joint assignments, computes the probability of each from the likelihoods, the detection probability and the clutter density, and gives every track–detection pair its marginal probability \(\beta\). In the example T1 belongs to z1 with probability 0.86 and to z2 with 0.13. Each track is then updated with the \(\beta\)-weighted combination of its detections, and its covariance is inflated by the spread between them, so ambiguity becomes uncertainty instead of a hidden error. JPDA is robust in clutter, at the cost of computation (the number of joint events grows quickly with the number of tracks in a cluster) and of tracks that are pulled towards each other when they stay close for long.

Multiple hypothesis tracking (MHT) postpones the decision. It keeps several association hypotheses alive over several frames, scores them as more data arrive, and prunes unlikely ones (for example by keeping only hypotheses that differ in the last N scans). If the swap in the example is real, two frames later the pedestrians have moved apart and the swap hypothesis explains the data better; MHT then switches to it, retroactively. MHT is the most powerful and the most expensive of the three and is used where identities matter over long times or in dense scenes.

A more recent family, random finite set filters (PHD, CPHD, and the Poisson multi-Bernoulli mixture, PMBM), treats the set of objects itself as the random variable and folds detection, clutter, birth and death into one Bayesian recursion. They are increasingly used in research and in some production trackers, especially for extended objects such as vehicles that produce many radar detections.

9.4Associating detections from different sensors

Detection-level fusion associates detections of every sensor with the same set of tracks, and each sensor needs its own view of what "close" means. The camera detection is compared in camera measurement space: a small bearing difference is significant, a range difference of two metres is not. The radar detection is compared in radar space, where range and range rate are sharp and azimuth is vague. Computing \(d^2\) with each sensor's own \(h\) and \(R\) handles this automatically; converting all detections to Cartesian positions with circular gates does not, and leads to camera detections associating with the wrong one of two pedestrians at the same bearing and different ranges.

Other attributes help. Class is the strongest: a camera "pedestrian" detection should not update a track that the radar has seen for seconds as a large, fast object. Radar range rate separates moving from stationary objects for most geometries, and the radar cross section separates cars from people. Vehicles produce many radar detections per cycle from different parts of their body and need extended-object handling (clustering detections, or a measurement model for the object's shape), whereas a pedestrian produces one to a few detections, smeared in Doppler by the moving limbs (micro-Doppler).

Interesting detail

A pedestrian crossing the road looks stationary to the radar. The walking motion is perpendicular to the line of sight, so the range rate is almost exactly that of a pole at the same place: in the worked example of Section 8.4, 1.1 m/s of crossing speed changes the range rate by only 9 cm/s. Radar processing that suppresses stationary targets to reduce clutter therefore also suppresses crossing pedestrians, which is one of the reasons why pedestrian AEB relies on the camera to classify and confirm them.

9.5Track management: birth, confirmation, coasting and death

Association leaves unassigned detections and unassigned tracks. An unassigned detection starts a tentative track, which is confirmed only after it has been associated enough times, classically \(M\) out of \(N\) cycles (for example 3 out of 5), or when its existence probability, updated each cycle from detections and misses with the sensors' detection probabilities and clutter rates, crosses a threshold. A track without a detection coasts on its prediction, its covariance growing, and is deleted after a number of misses or when its existence probability falls. Duplicate tracks of one object, created when association failed for a few cycles, are detected by their mutual Mahalanobis distance and merged.

In a multi-sensor tracker these rules become sensor-aware, and this is where functional requirements enter the tracker. A pedestrian track that only the radar has seen may be kept for situational awareness but not used for emergency braking, because the radar cannot classify it; a track confirmed by both sensors can be. A miss counts as evidence of absence only if the sensor could have seen the object: a camera miss at night beyond its detection range, or a radar miss behind a parked car, should not lower the existence probability (Section 11.3). The confirmation logic directly trades time to react against false alarms, and it is one of the most closely reviewed parts of an AEB fusion.

Track management is sensor-aware: tentative tracks need several hits, braking-relevant pedestrian tracks need camera confirmation, and a miss only counts against a track if the sensor could have seen the object."

10Uncertainty, confidence and conflicting measurements

A fused track is only useful together with a statement of how good it is. The braking function must know not just where the pedestrian probably is, but how sure the fusion is that the pedestrian exists, is a pedestrian, and is inside the car's path. Those statements are only as good as the uncertainty models behind them, and the most dangerous failure of a fusion is not a large error but a small claimed uncertainty around a large error.

10.1A covariance is a promise

Every \(R\) in a measurement model is a claim about the sensor: "my errors are zero-mean, Gaussian, with this spread, and independent from one measurement to the next." Datasheet figures are a starting point, but the claim must be checked against data: record drives with a reference system (a high-grade GNSS/INS on the ego car and on a target, or a reference lidar), compute each sensor's errors, and look at their distribution per range bin, per angle and per condition. Real sensor errors are rarely as tidy as the model. Radar azimuth errors grow towards the edge of the field of view and around strong reflectors; camera range errors grow with the square of distance and depend on the road's slope; both have heavy tails from occasional gross errors. The model can absorb most of this by making \(R\) a function of range, angle and conditions; the tails are handled by gating and by robust updates.

Consistency can be tested with two statistics. The NEES (normalised estimation error squared, \((x - \hat x)^{\top}P^{-1}(x - \hat x)\)) compares the actual error with the claimed covariance and needs ground truth; it should average the state dimension. The NIS (normalised innovation squared, \(y^{\top}S^{-1}y\)) compares the innovations with their claimed covariance and needs no ground truth; it should average the measurement dimension. NIS per sensor on every recorded drive is the routine check; NEES on reference drives is the release check.

10.2Propagating uncertainty, including calibration and time

Uncertainty is propagated in the same three ways as in Chapter 8: linearised (\(J P J^{\top}\)), with sigma points, or by Monte Carlo sampling. The less obvious step is to include the uncertainty of the inputs the fusion normally treats as exact: calibration and time. If the camera's yaw is known to \(\sigma_\psi\) and its timestamps to \(\sigma_t\), the effective measurement noise is

\[ R_{\text{eff}} = R + J_\psi\,\sigma_\psi^2\,J_\psi^{\top} + J_t\,\sigma_t^2\,J_t^{\top}, \qquad J_t = \frac{\partial h}{\partial t}, \]

where \(J_t\) is the rate at which the measurement changes, which depends on the motion. For the camera bearing of the running example in a 10°/s turn, the bearing changes at about 0.22 rad/s, so 5 ms of timestamp uncertainty adds 0.06° to the 0.15° bearing noise, about 8% more standard deviation; a 0.1° calibration uncertainty adds more, about 20%. Time uncertainty is therefore motion-dependent noise: invisible when standing, significant in turns, which is the quantitative form of Section 6.4.

There is a catch. Inflating \(R\) treats a calibration error as if it were new random noise in every measurement, but it is the same error every time: a bias. A filter that averages many measurements will average the noise away and keep the bias, so its covariance shrinks below the true error even with the inflated \(R\). The principled remedies are to estimate the bias as an extra state (the filter then calibrates online), or to carry it as a "consider" parameter in a Schmidt–Kalman filter, which accounts for its effect on the covariance without estimating it. The pragmatic remedy is a lower bound on the track covariance that reflects the calibration accuracy.

10.3Existence, class and state: three kinds of confidence

A track carries three different uncertainties, and functions need all three. The state uncertainty is the covariance: where the object is and how it moves. The existence probability is the probability that the track corresponds to a real object at all, raised by detections and lowered by misses where a detection was expected, with each sensor's detection probability and false-alarm rate. The class distribution says what the object is, accumulated from the camera's classifier and supported by radar features such as cross section and micro-Doppler.

Detection scores from neural networks are often used as inputs to existence and class, but a score of 0.9 is not a probability of 0.9 unless the network has been calibrated on representative data (for example with temperature scaling and reliability diagrams), and calibration that holds in daylight may not hold at night. A fusion that multiplies uncalibrated scores as if they were independent probabilities produces existence probabilities that are confidently wrong in exactly the way covariances can be.

A braking decision typically combines all three: the existence probability above a high threshold, the class "pedestrian" or "cyclist" with sufficient probability, and the lateral position, with its uncertainty, inside the predicted path. The last condition is where Chapters 2 to 8 meet the function: a lateral uncertainty of 0.1 m lets the function decide early whether a pedestrian at the edge of the path is inside it; an uncertainty of 0.5 m forces it to wait or to accept more false interventions.

10.4When sensors disagree

Disagreements come in two kinds. In the first, one sensor sees an object and the other does not. Whether that is a miss or a false detection depends on whether the silent sensor could have seen it: a camera at night beyond its detection range, a radar looking at a pedestrian behind a parked car, a sensor whose field of view does not cover the place. A fusion that models each sensor's detection probability as a function of range, angle, occlusion and conditions turns this into arithmetic: a miss reduces the existence probability by a factor of about \(1 - P_D\) for that sensor in that situation, which is a strong reduction for a sensor that should have seen the object and none for one that could not.

In the second, both sensors see something but at incompatible places, outside each other's gates. Then one of them is wrong (a radar multipath ghost, a camera detection on a reflection or a poster), or there are two objects, or a calibration or timestamp has failed. The rule here is never to average incompatible hypotheses. If one sensor says the object is left of a pole and another says it is right of it, the average is the pole, the one place it certainly is not. Gating keeps the hypotheses separate; association with clutter models and existence probabilities decides which survives; a third sensor or later measurements break the tie.

Several tools help decide whom to trust. Context-dependent trust lowers a sensor's weight where it is known to be weak (radar under bridges and in tunnels, camera in low sun or spray), by raising its \(R\), lowering its detection probability or excluding it from confirming tracks. Robust updates replace the Gaussian likelihood by a heavy-tailed one (Student-t, Huber), so that a gross outlier inside the gate pulls the estimate less. Voting with three or more independent sources (two out of three) is the classic safety pattern when diverse sensors are available. Evidence theory (Dempster–Shafer) is used in some occupancy grids to keep "no evidence" separate from "evidence of free space", which plain probabilities blur.

10.5Overconfidence: the failure to fear

An overconfident filter is worse than an inaccurate one, because every consumer believes it. The causes have appeared throughout this chapter: biases treated as noise (calibration, timestamps), correlated errors fused as independent (Section 7.3), information counted twice in feedback loops, process noise too small for the targets' real manoeuvres, linearisation at large uncertainty (Section 8.5), and uncalibrated detection scores. The symptoms are NIS that averages too high, tracks lost because true detections fall outside shrinking gates, and confident decisions on wrong positions.

The defences are equally consistent: model uncertainty generously where unsure, bound covariances from below, test consistency with NIS routinely and NEES on reference data, and prefer architectures that avoid unknown correlations. A slightly underconfident filter costs some performance; an overconfident one costs safety.

Talk track

"Every track carries three confidences: the covariance of its state, the probability that it exists, and its class distribution, and each must be calibrated against data, not taken from a datasheet. We include calibration and timing uncertainty in the measurement noise, handle biases as states or covariance floors, and check consistency with NIS per sensor on every drive. When sensors disagree we never average incompatible hypotheses: we ask whether the silent sensor could have seen the object, and we let gating, existence and more data decide."

11Missing, delayed, faulty and degraded sensors

Sensors fail, and in a car they fail in many partial ways: a frame lost, a cycle late, a lens dirty, a bumper knocked, a clock adrift. Fusion is the place where these failures either become graceful degradation or become wrong decisions. The difference lies in detecting them quickly, telling the estimator what they mean, and telling the functions what the fusion can still promise.

11.2Detecting it

Communication checks catch missing, repeated, stale and corrupted data: timeouts per sensor, alive counters, and end-to-end protection (AUTOSAR E2E profiles with a CRC, a sequence counter and a data identifier on safety-relevant messages), which detects loss, repetition, insertion, wrong sequence and corruption between sender and receiver. Timestamp checks catch time faults: measurement times must increase, latencies must lie within a plausible window, and the source must report itself as synchronised. A repeated frame with a new timestamp is caught only by content checks (frame counters from the sensor's embedded data, image difference), which is why camera pipelines carry the sensor's own frame counter end to end (Camera pipeline 13).

Self-diagnosis catches degradation. Radars detect blockage from the absence of expected near-range clutter and ground returns, and interference from raised noise floors. Cameras detect blockage and blinding from image statistics: sharpness, contrast, saturation, unchanging regions. Both report a degradation level with the data. Cross-sensor consistency catches what self-diagnosis misses: an object that the radar has tracked for two seconds in the camera's clear field of view without a single camera detection says something about the camera or about the radar track; a persistent bearing offset between associated detections says something about calibration (Section 3.5). These checks are slow by nature (they need statistics), and they are debounced before they set a diagnostic trouble code, to avoid flagging a single shadow as a blocked camera (Camera ECU 11.3).

11.3Reacting inside the fusion

The fusion keeps a state per sensor (available, degraded, faulty, unavailable) and adapts the estimator to it. A missing sensor contributes nothing; tracks it alone supported coast on their predictions with growing covariance and decaying existence, and are deleted when their uncertainty is too large to be useful. A delayed sensor is fine within the fusion's lag or history (Chapter 5) and is treated as missing beyond it, rather than applied late. A degraded sensor is used with inflated \(R\), a reduced detection probability, and reduced rights: it may update existing tracks but not create or confirm them, and its classifications count less. A faulty sensor is excluded completely, because a fault such as a misalignment produces data that are consistent with themselves and wrong, the hardest kind for an estimator to reject.

The most important idea in this section is negative information. A sensor that detects nothing at a place where it should have seen an object provides evidence against the object; a sensor that is blind there provides none. The existence update must use the detection probability the sensor has now, given its state, the conditions and occlusions, not its nominal value. Otherwise a dirty camera that stops detecting pedestrians does not just stop helping: it actively deletes the radar's pedestrian tracks, turning a degraded sensor into a missed braking event.

Pitfall

Treating the time base as infallible. All sensors share one gPTP grandmaster; if it fails or steps its time, every sensor's timestamps are affected at once, and cross-sensor checks see nothing wrong because all sensors moved together relative to each other but not relative to the ego-motion history. The time base is a common cause: monitor its status, define holdover behaviour, and check timestamps against a local monotonic clock as well.

11.4Reacting at system level: graceful degradation

The fusion's output must say what it can still promise: per-sensor status, the areas where it can and cannot detect objects (a coverage or visibility map), and for each track which sensors support it. Functions use this to choose a degraded mode, defined in advance and analysed in the safety concept.

12The whole path: one pedestrian from photons and chirps to a fused track

This chapter puts the pieces together for one cycle of the running example, with the numbers of the simulation behind Figure 8.1, and then turns to the two questions every fusion team faces in the end: how to prove it works, and how to find out why it does not.

12.1One cycle, step by step

The track exists since the first camera detection at 0 ms; the radar has updated it at 12 and 62 ms and the camera at 33 and 67 ms. The fusion uses rewind and replay (Section 5.4).

  1. 96–104 ms, light. The rows of the camera image that contain the pedestrian integrate light for 8 ms. The capture hardware in the SoC, whose clock is disciplined to the vehicle's gPTP time, stamps the frame-start packet at 95.0 ms. Software adds the row offset (600 rows × 15 µs) and subtracts half the exposure read from the embedded data: the detection's time is 100.0 ms.
  2. 102–122 ms, chirps. The radar transmits its chirp frame. Its clock is a gPTP slave of the central computer, and it stamps the detection list with the middle of the frame: 112.0 ms. Range 29.7 m, azimuth −6.4°, range rate −8.2 m/s, each with its own noise.
  3. 142 ms, the radar list arrives. The fusion checks the E2E counter and CRC, the synchronisation flag and the latency (30 ms, inside the expected window), and puts the list into the buffer. It interpolates the ego pose at 112 ms from the odometry history, computes the radar's position in the odometry frame through the extrinsics, predicts the track from 67 to 112 ms, gates (NIS 1.15, far inside the gate of 11.34), and updates. The along-sight uncertainty drops from 0.18 to 0.14 m; across the line of sight it stays at 0.10 m, because the radar's azimuth adds little there.
  4. 150 ms, the camera detection arrives, out of order. It was measured at 100 ms, before the state's time. The fusion restores the stored state at 67 ms, predicts to 100 ms with the ego pose interpolated at 100 ms, updates with the camera (across-sight uncertainty 0.090 → 0.061 m), predicts to 112 ms and re-applies the radar detection. The result is exactly what in-order processing gives: position uncertainty 0.14 m along and 0.07 m across the line of sight.
  5. About 152 ms, output. The track is published with the time it refers to, 112 ms, together with its covariance, an existence probability, the class "pedestrian" from the camera, and the information that both sensors support it. The braking function extrapolates it to its own time; 40–50 ms of extrapolation add only a few centimetres of position uncertainty.
  6. What the function cannot know yet. At this point, 112 ms after the first detection, the crossing speed is still uncertain by ±0.87 m/s: whether the pedestrian is walking into the path is not yet decided. It takes until about 300 ms for that uncertainty to fall below 0.3 m/s. That quarter of a second, set by sensor geometry rather than by computing speed, is part of the reaction time of pedestrian AEB.

Every step in this list is a place where the earlier chapters' pitfalls can enter: a timestamp from the wrong event in step 1, an unsynchronised radar clock in step 2, arrival-order processing in step 4, a missing validity time in step 5. Most fusion bugs found in vehicles are in this list, not in the filter equations.

12.2Proving that it works

Fusion is validated against a reference that is much more accurate than the sensors: an RTK-GNSS/INS on the ego car and on the target (for test-track scenarios with pedestrian dummies on moving platforms, as in Euro NCAP's AEB tests, Standards 8.2), or a reference lidar with offline-labelled tracks for real traffic. Against it, the key metrics are position and velocity errors per range and angle bin (bias and spread), consistency (NEES should average the state dimension), latency from measurement to output, time to confirm a new object, false tracks per hour, missed objects, identity switches, and the calibration of existence probabilities.

Most of this work happens in replay: recorded sensor data with their original timestamps fed into the fusion in software or on a hardware-in-the-loop bench, which makes every change comparable on the same thousands of kilometres. Replay must preserve the timing behaviour, including arrival order and latencies, or it will validate a system that never sees out-of-order data. Fault injection on replayed data (drop a sensor, add a timestamp offset, rotate an extrinsic, freeze a frame) tests the degradation design of Chapter 11, and the interactive of Section 6.4 lists the first test cases: a 0.5° yaw error and a 30 ms timestamp error should be detected by the monitors, not by a customer.

12.3When camera and radar tracks do not match: a debugging order

The most common fusion bug report is "camera and radar see the same object in different places". The fastest way to the cause is to ask when the mismatch appears, because each cause has its own signature.

  1. Static test first. Car standing, a corner reflector with a marker at several known positions. A mismatch here is spatial: frames, signs, extrinsics or calibration version. An error that grows with range is a rotation; a constant one is a translation; a mirrored one is a sign or axis convention.
  2. Driving straight. A mismatch along the driving direction that grows with speed is a time offset: divide the distance by the speed to get it.
  3. Turning. A lateral mismatch that grows with yaw rate and range is a time offset seen through rotation; the slope against yaw rate gives the offset (Section 6.5).
  4. Only under load or at night. Variable latency processed in arrival order (Chapter 5), software timestamps under load, or exposure-time corrections that are right only in daylight (Section 5.2).
  5. Only for some objects. Association: two pedestrians swapped, a radar ghost captured by a track, a vehicle's many radar detections clustered wrongly.
  6. Everywhere, slowly worsening. Calibration drift or a moved sensor: compare with the online calibration and the cross-sensor residual monitor.
  7. Fused track right, function wrong. Output time and extrapolation: who extrapolates, from which time, by how much.
Talk track

"For one pedestrian: the camera's detection is stamped at mid-exposure of its rows, the radar's at the middle of its chirp frame, both in gPTP time. They arrive out of order, so the fusion rewinds, applies them in measurement order with the ego pose interpolated at each time, and publishes a track with the time it refers to. Position converges within a few updates because the sensors complement each other; crossing speed needs about a quarter of a second of camera bearings, and that, not compute, sets part of the AEB reaction time. To debug a mismatch, we ask whether it appears standing, driving, turning or under load, because each cause has a different signature."

QSelf-check questions

Core questions with short model answers; the chapter sections hold the depth. The second group checks understanding with "why" and "what if" questions rather than definitions.

Q.1Core questions

Why fuse camera and radar instead of using the best single sensor?Basic

They are complementary: the camera measures bearing precisely and classifies objects but estimates range poorly; the radar measures range and radial velocity precisely and works in darkness and rain but has coarse angle and no reliable class. Fused, the position is precise in both directions, both velocity components become observable, and an object confirmed by two physical principles is much less likely to be a ghost. Fusion also gives graceful degradation when one sensor is blinded.

What are intrinsics and extrinsics, and how would you calibrate a radar to a camera?Basic

Intrinsics describe how a sensor maps the world to its raw measurement (focal length and distortion, antenna phase calibration); extrinsics are its pose relative to the vehicle or another sensor. For camera to radar, use corner reflectors with visual markers at several ranges and angles: the camera gives each marker's 3D position by PnP, the radar its range and azimuth, and a weighted nonlinear least-squares fit solves for yaw and planar offset; height and pitch are set mechanically because the radar measures little elevation. Check the residuals against the sensors' noise.

Explain early, feature-level, detection-level and object-level fusion.Basic

Early fusion combines raw data in one algorithm, usually a network; feature-level fusion combines per-sensor learned features in a common space such as BEV; detection-level fusion feeds every sensor's detections into one tracker; object-level fusion combines per-sensor tracks. Moving later reduces bandwidth and coupling and improves per-sensor explainability, but loses information and introduces correlated track errors; moving earlier needs tighter synchronisation and calibration and is harder to validate.

How does PTP synchronise two clocks?Intermediate

The master sends Sync at \(t_1\) (sent in Follow_Up for two-step clocks), the slave receives it at \(t_2\), the slave sends Delay_Req at \(t_3\), the master receives it at \(t_4\) and returns it. Offset is \(\tfrac12[(t_2-t_1)-(t_4-t_3)]\) and delay \(\tfrac12[(t_2-t_1)+(t_4-t_3)]\), assuming a symmetric path. All stamps are taken in hardware at the network interface, and a servo corrects offset and rate. gPTP (802.1AS) is the layer-2 automotive profile with peer-to-peer link delay measurement and rate-ratio tracking; vehicles use a static grandmaster without BMCA.

Hardware versus software timestamps?Basic

A hardware timestamp is latched by a circuit when the event's signal passes (frame start in the camera receiver, a packet at the MAC); it has nanosecond to microsecond error and does not depend on load. A software timestamp is taken by code and includes interrupt latency, scheduling and queueing, from microseconds to tens of milliseconds, worst exactly under load. Fusion needs the time of the physical event, so stamps should be taken in hardware and corrected to the event (for example mid-exposure).

Which timestamp should a camera detection carry?Intermediate

The middle of the exposure of the rows the object occupies, in the vehicle time base: the hardware frame-start stamp, plus row index times line time for the rolling shutter, minus half the exposure time of that frame read from the embedded data, plus a fixed sensor offset. Not the arrival time or the time the detector finished.

How do you handle out-of-sequence measurements?Intermediate

Either buffer and process in measurement order with a fixed lag at least as large as the worst sensor latency, or process on arrival and, when an older measurement arrives, restore the stored state before it, apply it and replay the newer measurements; or use an OOSM retrodiction update. Measurements older than the history are discarded and counted. The choice trades latency against memory and CPU.

What does the EKF do differently from the linear Kalman filter?Basic

It propagates the mean through the true nonlinear motion and measurement functions and the covariance through their Jacobians evaluated at the current estimate; the rest is the Kalman filter. It is the default for radar (range, azimuth, range rate) and camera (bearing) models. It can become inconsistent when the uncertainty is large relative to the curvature of the functions.

EKF or UKF?Intermediate

The UKF propagates \(2n+1\) sigma points through the true functions, captures the mean to second order and needs no Jacobians, at similar cost for small states. Prefer it for stronger nonlinearities (large angular uncertainty, turning motion models with uncertain yaw rate) or when Jacobians are error-prone. Both represent the belief as one Gaussian, so neither handles multimodality; watch angle averaging in the UKF.

When would you use a particle filter?Intermediate

When the belief is multimodal or constrained, for example map-based localisation (which lane), or track-before-detect of weak targets. Particles represent arbitrary distributions, but the number needed grows roughly exponentially with state dimension, so for tracking many objects with near-Gaussian beliefs Kalman-type filters are preferred; Rao–Blackwellised filters combine both.

What is gating, and why use the Mahalanobis distance?Basic

Gating excludes detection–track pairs that are implausible before assignment. The squared Mahalanobis distance \(y^{\top}S^{-1}y\) measures the innovation in units of its expected covariance in every direction and follows a chi-square distribution, so a threshold such as 9.21 for 2-D gives a known 99% probability of keeping the true detection. Euclidean distance ignores that a camera is precise across and vague along the line of sight.

Compare GNN, JPDA and MHT.Intermediate

GNN picks the one-to-one assignment with minimum total cost (Hungarian algorithm): fast, deterministic, commits and can swap identities. JPDA computes marginal association probabilities over all feasible joint events and updates each track with a probability-weighted mix, inflating the covariance: robust in clutter, costlier, tracks can coalesce. MHT keeps several hypotheses over frames and prunes later: most powerful for crossing and dense targets, most expensive.

How are tracks created, confirmed and deleted?Basic

Unassigned detections start tentative tracks, confirmed after M of N hits or when an existence probability exceeds a threshold; unassigned tracks coast on their prediction with growing covariance and are deleted after misses or when existence falls. In multi-sensor systems this is sensor-aware: for example braking-relevant pedestrian tracks require camera confirmation, and misses only count when the sensor could have seen the object.

What is an IMM filter?Intermediate

An interacting multiple model filter runs several motion models in parallel (for example walking and standing, or straight and turning), mixes their estimates according to model transition probabilities at each step, and weights them by their likelihoods. The track follows manoeuvres without the noise of one model with huge process noise.

How do you tune Q and R and check that a filter is consistent?Intermediate

Derive R from recorded sensor errors against a reference, per range, angle and condition; set Q from the targets' realistic accelerations. Check NIS per sensor (should average the measurement dimension, about 99% inside the chi-square gate) on all drives and NEES (should average the state dimension) on reference drives. Persistent high values mean overconfidence: wrong R, unmodelled bias, calibration or timestamp errors.

What is covariance intersection and when do you need it?Advanced

A fusion rule \(P^{-1} = \omega P_a^{-1} + (1-\omega)P_b^{-1}\) that is consistent for any unknown correlation between the inputs. It is needed in track-to-track fusion, where common process noise or shared measurements correlate the tracks and naive independent fusion would be overconfident.

How is radar alignment maintained over the vehicle's life?Intermediate

Static alignment at end of line and after repairs with a reflector or target relative to the thrust axis, then online: the range rates of stationary detections follow \(-v\cos(\theta_m + \psi)\), so a least-squares fit over many detections gives the yaw misalignment. Corrections are limited and monitored; large deviations raise a diagnostic and degrade the function.

How should the fusion react to a degraded sensor?Basic

Detect it (self-diagnosis such as blockage, communication and timestamp checks, cross-sensor consistency), then use it with inflated noise and reduced detection probability, stop it from creating or deleting tracks, and report reduced capability in the output so functions switch to a designed degraded mode. Exclude it entirely if it is faulty rather than degraded.

Q.2Why and what if

Why can a radar not measure how fast a pedestrian crosses the road?Basic

Doppler measures only the velocity component along the line of sight. A crossing pedestrian moves perpendicular to it, so the range-rate Jacobian with respect to crossing speed is \(\sin\theta\), a few percent at small azimuths. The crossing speed must come from successive bearings, which the camera measures far better.

What if the camera timestamps are 30 ms late? When would you notice?Intermediate

Not when standing still. Driving straight, camera positions lag along the driving direction by speed × 30 ms, mostly hidden in the camera's large range uncertainty. In turns, bearings are off by yaw rate × 30 ms, which at 10°/s and 34 m is about 0.18 m sideways, more than twice the camera's lateral sigma: lateral residuals that grow with yaw rate are the signature, and the slope gives the offset.

Why is a 1° yaw error worse than a 2 cm translation error?Basic

A translation error shifts every measurement by 2 cm; a rotation error shifts it by distance × angle, 1.75 cm per metre, so 0.5 m at 30 m and 1.4 m at 80 m, enough to move an object into the next lane.

What happens if measurements are processed in arrival order?Intermediate

A measurement older than the track's current time is applied as if it were current, with the wrong ego pose and the wrong object position. Errors scale with the arrival inversion and with motion, and they appear under load, when latencies vary, so they pass lab tests and show up in the car.

Why update with camera detections in bearing and range rather than converting them to x, y first?Intermediate

In its own space the camera's noise is simple: small in bearing, large and range-dependent in range, uncorrelated. In Cartesian coordinates it becomes a rotated, correlated, state-dependent ellipse, and circular gates or a fixed R would misrepresent it. Using \(h\) and its Jacobian lets the filter apply the bearing precisely and the range loosely.

What if the camera's NIS averages 8 although its measurement is 2-dimensional?Intermediate

The filter is overconfident for that sensor: its innovations are about twice as large as claimed. Causes: R too small, a calibration or timestamp bias, wrong exposure correction, association errors or too small Q. Split NIS by range, angle, yaw rate and condition to find which; inflating R blindly hides the cause.

Why is an overconfident filter more dangerous than a noisy one?Basic

Consumers trust the covariance: a function may brake or not based on a lateral position claimed to ±0.1 m that is 0.5 m off. Overconfidence also shrinks gates until true detections are rejected and the track is lost. A noisy but honest filter only makes the function wait or be conservative.

What if a dirty camera's missing detections are treated like normal misses?Intermediate

Each miss lowers the existence probability of tracks the camera should see, so radar-supported pedestrian tracks are deleted or never confirmed: a degraded sensor actively removes objects. The existence update must use the camera's current detection probability, near zero when blocked.

Why can you not calibrate a sensor's height by driving on a flat road?Advanced

Hand-eye calibration \(AX = XB\) needs rotations about non-parallel axes. On a flat road the car rotates only about the vertical axis, so translation along that axis does not change any measured motion and is unobservable; parts of the rotation are weakly constrained too. Height comes from the ground plane, a target or the mechanical design.

What if two trackers' outputs are fused as if independent?Intermediate

Their errors are correlated by common process noise and possibly shared measurements, so the fused covariance is too small. The filter becomes overconfident, gates shrink and a feedback loop can count the same information repeatedly. Use the cross-covariance (Bar-Shalom–Campo) or covariance intersection, or fuse detections centrally.

Why does Doppler alignment converge slowly for a narrow-beam long-range radar?Advanced

The information about the yaw error is proportional to \(\sin\theta\), and near boresight the cosine is flat, so detections close to straight ahead barely constrain it. In the simulation 400 detections over ±60° gave ±0.04°, while the 43 within ±5° gave ±0.6°. A narrow field of view needs many more detections, and turns or wide-angle targets.

What if the gPTP grandmaster restarts and steps its time?Advanced

All synchronised sensors jump together, so cross-sensor consistency checks see nothing, but the step corrupts the order and the ego-pose lookup of every measurement around it, and a backward step makes new data look old. The time base is a common cause: monitor its status, slew instead of stepping, define holdover, and check stamps against a local monotonic clock.

Why can an EKF track well but fail at initialisation?Intermediate

An established track's uncertainty is small, so the measurement function is nearly linear over it. A track initialised from one detection with large angular or range uncertainty spans a region where the polar-to-Cartesian mapping bends; the linearised mean is biased and the covariance too small. Initialise conservatively, from two or three detections, or with a UKF.

Why can the radar not notice a 0.5° calibration error of the camera at 34 m?Intermediate

The error moves the camera detection 0.3 m sideways, but the radar's own azimuth uncertainty there is 0.6 m, so the disagreement is well within the gate. Across the line of sight the fused track is built almost entirely from the camera and inherits the error while claiming under 0.1 m uncertainty. Only a sensor of similar lateral precision or long-term residual statistics reveals it.

What if the exposure-time correction of camera timestamps is a fixed daytime value?Intermediate

At night exposures grow to 10–30 ms, so mid-exposure moves earlier by several milliseconds relative to the frame start, and the fixed correction leaves a timestamp error that appears only at night and in motion. Use each frame's exposure from the embedded data.

Multiple hypothesis tracking: keeps alternative association histories and decides later.
NEES
Normalised estimation error squared; needs ground truth; averages the state dimension for a consistent filter.
Negative information
Evidence from the absence of a detection; valid only where the sensor could have detected the object.
NIS
Normalised innovation squared \(y^{\top}S^{-1}y\); averages the measurement dimension for a consistent filter.
Out-of-sequence measurement
A measurement that arrives after newer ones have already been processed.
Particle filter
Bayes filter representing the belief by weighted samples, with resampling.
PTP
Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588: hardware-timestamped message exchanges that give clock offset and path delay.
Thrust axis
The direction the rear axle drives the car; the reference for ADAS sensor alignment.
Transform tree
The graph of coordinate frames (map, odometry, vehicle, sensors) and the transforms between them.
UKF
Unscented Kalman filter: propagates 2n+1 sigma points through the nonlinear models instead of linearising.
