Automotive sensor technologies

What each sensor on a car actually measures and why: cameras in brief (mono, stereo, fisheye, thermal), FMCW radar from chirp to point cloud and 4D imaging radar, lidar from time of flight to FMCW, ultrasonic parking sensors, GNSS, IMU and wheel odometry; what datasheet numbers mean, how weather, dirt, temperature and other cars degrade each one, and how an L2+ sensor set is chosen, placed, made redundant and paid for.

1The big picture: why a car needs several kinds of eyes

A driver uses two eyes, two ears, a sense of balance and a feel for the steering wheel, and fuses them without thinking. An automated car has to build the same picture from sensors that each exploit one physical effect: reflected light, emitted heat, radio echoes, laser echoes, sound echoes, satellite signals, inertia and turning wheels. Each effect is excellent at some questions and useless at others, and the whole art of choosing sensors is to cover every question the driving functions ask with at least one sensor that is good at it, and the important questions with two that fail differently.

1.1The questions a car must answer

Every driving function, from a parking beeper to a highway pilot, needs answers to a handful of questions. What is around me? A car, a pedestrian, a cyclist, a traffic cone, a lane marking, a traffic light and its colour. Where exactly is it? Its distance, its direction and its size, to a precision that depends on the manoeuvre: centimetres when parking, half a lane at 150 m on the motorway. How is it moving? Its speed towards me and across my path, because a car stopped in my lane at 200 m is a different problem from one driving away at the same speed as me. Where am I, and how am I moving? My own speed, yaw rate and position on the road and on the map, without which nothing measured by the other sensors can be put into a world frame. And finally: can I trust what I just measured? A sensor that is blinded by low sun, covered in slush or confused by a guardrail must know it, or at least another sensor must notice.

No single physical principle answers all of these. Light reflected from the scene (a camera) carries texture, colour and shape, so it is the only practical way to read signs, see lane markings and classify objects, but it does not carry distance and it needs illumination. A radio echo (radar) carries distance and radial speed with great precision and passes through fog and darkness, but its wavelength of a few millimetres makes it blurry in angle and blind to colour and paint. A laser echo (lidar) carries distance and fine angle, giving a 3D picture, but suffers in fog and snow and costs more. Sound echoes (ultrasonic) are cheap and see glass and thin poles at parking distances, but only to a few metres. Satellite signals give absolute position to metres, inertial sensors give rotation and acceleration at a kilohertz, wheel sensors give distance travelled; none of these three says anything about the world around the car.

Why it matters

Most arguments about sensor sets, "cameras only" versus "camera plus radar" versus "add a lidar", become simple once you ask which question each sensor answers well, in which conditions, and what happens when it fails. A system engineer who can say "the radar gives us closing speed and works in fog, the camera gives us class and lateral position, the two fail for different reasons" has already justified most of a typical L2+ architecture.

1.2Active and passive, and why the wavelength explains so much

A passive sensor only listens: a camera collects light that the sun, a street lamp or the car's own headlights scattered off the scene, and a thermal camera collects the infrared light that every warm object emits by itself. An active sensor sends its own signal and listens for the echo: radar sends radio waves, lidar laser pulses, ultrasonic sensors sound. Active sensors measure distance directly from the time the echo takes and work in the dark, because they bring their own illumination. Their price is that they emit: they can interfere with each other, they must respect eye-safety and radio regulations, and their range is limited by how much energy comes back, which for a point-like target falls with the fourth power of distance for radar and roughly with the square for lidar on large surfaces.

Much of a sensor's behaviour follows from one number: its wavelength, compared with the size of the things it must see through or see. Figure 1.1 puts all automotive sensors on one axis.

When particles are much larger than the wavelength, as fog droplets (1–20 µm) are for visible and near-infrared light, they scatter it strongly in all directions: the light is attenuated, and for active sensors part of it comes straight back as false echoes close to the sensor. When particles are much smaller than the wavelength, as fog droplets are for a 3.9 mm radar wave, scattering is negligible (it falls with the fourth power of the size-to-wavelength ratio), which is why radar sees through fog. Rain drops of a few millimetres are comparable to the radar wavelength, so heavy rain does attenuate a 77 GHz radar measurably: by about 9 dB per kilometre (one way) at 25 mm/h and over 20 dB/km in a 100 mm/h cloudburst (ITU-R P.838). Over the 300 m round trip to a target at 150 m that cloudburst costs about 7 dB, which by the radar equation (Section 3.7) shortens the maximum range by about a third: noticeable for a long-range radar, irrelevant for a corner radar. The water film on the radar's cover (the radome) usually matters more than the rain in the air (Section 8.2).

The wavelength also sets the sharpness of a sensor's view. The finest angle an aperture of size \(D\) can resolve is about \(\lambda/D\). A camera lens with a 5 mm aperture at 0.5 µm could in principle resolve 0.1 milliradians (0.006°), far finer than its pixels. A radar antenna array 8 cm wide at 3.9 mm resolves about \(0.0039/0.08 \approx 0.05\) rad, three degrees, and that is a large radar. This single ratio is why radars are blurry in angle, why imaging radars have hundreds of virtual antenna channels, and why nobody builds a centimetre-wavelength camera.

Interesting detail

Ultrasonic sensors at 48 kHz have a wavelength of 7 mm in air, almost the same as a 77 GHz radar, so their beam shapes from a small membrane are similarly wide. But sound travels at 343 m/s instead of 300 000 km/s: an echo from 1 m takes 5.8 ms instead of 6.7 ns. Everything that is hard about radar electronics (measuring nanoseconds, generating gigahertz chirps) is trivial for ultrasound, which is one reason an ultrasonic sensor costs a few euros. Everything that is easy for radar (thousands of measurements per second, no dependence on air temperature and wind) is hard for ultrasound.

2Cameras: a short recap, and the variants that matter

The camera is the most important sensor of a modern car and the one this book covers most thoroughly elsewhere. This chapter only recaps what makes it a sensor, then looks at the variants that change what it measures: monocular versus stereo, narrow versus fisheye, and visible versus thermal.

2.1The camera as a measuring device

A camera measures, for every pixel, how much light arrived from one direction during the exposure. Its geometry is excellent: with a calibrated lens, a pixel position is a direction in space accurate to a fraction of a pixel, which for an 8-megapixel front camera with a 120° field of view is about 0.03° per pixel. Its content is rich: colour, texture and shape let a neural network tell a pedestrian from a pole, read a speed-limit sign and find lane markings that no other sensor can see at all. Its raw data is a stream of images, typically 30 frames per second, each frame millions of pixels of 12–24 bits after the sensor's HDR combination, about 3–6 Gbit/s for an 8 MP camera before the ISP (Camera pipeline 7).

Its weaknesses follow from being passive and two-dimensional. It needs light, and the scene's brightness spans more than 120 dB between a dark tunnel exit and the low sun, which forces HDR sensors and tone mapping (Camera pipeline 5). It looks through the windscreen and needs the wiper. And a single camera does not measure distance: everything at the same direction lands on the same pixel, whether it is 10 m or 100 m away.

2.2Monocular distance, and why it degrades with the square of range

A single camera still estimates distance, from cues: where the object touches the road (with a known camera height and a flat road), how large it appears (with a known object size), and, in a neural network, everything else the training data taught it. The ground-contact cue shows the fundamental problem. With the camera at height \(h\), a point on the road at distance \(d\) appears at an angle \(\alpha \approx h/d\) below the horizon. A small error \(\delta\alpha\), from pitch motion, calibration or a sloped road, changes the estimated distance by

\[ \delta d \approx \frac{d^2}{h}\,\delta\alpha . \tag{2.1}\]

The error grows with the square of distance. For \(h = 1.3\) m and a pitch error of 0.1°, it is 0.13 m at 10 m, 3.4 m at 50 m and 13 m at 100 m. Learned monocular depth does better than this geometric worst case on average, but its relative error of 5–10% grows in the same way, and it can be confidently wrong on objects it has not seen. This is why camera-only systems estimate distance well enough to stay in lane and follow a car at moderate speed, but rely on temporal filtering (and, increasingly, on radar) for time-to-collision at long range. The quantitative comparison with other sensors is in Section 7.4.

2.3Stereo cameras: distance from two viewpoints

Two cameras a baseline \(B\) apart see a point at slightly different image positions; the difference, the disparity \(d_p\) in pixels, gives depth directly by similar triangles:

\[ Z = \frac{f\,B}{d_p}, \qquad \delta Z = \frac{Z^2}{f\,B}\,\delta d_p . \tag{2.2}\]

With a focal length \(f = 1600\) px and a baseline of 25 cm, a point at 20 m has a disparity of 20 px. If the matcher finds disparities to a quarter of a pixel, depth is good to 0.25 m at 20 m, 1.6 m at 50 m and 6.3 m at 100 m. Stereo is therefore excellent in the near and middle field, where it gives a dense depth map without needing to know what the object is: a lost tyre, a fallen box and a speed bump all produce disparity, even if no network was trained on them. That generic obstacle detection is stereo's main selling point. Its weaknesses are the same square law as mono beyond 50–80 m, the need for very stable relative calibration (a twist of 0.01° between the two cameras is a disparity error of about 0.3 px at this focal length, more than the matching noise), twice the camera hardware, and a large mechanical package behind the windscreen. Matching and rectification are covered in Classic CV 14.

Numbers

Automotive stereo cameras use baselines of roughly 12–35 cm. Depth error grows as \(Z^2\): doubling the baseline or the focal length halves the error at every distance, while better sub-pixel matching helps by the same factor. A rule of thumb that is easy to recompute in a meeting: \(\delta Z \approx Z^2\,\delta d_p / (fB)\), so with \(fB = 400\) px·m and \(\delta d_p = 0.25\) px, the error in metres is \(Z^2/1600\).

2.4Fisheye and surround-view cameras

Four cameras with fisheye lenses of 190° or more, in the front grille, the rear and the two side mirrors, see the entire surroundings of the car at close range. They exist first for the driver (the top view and the 3D bowl view on the display) and increasingly for perception: parking-slot detection, low obstacles and kerbs, cross traffic at low speed. Their resolution is spread over a huge field of view, typically 1–3 MP over 190°, so they see a car well at 10–20 m but are of little use beyond 30–40 m. Their geometry needs fisheye models instead of the pinhole model, and because they are mounted low and on moving parts such as mirrors and tailgates, their calibration is a continuing concern. All of this is covered in Camera geometry 4 to 8.

2.5Thermal cameras: seeing emitted heat

A thermal camera solves the camera's darkness problem by not needing illumination at all. Every object emits electromagnetic radiation according to its temperature; by Wien's law the emission peaks at \(\lambda_{\max} \approx 2898\,\mu\text{m·K} / T\), which for human skin at 306 K is 9.5 µm and for a road at 290 K is 10 µm. A camera sensitive to the long-wave infrared (LWIR) band of 8–14 µm therefore sees people, animals and running engines by their own glow, at night exactly as by day, and is not dazzled by oncoming headlights, which emit little in that band.

The detector is usually an uncooled microbolometer: an array of tiny suspended membranes of vanadium oxide or amorphous silicon, each of which warms up by a fraction of a degree when infrared light falls on it and changes its electrical resistance accordingly. Typical automotive resolutions are 320 × 240 or 640 × 512 pixels with a 12 µm pitch, and the sensitivity, the NETD, is below 50 mK: the camera tells apart surfaces that differ by a twentieth of a degree. The optics cannot be glass, which is opaque in the LWIR; lenses are made of germanium or chalcogenide glass, and the camera must look through a window of the same materials. So a thermal camera sits in the grille or behind the bumper, with its own heating and cleaning, not behind the windscreen with the other cameras.

A thermal camera is the best sensor for one specific job: detecting pedestrians and animals at night, at 100–200 m with a narrow lens, through headlight glare. It struggles when the background has the same temperature as the object (a warm summer evening, a person in a thick winter coat whose outside is near ambient), it has no colour and cannot read signs or traffic lights, and its resolution is low. Microbolometers drift and need periodic non-uniformity correction; older designs close a small shutter for a fraction of a second every few minutes to recalibrate, freezing the image, which an automotive design must avoid or hide. And they are still several times more expensive than a visible camera. Thermal cameras have been offered for night-vision displays in premium cars for two decades and are now discussed for night-time pedestrian AEB, which regulations and Euro NCAP test in the dark (Standards 7.2).

Interesting detail

Near-infrared (NIR) cameras at 850 or 940 nm are a different thing altogether: ordinary silicon sensors without the infrared-cut filter, used with their own infrared LEDs, as in driver-monitoring cameras inside the cabin. They see reflected infrared light, not heat, and are blind in the dark without their illuminator, exactly like a visible camera.

2.6What cameras are good and bad at

Cameras are unmatched at classification and semantics: object class, lane markings, signs, traffic lights, brake lights, free space, the gesture of a police officer. They are excellent at angle: the lateral position of an object and the bearing rate that reveals a crossing pedestrian. They are cheap, small and have very high resolution. They are weak at distance and speed along the line of sight, especially beyond 50 m; they need light and suffer from glare, low sun and the transition into tunnels; they need a clean window; and they are fooled by pictures, reflections and shadows that radar and lidar would not see as objects. Every one of these weaknesses is a reason for a second sensor.

Talk track

"A camera measures direction and appearance, not distance. That makes it the only sensor that can read the road, signs and lights and classify objects, and gives it excellent lateral accuracy, but its range estimate degrades with the square of distance. Stereo measures depth directly and finds unknown obstacles, but has the same square law at long range. Fisheye cameras cover the near field for parking; thermal cameras see pedestrians at night by their own heat but cannot sit behind glass."

3Radar: range and speed from radio echoes

Radar is the workhorse of driver assistance: almost every new car has at least one, most L2+ cars have five. It measures distance and closing speed directly and precisely, in rain, fog, darkness and low sun, at a cost of a few tens of euros. This chapter follows one frame of a modern FMCW radar from the transmitted chirp to the list of points it reports, and shows why its view of the world is precise in range and speed but blurry in angle.

3.1Why radar, and what is inside one

The camera's two big weaknesses, distance at long range and dependence on light, are exactly radar's strengths. A radio echo comes back after a time that is exactly proportional to distance, and its frequency is shifted by exactly the target's radial speed; neither depends on illumination, colour or contrast, and millimetre waves pass through fog, dust and darkness almost unaffected. That is why the first adaptive cruise controls in the late 1990s used radar, and why radar is still the sensor of choice for following distance, time to collision and lane-change warnings.

A modern automotive radar is a small box behind the bumper or the badge, with four parts. The MMIC (monolithic microwave integrated circuit), today usually a single RF-CMOS chip, generates the transmit signal around 77 GHz, drives a few transmit antennas and receives on a few receive antennas, mixing each received signal down to a low-frequency signal that its ADCs digitise. The antennas are arrays of small patches etched on a special high-frequency circuit board, or 3D waveguide antennas; their layout determines the field of view and the angular resolution. A radar processor with hardware FFT accelerators and a DSP turns the samples into detections. And the radome, the bumper fascia or badge in front of it, must be nearly transparent at 77 GHz, which constrains its material, thickness and paint (metallic paints and chrome are a problem). The car's radars operate in the 76–81 GHz band: 76–77 GHz for long-range radars and the wider 77–81 GHz for short-range radars that need fine range resolution. Older 24 GHz radars are being phased out because the wide band they used is no longer allowed and the narrow one is too narrow.

3.2FMCW: measuring distance as a frequency

Measuring the time of flight of a pulse directly would require timing nanoseconds and transmitting short, powerful pulses. Almost all automotive radars instead use frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) signals, which turn time into frequency. The radar transmits a chirp: a signal whose frequency rises linearly, for example from 77 to 78 GHz in 25 µs, a slope \(S = B/T_c\) of 40 MHz per microsecond. The echo from a target at range \(R\) is a copy of the chirp delayed by \(\tau = 2R/c\). The receiver multiplies the echo with the signal it is transmitting at that moment (it mixes them), and the product contains the difference of their frequencies. Because both are ramps with the same slope, that difference is constant during the chirp: the beat frequency

\[ f_b = S\,\tau = \frac{2 R S}{c} . \tag{3.1}\]

A target at 50 m produces a delay of 334 ns and, with a slope of 40 MHz/µs, a beat frequency of 13.3 MHz. The radar has converted a 334-nanosecond delay into a 13-megahertz tone, which an ordinary ADC can sample at 20 MHz and an FFT can measure. Several targets produce several tones at once, and a Fourier transform of the samples of one chirp separates them: the range FFT, whose bins are range bins. Figure 3.1 lets you change the waveform and see what each parameter controls.

Two things in the figure are worth noticing. The echo delay is tiny compared with the chirp: 334 ns against 25 µs. The radar does not measure the delay by looking at the time axis at all; it measures the frequency of a tone that lasts the whole chirp, and the longer the tone, the more precisely its frequency can be found. And the number of beat cycles in one chirp is \(f_b T_c = 2RB/c\), which is exactly the range divided by the range resolution of the next section: a target "one resolution cell" further away produces exactly one more cycle per chirp.

3.3Range: resolution comes from bandwidth, maximum range from slope

Two targets can be told apart in range when their beat frequencies differ by at least one FFT bin, which is \(1/T_c\) for a chirp of duration \(T_c\). Translating that frequency difference into range with (3.1) gives the most important formula of FMCW radar:

\[ \Delta R = \frac{c}{2B} . \tag{3.2}\]

Range resolution depends only on the swept bandwidth \(B\), not on the chirp duration or the carrier frequency. A long-range radar sweeping 300 MHz resolves 50 cm; a 1 GHz sweep resolves 15 cm; a short-range radar using the full 4 GHz of the 77–81 GHz band resolves 3.75 cm. Resolution is not accuracy: a single isolated target can be located to a small fraction of a bin by interpolating the peak, so radars typically report range to about ten centimetres even with 50 cm cells. Resolution matters when two targets are close: a pedestrian standing next to a parked car, a motorcycle beside a truck. In the explorer, set the second target 0.12 m behind the first: with 1 GHz (15 cm cells) the two peaks merge into one; with 2 GHz (7.5 cm) they separate. Then go back to 1 GHz with 0.2 m: separated with a rectangular window, merged again with a Hann window, because windows, used in practice to suppress the sidelobes of strong targets that would hide weak ones, widen the peak by 1.5–2 times.

The maximum range is set by how high a beat frequency the receiver can digitise. Its IF (intermediate frequency) filter and ADC pass beat frequencies up to some \(f_{IF,\max}\), and by (3.1):

\[ R_{\max} = \frac{f_{IF,\max}\, c}{2S} . \tag{3.3}\]

With a 20 MHz IF and a slope of 40 MHz/µs, that is 75 m: a good short- and medium-range mode. A long-range mode uses a gentler slope, for example 300 MHz in 30 µs (10 MHz/µs), which puts a target at 200 m at 13.3 MHz and extends the maximum to 300 m, at the price of 50 cm resolution. This is the basic trade-off of the waveform: for a fixed IF bandwidth and chirp time, more bandwidth means finer resolution but shorter maximum range. Real radars therefore interleave several waveforms (a long-range narrow beam and a short-range wide beam, as in Figure 1.2) or switch between modes from frame to frame. Whether the radar actually detects a target at that maximum range is a different question, answered by the radar equation in Section 3.7.

3.4Velocity: a phase that turns from chirp to chirp

A moving target shifts the echo's frequency by the Doppler effect, \(f_D = 2v/\lambda\): 514 Hz per metre per second at 77 GHz. That is tiny next to the beat frequency (15 kHz at 30 m/s against 13 MHz at 50 m), far too small to measure within one 25 µs chirp, whose frequency resolution is 40 kHz. FMCW radars therefore measure velocity across chirps. Between two consecutive chirps, \(T_r\) apart, a target with radial velocity \(v\) moves by \(v T_r\), and the round trip changes by \(2 v T_r\), which changes the phase of its beat signal by

\[ \Delta\varphi = \frac{4\pi\, v\, T_r}{\lambda} . \tag{3.4}\]

At 77 GHz with \(T_r = 30\) µs, a target closing at 1 m/s turns its phase by 5.5° per chirp, while its range changes by only 30 µm, far below a range bin. A frame of \(N\) chirps therefore gives, for each range bin, a sequence of \(N\) complex values whose phase rotates at a constant rate, and a second FFT across the chirps, the Doppler FFT, turns that rotation into a velocity bin. The same Fourier reasoning as for range gives the two limits:

\[ v_{\max} = \frac{\lambda}{4T_r}, \qquad \Delta v = \frac{\lambda}{2 N T_r} . \tag{3.5}\]

The phase step can only be measured between −180° and +180°, so velocities beyond \(\pm v_{\max}\) fold back, exactly like the wagon wheels that turn backwards in films. With \(T_r = 30\) µs, \(v_{\max}\) is ±32 m/s (±117 km/h) and with 128 chirps the velocity resolution is 0.51 m/s, from a frame lasting 3.84 ms. Shorter chirps raise \(v_{\max}\); more chirps, or a longer frame, improve \(\Delta v\). On a motorway, oncoming traffic closes at 70 m/s and more, so a single waveform is ambiguous; radars resolve it by alternating two chirp periods and using the Chinese remainder theorem, by tracking, or by checking which of the candidate velocities fits the measured range change over several frames. In the explorer, set the velocity to −50 m/s and watch the target reappear at +14.9 m/s.

Interesting detail

The Doppler measurement is not just a velocity sensor; it is also a separator. Two objects at the same range and almost the same angle, a pedestrian next to a parked car, end up in different cells of the range–Doppler map if their radial speeds differ by more than half a metre per second. Walking legs and turning wheels add a spread of velocities around the body's speed, the micro-Doppler signature, which classifiers use to tell pedestrians and cyclists from static clutter.

3.5Angle: phase across antennas, and the virtual array

Range and velocity come from one receive antenna; direction needs several. A plane wave arriving from angle \(\theta\) reaches neighbouring receive antennas a distance \(d\) apart with a path difference \(d\sin\theta\), so their signals differ in phase by

\[ \Delta\psi = \frac{2\pi\, d\,\sin\theta}{\lambda} . \tag{3.6}\]

With antennas half a wavelength apart (1.95 mm at 77 GHz), the phase difference runs from −180° to +180° as \(\theta\) runs from −90° to +90°, so the whole half-space is unambiguous. A third FFT, across the antennas, the angle FFT, turns the phase progression into an angle spectrum. Its sharpness depends on the array length, as for any aperture: with \(N\) antennas at \(\lambda/2\), two targets can be separated when they are about

\[ \Delta\theta \approx \frac{2}{N\cos\theta}\ \text{rad} \tag{3.7}\]

apart: 9.5° for 12 antennas at boresight, twice as much at 60° off boresight, where the array is seen foreshortened. To get many antennas from few chips, radars use MIMO: with 3 transmitters and 4 receivers, placed so that every transmitter–receiver pair has a different total path, the radar synthesises a virtual array of 3 × 4 = 12 elements. The transmitters must be told apart at the receivers, most simply by taking turns chirp by chirp (time-division MIMO), which costs velocity range (the effective chirp period for one transmitter is three times longer) and needs a correction for the target's motion between turns; other schemes encode the transmitters in phase or frequency.

The figure also shows the difference between resolution and accuracy that confuses many datasheet discussions. A conventional 12-channel radar cannot separate two cars 6° apart, but if there is only one target, it finds the centre of its peak to a few tenths of a degree, because with a good signal-to-noise ratio the peak's position is far better defined than its width: a classic rule of thumb is beamwidth divided by \(1.6\sqrt{2\,\text{SNR}}\). So "azimuth accuracy 0.3°" and "angular resolution 8°" in the same datasheet are not a contradiction. The trouble starts when two objects share a range and velocity cell, such as a car and the guardrail beside it, or the two corners of a truck: the radar reports one point in between, at an angle that belongs to neither.

Elevation works the same way with antennas stacked vertically. Conventional radars have none or only a coarse elevation measurement, which is the root of their most notorious weakness (Section 3.9). Radars with a full two-dimensional virtual array measure elevation too, and are called 4D radars (Section 3.8).

3.6From samples to a point cloud: the processing chain

One frame of the example waveform produces a radar cube of 512 samples per chirp × 128 chirps × 12 virtual channels, about 786 000 complex samples every few milliseconds. The processing that turns this cube into a few hundred points is the same in nearly all automotive radars, and is mostly done by fixed-function FFT hardware on the radar chip.

Figure 3.4 shows the range–Doppler map of one simulated frame (experiments/fmcw_sim.py): the ego car drives at 15 m/s past a row of guardrail posts on the right, a road sign on the left, a pedestrian on the kerb at 25 m, a car ahead at 42 m that is 4 m/s slower, and an oncoming car at 60 m. The frame is synthesised as the beat signal of point scatterers with the chapter's example waveform and processed exactly as in the stepper.

The simulation reports 17 detections and no false alarms. The pedestrian at 25.2 m is detected at −6.7° with 26 dB SNR and is separated from the guardrail post 1.2 m further away by range, not by velocity: a pedestrian standing on the kerb, or walking across the road, has almost the same radial speed as the stationary world around them, which is why crossing pedestrians are a hard case for radar. The car ahead is one point at 0° instead of two corners at ±1.1°, because 12 channels resolve only about 10°. The post at 26.4 m appears at −9.9° against a true −9.8°, and velocities are quantised to the 0.51 m/s bins (−15.2 against a true −14.9) because this simple processing does not interpolate between bins. The four posts beyond 60 m have the same radar cross-section as the near ones but are not detected: their echoes are 12 dB weaker per doubling of range, which leads to the radar equation.

3.7What the data looks like: RCS, the radar equation and the interface

How strongly a target reflects is described by its radar cross-section (RCS), the area of an ideal isotropic reflector that would return the same power, usually given in dBsm (decibels relative to one square metre). Typical values are about 10 m² (10 dBsm) for the rear of a car, a few m² for a motorcycle, 0.1–1 m² (−10 to 0 dBsm) for a pedestrian, and hundreds to thousands of m² for a truck or a corner reflector; they fluctuate by 10 dB and more with aspect angle, as different surfaces line up with the radar. The received power follows the radar equation:

\[ P_r = \frac{P_t\, G^2\, \lambda^2\, \sigma}{(4\pi)^3\, R^4} . \tag{3.8}\]

The fourth power of range is the essential fact: doubling the range costs 12 dB, and the maximum detection range grows only with the fourth root of everything else. A radar that detects a 10 m² car at 200 m detects a 0.5 m² pedestrian at about \(200 \times (0.05)^{1/4} \approx 95\) m, and four times the transmit power buys only 41% more range. Integration across chirps and channels helps (128 chirps × 12 channels add up to about 32 dB of gain against noise), which is why the frame in Figure 3.4 sees a pedestrian at 25 m with plenty of margin.

What the radar sends to the rest of the car has changed over time, and engineers should know the three levels. Object lists: the radar clusters and tracks its own detections and reports maybe 32–64 objects with position, velocity, size and class over CAN or CAN FD; that is how most radars of the last twenty years work, and it hides everything the radar's own tracker threw away. Detection lists: the radar reports its raw point cloud, a few hundred to a few thousand points per cycle, over automotive Ethernet, and the central computer clusters, tracks and fuses (Calibration and fusion 7.1). Earlier data: some architectures move even the FFTs to the central computer and stream the range–Doppler data or the raw cube from simple "satellite" radar heads, so that the central processor can fuse at the lowest level; this needs gigabits per radar.

Numbers

Typical conventional radars today: front radar up to 200–300 m in a ±9–10° long-range beam and 70–100 m at ±60°, range accuracy around 0.1 m, velocity accuracy around 0.1 m/s, azimuth accuracy 0.1–0.5° near boresight with a resolution of several degrees; corner radars 80–100 m over ±75°. Cycle times are 50–70 ms. A 77 GHz Doppler of 514 Hz per m/s, a 15 cm range bin per GHz of bandwidth (precisely: 15 cm at 1 GHz) and 12 dB per doubling of range are the three numbers to remember.

3.84D imaging radar

A 4D or imaging radar measures four quantities per point (range, velocity, azimuth and elevation) with angular resolution good enough to start looking like a coarse lidar. The key is the virtual array: cascading four standard MMICs (12 transmitters and 16 receivers) gives 192 virtual channels, and dedicated chips reach several hundred to a few thousand. Spread over two dimensions, that gives about 1° in azimuth and 2° in elevation; with super-resolution processing, finer still. The point cloud grows from a few hundred to several thousand points per frame, and it now has height.

Height is what changes the radar's role. A conventional radar sees a strong stationary reflection 150 m ahead and cannot tell whether it is a stopped car (brake), an overhead gantry (drive under it) or a manhole cover (drive over it). With elevation it can. Fine azimuth resolution separates a motorcycle from the truck next to it and a pedestrian from a parked car, and gives object extent and heading. 4D radars are therefore discussed both as a cheaper alternative to lidar for L2+ and L3 and as a better front radar. The costs are a larger antenna (often 10–15 cm wide), more chips and power, far more data (hundreds of Mbit/s if detections are exported), heavier processing, and point clouds that are still much sparser and noisier than a lidar's, with all the multipath effects of radar.

3.9What radar is bad at, and the pitfalls

Stationary objects. For a moving car, almost everything in the world is stationary: the road, signs, guardrails, bridges, parked cars, and a stopped car at the end of a traffic jam. Without elevation and with coarse angle, a radar cannot reliably tell which of them is in the lane and above the road surface. For many years, radar-based adaptive cruise controls therefore ignored stationary objects that had never been seen moving, which is why user manuals warned that the system might not brake for a stopped vehicle ahead. Camera confirmation and 4D radar address this; it remains a classic question and a classic SOTIF triggering condition (Functional safety 8.3).

Multipath and ghosts. Radio waves reflect off guardrails, tunnel walls, the road surface and other cars. An echo that bounced off a guardrail on its way appears as a "ghost" target behind the guardrail, mirror-imaged; a strong reflector can appear at twice its range through a double bounce between it and the car's own bumper. Multipath is not always harmful: the echo that travels under the car ahead lets a radar see the car two ahead brake, which a camera cannot.

Low RCS and clutter. Pedestrians, children and cyclists return a hundred times less power than cars, often next to a strong reflector, which raises the local CFAR threshold. Soft targets such as cardboard boxes and animals can be almost invisible.

Interference. As more cars carry five radars in the same band, a radar receives other radars' chirps. When a foreign chirp crosses one's own, the mixer produces a short broadband burst that raises the noise floor of the whole range profile, burying weak targets; true ghost targets are rarer, because the foreign chirp would need nearly the same slope. Radars detect and blank the corrupted samples, randomise their chirp timing and parameters, and newer designs use other modulations such as phase-coded (PMCW) or OFDM radar. Mutual interference is one of the scaling concerns for dense traffic.

Mounting and radome. The radar's alignment determines its azimuth accuracy, and a yaw error of 1° is half a lane at 100 m; misalignment after a parking knock is a common field problem, handled by end-of-line and online alignment (Calibration and fusion 3.4). Paint layers, bumper thickness and a film of water, slush or ice on the radome attenuate and distort the beam (Section 8.3), and radars monitor their own blockage.

Pitfall

Reading the "maximum range" on a radar datasheet as the range at which it will detect a pedestrian. That number is quoted for a large target, typically 10 m² or a corner reflector, often in the narrow long-range beam only. By the fourth-root law, the same radar sees a pedestrian at roughly half of it, and at the edge of its field of view, where antenna gain is lower, at a fraction of that.

Talk track

"An FMCW radar sends chirps and mixes the echo with what it is transmitting, so each target becomes a tone whose frequency is proportional to range. An FFT over each chirp gives range, with resolution c over 2B, 15 cm per gigahertz; an FFT across chirps gives velocity from the phase rotation; an FFT across the antennas gives angle. It is precise in range and speed and works in any weather, but its angular resolution is a few degrees, it has trouble with stationary objects without elevation, and it sees ghosts from multipath. 4D imaging radars add elevation and about one-degree resolution with hundreds of virtual channels."

4Lidar: a 3D picture from laser echoes

Lidar measures the distance to hundreds of thousands of points per second with centimetre precision and fine angular resolution, and so gives the car something no other sensor gives directly: the 3D shape of its surroundings, independent of lighting. It is the standard sensor of robotaxis and data-collection fleets and appears in a growing number of L2+ and L3 production cars. This chapter explains how it measures, why its range depends so strongly on what it looks at, the different ways of steering the beam, and what the resulting point cloud is good and bad for.

4.1Why lidar

A camera gives fine angles but no distance; a radar gives distance but coarse angles. A lidar gives both: it fires a narrow laser beam, about 0.05–0.2° wide, in a known direction and times the echo, so every measurement is a 3D point. Because the light is its own and at a wavelength the sensor filters for, it works the same at noon and at midnight. Its point cloud makes geometric questions easy that are hard for cameras: the height and extent of an object, the free space between two parked cars, whether something on the road is flat or sticks up, the exact distance to the car ahead at 150 m. It does not need to know what an object is to know that it is there and how big it is, which is valuable for the long tail of objects no network was trained on. That is why lidar is used as the reference sensor for labelling and validating camera and radar perception, even on cars that do not carry one in production.

4.2Time of flight: timing light to the nanosecond

Most lidars are pulsed time-of-flight sensors. A laser emits a pulse a few nanoseconds long; a fraction of it is scattered back by the target and focused onto a fast photodetector; the time \(t\) between emission and echo gives the range \(R = ct/2\). Light travels 30 cm per nanosecond, so one nanosecond of round-trip time is 15 cm of range, an echo from 100 m arrives after 667 ns and one from 200 m after 1.33 µs. Timing circuits resolve tens of picoseconds and fit the pulse shape, giving range precision of 1–3 cm.

The detector is either an avalanche photodiode (APD), which amplifies the photocurrent internally by a factor of tens to hundreds, or a SPAD (single-photon avalanche diode), biased so far that a single photon triggers a full avalanche. SPADs come in arrays and in SiPMs (many SPADs summed into one pixel). Because a single SPAD "click" can equally be a sunlight photon, SPAD lidars fire many shots per point and build a histogram of click times: the echo is the bin where clicks pile up above the uniform background of sunlight and dark counts.

The wavelength is the main design choice. At 905 nm (and nearby 850–940 nm), cheap silicon lasers and detectors work, but the eye focuses this invisible light onto the retina, so eye-safety (IEC 60825-1 Class 1, safe under all conditions of normal use) strictly limits the pulse energy and therefore the range. At 1550 nm, light is absorbed in the front of the eye before it reaches the retina, and the permitted exposure is orders of magnitude higher, so a 1550 nm lidar can send much more energy and see further; it needs indium-gallium-arsenide detectors and fibre lasers, which cost more, and water absorbs 1550 nm light more strongly, which matters for a wet window. Both wavelengths are invisible and both are used in production lidars.

The firing rate of each laser channel limits the unambiguous range: if the next pulse leaves before the previous echo is back, a far echo may be attributed to the wrong shot. A channel firing at 375 kHz can measure up to \(c/(2\cdot 375\,\text{kHz}) = 400\) m unambiguously; at 1 MHz only 150 m. Designs interleave channels, randomise the firing times or code pulses to push past this limit and to reject other lidars' pulses.

4.3How far a lidar sees: reflectivity and the link budget

A lidar's range specification always comes with a reflectivity, such as "200 m at 10%", and that condition matters more than the number. For a target larger than the laser spot, which a car or a wall is at most ranges, the target scatters the light roughly diffusely (Lambertian) into a hemisphere, and the receiver catches a fraction proportional to its aperture area over the distance squared:

\[ P_r \approx P_t\,\frac{\rho\, A_r}{\pi R^2}\,\eta\, e^{-2\alpha R} . \tag{4.1}\]

Here \(\rho\) is the target's reflectivity at the laser wavelength, \(A_r\) the receiver aperture, \(\eta\) the optical efficiency and \(\alpha\) the atmospheric attenuation. Unlike radar's point targets, an extended target loses power only with the square of range, so a lidar's reach grows with the square root of reflectivity. If a lidar reaches 200 m on a 10% target, it reaches about 140 m on a 5% target and, in principle, over 500 m on an 80% white wall. The typical worst case on the road is a black car, whose paint can reflect only a few per cent in the near infrared, and a dark tyre or a person in black clothes. Lane markings, licence plates and traffic signs, which contain retroreflective beads, return far more than a diffuse surface and can saturate the detector.

The last factor in (4.1) is the weather. In fog, the attenuation coefficient follows from the visibility \(V\) as \(\alpha \approx 3.9/V\) (Koschmieder's relation, defined for visible light and similar in the near infrared, because fog droplets are much larger than either wavelength). In fog with 200 m visibility, a target at 50 m loses 8.5 dB on the round trip and one at 100 m 17 dB; in thick fog with 50 m visibility, a target at 25 m already loses 17 dB and one at 50 m 34 dB, which no realistic sensor margin covers. The lidar does not just lose range; droplets near the sensor scatter part of the pulse straight back and produce a cloud of false points around the car (Section 8.2).

Pitfall

Comparing two lidars by their headline range. "250 m" at 80% reflectivity and "200 m at 10%" are different sensors: the first may reach only about 90 m on a 10% target. Ask for the range on 10% (or lower) targets, with which probability of detection, in which part of the field of view and in which ambient light: range under full sunlight is shorter, because the sunlight background raises the threshold.

4.4Covering the field of view: spinning, scanning, flash

One laser beam measures one direction at a time. A lidar must cover a field of view of, say, 120° × 25° with a grid of 0.1°, about 300 000 directions, ten or twenty times per second. Four architectures exist (Figure 4.1, bottom).

Mechanical spinning lidars mount a column of 16 to 128 lasers and detectors on a head that rotates 10–20 times per second, sweeping a full 360° with vertical angles fixed by the laser positions. They are proven and give an all-round view, which is why they sit on robotaxi roofs and survey vehicles, but they are large, have moving parts and need a prominent mounting place. Scanning lidars for cars keep the electronics fixed and steer one or a few beams with a moving mirror: a micro-electro-mechanical (MEMS) mirror a few millimetres across that oscillates at kilohertz, a rotating polygon mirror, or a combination with a slower mirror for the second axis. They cover a forward field of view of roughly 120° × 25°, fit into a grille or behind the windscreen, and can concentrate points in a region of interest around the horizon, where distant objects appear. Most production automotive lidars are of this kind.

Flash lidars have no scanning at all: one wide laser flash illuminates the whole field of view, and a 2D array of SPAD pixels times the echo in every pixel at once, like a camera that measures distance. With no moving parts they are robust and small, but the laser energy is spread over the whole scene, so their range is short, typically tens of metres; they are used for the near field and as side or rear sensors. Solid-state beam steering with optical phased arrays, the optical analogue of the radar's antenna array, steers the beam electronically on a photonic chip; it is attractive and still emerging.

4.5Point rate and resolution: how many points land on a pedestrian

A lidar's point rate is its number of channels times the horizontal positions per sweep times the frame rate. A 128-channel spinning lidar with 2048 horizontal positions at 10 Hz produces 2.6 million points per second; a 32-channel one with 0.2° steps, 0.58 million. At about 16 bytes per point (coordinates, intensity, time, ring), 2.6 million points per second are 336 Mbit/s, which is why lidars connect over gigabit Ethernet.

What matters for perception is how many of these points land on an object, which falls with the square of range because the angular grid spreads out: at 100 m, a 0.1° step is 17.5 cm, a 0.2° step 35 cm. Figure 4.2 counts the points on a standing pedestrian for three typical beam patterns (experiments/lidar_points.py).

The table behind the figure shows the same law for a car's rear (1.8 m × 1.4 m): about 13 points from the 128-beam lidar at 100 m and 3 at 200 m, against 83 and 21 from the 0.1° scanner. A detector needs some minimum number of points to declare and classify an object, so the usable detection range for small objects is set by angular resolution at least as much as by the link budget, and it is far shorter than the headline range. This is the reason forward-looking automotive lidars concentrate their resolution in a narrow band around the horizon.

4.6FMCW lidar: the radar principle with light

An FMCW lidar transmits a continuous laser beam whose optical frequency is chirped, and mixes the echo with a copy of the outgoing light, exactly like an FMCW radar (Section 3.2), only at 193 THz instead of 77 GHz. The beat frequency gives range, and the Doppler shift gives the radial velocity of every single point directly: 1.29 MHz per m/s at 1550 nm, large enough to measure within one chirp. A point cloud in which every point carries its velocity makes it trivial to separate a walking pedestrian from the static background in one frame, without tracking.

Coherent detection brings a second advantage. Only light with the right optical frequency and phase mixes with the local copy, so sunlight and other lidars' pulses are almost entirely rejected, and the sensitivity approaches the physical limit of counting photons. The difficulties are in the optics: a laser with a very narrow linewidth whose frequency can be swept linearly over gigahertz, and a receiver that preserves the light's phase. Each point needs microseconds of signal, so FMCW lidars need many parallel channels to reach high point rates, which pushes them towards silicon-photonics chips. FMCW lidars exist in small series and are expected to grow; most lidars on the road today are pulsed time-of-flight.

4.7What the data looks like

A lidar delivers a point cloud: per point, x, y, z (or range and two angles), an intensity or calibrated reflectivity, a timestamp, the laser channel ("ring") and often a return number. Dense, but structured: a spinning lidar's points form rings on the road, and a scanner's points form a raster. How networks consume this is covered in BEV perception 7.1; here three properties of the raw data matter.

A spinning lidar's raw output is naturally an image: one row per laser beam and one column per firing position, with a range, a reflectivity and the ambient light in every pixel. Figure 4.3 shows one real frame from a 128-beam short-range spinning lidar on a car roof in that form, and Figure 4.4 the same frame as 3D points seen from above. The range image is why some networks process lidar as a 2D image with ordinary convolutions; the top view is what most 3D detectors and maps work with.

Multiple returns. A laser spot of 10–20 cm at 100 m can partly hit a branch and partly the wall behind it, or partly a raindrop and partly the car behind it. Lidars report the strongest and the last echo (or more), which lets software see through vegetation, fences and light precipitation.

Intensity. The strength of the echo, corrected for range, gives a reflectivity image. Lane markings, road signs and licence plates stand out clearly, which helps localisation against maps; black cars and tyres are faint (Figure 4.3, middle).

Motion distortion. A spinning lidar takes 100 ms for one 360° sweep at 10 Hz; at 30 m/s the car moves 3 m in that time, and a 20°/s turn rotates the car by 2°. The points at the start and end of the sweep are therefore measured from different positions, and the same wall appears bent or doubled unless every point is transformed with the ego pose at its own timestamp (deskewing). Scanning lidars have the same effect over their frame time. That is why lidar processing needs accurate ego-motion from the IMU and wheels (Chapter 6) and precise time synchronisation (Calibration and fusion 4).

4.8What lidar is good and bad at

Lidar is excellent at geometry: precise 3D position, size and shape of objects, free space, the road surface and kerbs, at any light level, at ranges where cameras' depth estimates fail. It does not need the object to be in its training data to see it. It is weak at semantics: it does not see colour, reads signs and markings only through reflectivity, and cannot see traffic-light colours. It measures no velocity unless it is FMCW. It suffers in fog, heavy rain, snow and spray, both by attenuation and by false points near the sensor, and from dirt and ice on its window, which is why production lidars come with washer nozzles and heaters. Retroreflectors can "bloom" into neighbouring directions and create ghost points; wet roads and glass reflect the beam away (giving missing points) or mirror objects (giving phantom points below the road). Lidars can interfere with each other, though coding and randomised firing have made this a minor issue. And they are still the most expensive sensor on the car, with prices for automotive units having fallen from tens of thousands of dollars to several hundred to about a thousand.

Steering the beam by spinning, by a mirror or with a flash gives a 3D point cloud with centimetre range accuracy and a tenth of a degree in angle, day and night. Its range depends on the target's reflectivity, about with the square root, so always ask for range on 10 per cent targets. 5Ultrasonic sensors: centimetres at parking speed

The ultrasonic sensor is the oldest and cheapest sensor of driver assistance: the round buttons in the bumpers that make the parking beeper beep. Twelve of them cost less than one radar. They measure distances of a few metres with centimetre precision to almost anything, including glass and thin poles, in the near field where cameras have blind spots. This chapter explains how they measure, how several of them locate an object, and why they are useless beyond a few metres and above walking pace.

5.1Why ultrasonic sensors

Parking needs the distance to whatever is closest to the bumper, down to 20–30 cm, for objects of every kind: a wall, a glass door, a pole, a bollard, a kerb, a child's bicycle. A camera has blind spots just below and in front of the bumper, and a single camera cannot measure a distance to a smooth wall; radar struggles at very short range and with plastic or glass. An ultrasonic sensor sends a short burst of sound and listens for the echo. Sound reflects off almost every solid surface regardless of colour, transparency or light, and at parking distances its slow speed makes timing easy. The sensors are small, robust, painted in the bumper's colour and cost a few euros each, so cars carry 8–12 of them for park distance control, and they feed automated parking and low-speed emergency braking.

5.2Send, ring, listen: how one sensor measures

The sensor is a piezoceramic disc glued to the inside of a small aluminium pot, whose bottom is the membrane visible in the bumper. A short burst of electrical pulses at the resonance frequency, typically 40–58 kHz, makes the membrane vibrate and emit a sound pulse of a fraction of a millisecond. Then the same membrane listens: an echo makes it vibrate again, and the piezo turns that into a voltage that is amplified, filtered and compared with a threshold. The distance follows from the round-trip time and the speed of sound:

\[ R = \frac{c_s\, t}{2}, \qquad c_s \approx 331.3 + 0.606\,T\ \text{m/s} \ (T \text{ in } ^\circ\text{C}). \tag{5.1}\]

At 20 °C sound travels at 343 m/s, so an echo from 1 m arrives after 5.8 ms and one from 5 m after 29 ms. The membrane keeps ringing for most of a millisecond after the burst, and it cannot detect echoes until the ringing has decayed: this dead zone is the first 15–20 cm, which is why parking systems use the minimum distance carefully and why newer sensors actively damp the ringing. The speed of sound depends on air temperature: a system that assumed 20 °C at −20 °C would overestimate distances by 7.6%, 2.3 cm at a 30 cm stopping distance, so the parking ECU uses the outside temperature from the vehicle bus. Wind, humidity and air turbulence have smaller effects. The sound beam is wide, typically ±60° horizontally and narrower vertically so that the road surface does not echo, so one sensor gives a distance but almost no direction.

5.3Where is it? Several sensors and cross echoes

Direction comes from combining sensors. Two sensors a distance \(2a\) apart that measure distances \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) to the same object place it on the intersection of two circles; with the sensors at \(x = \mp a\) on the bumper,

\[ x = \frac{r_1^2 - r_2^2}{4a}, \qquad y = \sqrt{r_1^2 - (x+a)^2} . \tag{5.2}\]

This trilateration is as good as the baseline allows. With sensors 0.4 m apart and 1 cm range noise, a simulation (experiments/sensor_numbers.py) gives a position good to about 4 cm sideways at 1 m but 9 cm at 2.5 m, while the distance along the line of sight stays within 1 cm: the same angle-versus-range asymmetry as radar, at a much smaller scale. Sensors also listen to each other's pulses: when one sends and its neighbour receives, the path length \(r_1 + r_2\) constrains the object to an ellipse, which adds measurements without extra firing time. The parking software combines these measurements over many cycles while the car moves, building a local map of obstacle points and lines (a wall, a parked car's side), and this map, not the raw distances, is what automated parking plans with.

Ultrasonic sensors also measure the free length of a parking slot while driving past: the side sensors see the first parked car end and the next one begin, and the wheel odometry measures the distance in between. A thin pole, a low kerb or the slanted front of a car can confuse the edges; the fusion with surround-view cameras (Camera geometry 8.3) fixes most of this in modern systems.

5.4Firing schedule, crosstalk and interference

A sensor must wait for echoes from its maximum range before firing again, about 30 ms for 5 m. Firing twelve sensors one after another would take 360 ms per cycle, during which a car at 5 km/h moves 0.5 m. Sensors are therefore fired in groups that are far enough apart not to hear each other, and the measurements of neighbours that do hear each other are used as cross echoes. A sensor cannot tell its own echo from another sensor's pulse or another car's parking sensor in the next parking space, except by timing and by the signal itself: modern sensors encode their pulses with frequency chirps or codes, so that each receiver correlates only with its own pattern and several sensors can fire at once. Ultrasonic noise from air brakes of buses and trucks, pneumatic tools and some road surfaces can still raise the noise floor and cause false warnings.

5.5What ultrasonic sensors are good and bad at

Good: cheap, robust, independent of light and colour, they see glass, plastic, thin poles and walls at 0.2–5 m with centimetre precision, exactly the near field cameras and radars cover poorly. Bad: short range (newer sensors reach 7–10 m, still only parking range), poor angular information, slow update (tens of milliseconds per measurement, so they are useful only up to about 10–15 km/h), and blind spots for objects that are very low, very thin or that deflect the sound away (a slanted surface reflects it elsewhere). Soft materials such as snow or thick clothing absorb sound. A layer of snow, ice or mud on the membrane damps it and makes the sensor blind, which the sensor detects from its changed ringing and reports as blocked. The data they deliver is simple: per sensor and cycle, one or a few echo distances with amplitudes, over a simple sensor bus to the parking ECU; newer sensors also report the echo envelope, from which software estimates whether an object is a high wall or a low kerb that the car could drive over.

6GNSS, IMU and wheel odometry: knowing where you are and how you move

Every sensor so far looks outward. A car must also know its own motion and position, because every outward measurement is taken from a moving platform, and because maps, navigation and lane-level features need the car's place on Earth. Three kinds of sensors do this: satellite navigation (GNSS) for absolute position, inertial sensors (IMU) for rotation and acceleration, and the wheel and steering sensors that every car already has. None is sufficient alone; together they are remarkably good.

6.1Why ego-motion and position matter to perception

Ego-motion enters perception in more places than one might expect. A radar measures radial velocity relative to the car, so the car's own speed must be subtracted to tell stationary from moving objects. A lidar sweep must be deskewed with the car's motion during the sweep (Section 4.7). A tracker predicts objects in a ground-fixed frame, which needs the car's rotation and translation between frames (Calibration and fusion 6.2). Online calibration compares sensor motions with the car's (3.3). Absolute position is needed for navigation, for map-based features such as lane-level highway pilots and speed-limit information, and as a source of absolute time. Errors in ego-motion show up as errors in everything else, often far from their cause.

6.2GNSS: position from satellite clocks

A GNSS receiver measures how long signals from satellites took to arrive. Each satellite of GPS, Galileo, GLONASS or BeiDou broadcasts its precise orbit and the time from its atomic clock; the signal needs about 67 ms from a GPS orbit 20 200 km high. Multiplying the travel time by the speed of light gives the distance to the satellite, and distances to several satellites at known positions give the receiver's position by trilateration, as with the ultrasonic sensors, but in 3D. The catch is the receiver's clock: a cheap crystal oscillator is not synchronised with the satellites' atomic clocks, and an error of 1 µs is 300 m of range. The receiver therefore treats its clock offset as a fourth unknown and solves for position and time together, which needs at least four satellites. Each measured range is therefore called a pseudorange: the true range plus the unknown clock offset times \(c\). A side effect is that a GNSS receiver is also an excellent clock, providing absolute time to tens of nanoseconds through a pulse-per-second output, which cars use as a time source (Calibration and fusion 4.5).

6.3GNSS errors and corrections: from metres to centimetres

A single-frequency receiver in open sky typically achieves 2–5 m. The error has several sources. The ionosphere delays the signal by metres to tens of metres depending on solar activity and time of day; because the delay scales with \(1/f^2\), dual-frequency receivers (L1 and L5 or E1 and E5a, a frequency ratio squared of 1.79) measure and remove it, which brings open-sky accuracy to about a metre. The troposphere, satellite clock and orbit errors add decimetres. The geometry of the visible satellites multiplies all errors (the dilution of precision): satellites spread across the sky give a good fix, satellites in a narrow strip of sky above a street give a poor one.

In cities the dominant errors are multipath and non-line-of-sight reception (Figure 6.1). A signal that arrives both directly and via a reflection distorts the correlation peak the receiver uses to time it, giving metres of error. A satellite hidden behind a building whose signal arrives only by reflection gives a range that is too long by the whole detour, possibly tens of metres, and nothing in the signal reveals it. A standalone receiver in a street canyon can be wrong by tens of metres and jump by several metres from one second to the next. In tunnels and multi-storey car parks there is no signal at all.

Corrections reduce the remaining errors. SBAS (satellite-based augmentation, such as EGNOS in Europe and WAAS in North America) broadcasts corrections for the whole continent and improves accuracy to about a metre. RTK (real-time kinematic) uses the phase of the carrier wave, whose wavelength is 19 cm on L1, instead of the timing of the code; with corrections from a reference station within some tens of kilometres, or a network of them, it reaches 1–2 cm, once the integer number of carrier cycles has been resolved. PPP (precise point positioning) and PPP-RTK use precise satellite orbits and clocks delivered over the internet or by satellite and reach about 10 cm, after a convergence time that modern services have shortened to under a minute. Lane-level navigation needs better than half a lane, about 0.5–1 m, reliably, which standalone GNSS cannot provide and corrected dual-frequency GNSS with inertial support can, in open sky.

For automated driving, accuracy is not enough; the system must also know when its position is wrong. Integrity monitoring computes a protection level, a bound that the true error exceeds only with a tiny probability, and raises an alarm when the position cannot be trusted. Jamming (cheap jammers overpower the very weak satellite signals) and spoofing (fake signals that pull the receiver to a wrong position) are real threats, which is one more reason never to rely on GNSS alone and to cross-check it against the IMU, the wheels and the map.

Numbers

1 µs of clock error = 300 m; 1 ns = 30 cm. Standalone single-frequency: 2–5 m; dual-frequency open sky: about 1 m; SBAS: about 1 m; PPP / PPP-RTK: about 10 cm; RTK: 1–2 cm. Urban multipath and non-line-of-sight: tens of metres. Update rate 1–10 Hz (some receivers 20 Hz). A lane is 3.5 m wide.

6.4The IMU: inertia measured on a chip

An inertial measurement unit contains three accelerometers and three gyroscopes on orthogonal axes, today as MEMS chips a few millimetres across. A MEMS accelerometer is a tiny proof mass on silicon springs; acceleration deflects it, and the deflection is measured as a change in capacitance between interleaved comb fingers. A MEMS gyroscope keeps a proof mass vibrating at a few kilohertz; when the chip rotates, the Coriolis force pushes the vibrating mass sideways in proportion to the rotation rate, and that sideways motion is measured capacitively. An accelerometer measures specific force: acceleration minus gravity, so at rest it reads 1 g upwards. The IMU runs at hundreds to thousands of samples per second with a latency of about a millisecond, much faster than any other sensor on the car, and it never loses its signal.

Its weakness is that position and orientation come from integrating its outputs, and integration turns every small error into a growing one. A constant gyro bias \(b_g\) gives a heading error that grows linearly, \(b_g t\). A constant accelerometer bias \(b_a\), integrated twice, gives a position error \(\tfrac{1}{2} b_a t^2\): 1 mg (0.01 m/s²) gives 18 m after one minute. Worse, a gyro error in roll or pitch tilts the computed vertical, and part of gravity then leaks into the horizontal acceleration: a tilt error of only 0.1° is an apparent acceleration of 1.75 mg, and because the tilt itself grows linearly with time, this error grows with \(t^3\). Real biases are not constant either: they drift with temperature and time (the bias instability of a datasheet), and white noise integrates into a random walk.

IMUs are graded by these errors. The MEMS sensors in an airbag or ESC control unit have biases that change by fractions of a degree per second with temperature and are compensated by estimating them online, for example whenever the car stands still. Better automotive-grade IMUs for automated driving reach bias instabilities of a few degrees per hour; tactical-grade sensors (fibre-optic gyros) are ten times better and navigation grade a thousand times better, at prices from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of euros. In a car, the useful number is the residual bias after the system has estimated it from GNSS, the wheels and standstill phases, which is what the interactive in Section 6.6 uses.

6.5Wheel odometry and the vehicle's own motion sensors

Every car with ABS has a wheel speed sensor at each wheel: an active magnetic sensor (Hall effect or magnetoresistive) that sees the teeth of a steel tone ring or the poles of a magnetic encoder ring in the wheel bearing pass by, typically 48 pole pairs or teeth per revolution. Counting both edges gives about 96 ticks per revolution, about 2 cm of travel per tick on a 2 m tyre circumference. Modern sensors also report the direction of rotation, which matters for parking manoeuvres and was missing in older sensors. The brake ECU turns the ticks into wheel speeds, and the vehicle's distance travelled is the ticks times the effective rolling circumference.

That circumference is the weak point. It changes by one to a few per cent with tyre pressure, tread wear, load, speed and tyre model; a 0.5% scale error is 5 m per kilometre, and wheel slip during strong acceleration, braking or on snow makes the wheels lie for a moment. Systems therefore estimate the effective radius online against GNSS. The difference between the left and right wheel speeds gives the yaw rate, \(\dot\psi = (v_r - v_l)/b\) with track width \(b\), but small relative errors matter: a 0.1% difference at 25 m/s on a 1.6 m track is 0.9°/s. Wheel speeds are excellent at low speed and for distance, poor for heading.

The other motion sensors come with the chassis. The steering angle sensor on the steering column gives the commanded curvature. The ESC's own yaw rate and lateral acceleration sensor (an automotive MEMS gyro and accelerometer, often in the airbag or brake ECU) gives heading change directly. Together with wheel speeds they are sent on the vehicle bus as a standard set of signals that every ADAS ECU uses for ego-motion.

6.6Dead reckoning through a tunnel

When GNSS disappears, in a tunnel or a car park, the car continues on dead reckoning: integrating its motion from its last good position. Figure 6.2 shows how quickly that drifts for the two usual ways of doing it, with residual errors typical after a good online calibration. Wheels plus gyro is the standard automotive combination: the wheels give the distance (error proportional to time, from the scale error), the gyro the heading (lateral error proportional to time squared, from the residual gyro bias). The IMU alone integrates acceleration twice and leaks gravity through tilt errors, so its error grows with the second and third power of time.

With the default values (90 km/h, 36°/h residual gyro bias, 1 mg accelerometer bias, 0.5% wheel scale error), wheels and gyro drift by 1.3 m in 10 s, 4.2 m in 30 s and 11 m after a minute, about evenly split between distance and heading error; the IMU alone is better for the first few seconds, 0.8 m after 10 s, but 80 m after a minute and over half a kilometre after two. A one-kilometre tunnel at 90 km/h takes 40 s. The lessons are general: an IMU alone bridges seconds, wheel odometry with a gyro bridges a tunnel to within a few metres, and lane-level accuracy over longer outages needs the world itself, lane markings seen by the camera or features matched against a map. The fusion of GNSS, IMU and wheels, usually an error-state Kalman filter that estimates the biases while GNSS is available, and its combination with map matching belong to the localisation chapter; the filtering machinery is in Calibration and fusion 8.

7Sensor characteristics: what the datasheet numbers mean

Sensor comparisons are made with a handful of numbers: range, field of view, resolution, accuracy, update rate and latency. Each hides a condition, and two of them are routinely confused. This chapter defines them precisely, then shows the single most useful comparison: how each sensor's uncertainty grows with distance.

7.1Range, field of view, resolution, accuracy and precision

Range is meaningless without a target and a probability: "detects a 10 m² target at 200 m with 90% probability per cycle" for a radar, "200 m on 10% reflectivity" for a lidar, "detects a car at 150 m" for a camera network (which also depends on lighting and the network). For perception, the useful range is often the classification range, which is shorter than the detection range. Field of view is the angular coverage, horizontal and vertical, and it trades against range: the same antenna or lens either looks wide and near or narrow and far, which is why front radars have two beams and cars carry both wide and telephoto cameras.

Resolution is the smallest separation at which two objects are reported as two: c/2B in radar range, the beam spacing for a lidar, roughly 2/N radians in radar angle. Accuracy is how close a single measurement is to the truth, including systematic errors (bias) such as a misaligned mount or a wrong speed of sound; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, the random part. A radar can be precise (its range readings scatter by 3 cm) yet inaccurate (all of them are 20 cm too long because of a cable-delay calibration), and it can have a coarse resolution of 8° in angle while locating a single target to 0.3°. Which number matters depends on the question: separating a pedestrian from a parked car is a resolution question, estimating time to collision is an accuracy question, and stable tracking is a precision question.

7.2Detection probability and false alarms

Every detector compares a signal with a threshold, and the threshold sets a trade-off: a lower threshold detects weaker targets (higher probability of detection, \(P_D\)) and lets more noise through (higher probability of false alarm, \(P_{FA}\)). A radar's CFAR is typically set for a false-alarm probability per cell of \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-6}\), which, over hundreds of thousands of range–Doppler cells per frame, still gives a few noise detections per frame that the tracker must reject. Datasheets that quote range without both numbers are not comparable. For perception networks the same trade-off appears as the precision–recall curve (NN training 7), and for functions as the balance between missed and unjustified braking (Functional safety 8.5). Beyond random false alarms, every sensor has systematic false positives that thresholds cannot remove: radar ghosts from multipath, lidar points from spray, camera detections of pictures of people. These are the triggering conditions SOTIF analyses for.

8Environmental limitations: weather, dirt, light, temperature and other cars

A sensor that works perfectly on a sunny test track meets rain, fog, snow, spray, salt, mud, low sun, tunnel exits, frost, summer heat, potholes and hundreds of other cars with the same sensors. Most field failures of perception systems are not software bugs but sensors meeting conditions their physics cannot handle, and the list of those conditions is the starting point of every SOTIF analysis (Functional safety 8.3, Standards 3.4).

8.1From physics to triggering conditions

Every degradation in this chapter can be predicted from the physics of the earlier ones. Visible and near-infrared light is scattered by particles larger than its wavelength (fog, spray, dust) and absorbed by dirt on the window; the camera additionally needs ambient light and an image that fits its dynamic range. Millimetre waves pass through fog and darkness but are attenuated by water films and reflected by metal; sound is absorbed by snow and carried away by wind. Satellites are hidden by buildings and tunnels. The point of the physics is that it tells the engineer where to look for trouble before the test drives find it, and which sensor will still work when another fails.

8.2Rain, fog, snow and spray

Fog is the strongest differentiator. Its droplets of 1–20 µm scatter visible and near-infrared light strongly: cameras lose contrast with distance, and the visibility \(V\) is by definition about where contrast falls to 2%. Lidars lose signal exponentially (Section 4.3: 17 dB at 50 m in fog of 100 m visibility) and see a "wall" of false points from the droplets close to the sensor, which software filters by intensity, echo shape and multiple-return analysis. Thermal cameras see somewhat further in light fog and haze, because 10 µm is comparable to the droplet size, but not through dense fog. Radar is practically unaffected.

Rain attenuates radar only slightly (Section 1.2) but adds clutter from drops and from the spray; it reduces lidar range moderately and adds sparse false points from drops; for cameras the main problem is not the rain in the air but the water on the windscreen between wiper strokes, which blurs the image, and the reflections on a wet road at night, where headlights and street lamps mirror into long streaks that can look like lane markings. Snow combines all of these: flakes give lidar false points and camera clutter, falling snow attenuates, and snow on the road hides lane markings and the road edge entirely, which no sensor can recover. Spray from the car ahead at motorway speed is a dense local cloud exactly where the sensors look; it is a lidar's worst everyday condition and a common camera problem.

8.3Dirt, water, ice and the cover in front of the sensor

Every sensor looks through something: the windscreen, a lens cover, a lidar window, a radome, a painted bumper. What sits on that surface often matters more than what is in the air. A film of road grime or salt scatters light and reduces a camera's contrast and a lidar's range; a drop of water on a fisheye lens blurs a quarter of its view; ice or a layer of wet slush on a radome attenuates the radar by several decibels and distorts its beam, so that angles are wrong as well as weak. Snow on an ultrasonic membrane damps it into silence.

Two engineering responses follow. Blockage detection: each sensor must notice that it is blind, from the absence of expected detections (a radar that sees no road clutter while driving), from image statistics (a camera with no sharp edges), from a changed ringing (an ultrasonic sensor) or from a window self-test (a lidar), and report it so that the function degrades openly instead of silently (Calibration and fusion 11.2). Cleaning and heating: the front camera relies on the wiper and a heated windscreen area, surround cameras and lidars get washer nozzles and sometimes air jets, radars and lidar windows get heaters against ice. Cleaning systems cost money, washer fluid and packaging space, and their design is part of sensor placement.

8.4Light: darkness, low sun, glare and tunnels

Light conditions affect only the passive sensors, and cameras most. At night a camera relies on headlights and street lamps: noise rises, exposure lengthens and motion blur appears, and unlit pedestrians at the roadside, especially in dark clothing, are the hardest night-time case, which is why night-time pedestrian AEB tests exist. Low sun in front saturates part of the image and produces lens flare and veiling glare on a dirty windscreen. Tunnel entries and exits demand more dynamic range than many sensors have for a few frames while auto-exposure catches up. Flickering LED lights, traffic lights and signs that pulse faster than the eye notices can be caught in their dark phase by short exposures (Camera pipeline 5). Thermal cameras, radar and lidar are unaffected by darkness; strong sunlight raises a lidar's background noise and shortens its range by a measurable amount.

8.5Temperature, vibration and ageing

Automotive sensors are qualified for roughly −40 °C to +85 °C ambient (more near the engine or behind a sun-heated windscreen) together with humidity, salt fog, stone chips, car-wash jets and years of vibration. Temperature shifts a camera's focus as the lens barrel and the plastic holder expand (Optics 1.5), changes MEMS IMU biases, drifts a microbolometer, changes the speed of sound for ultrasonic sensors and alters the radar's RF chain, which is calibrated over temperature. Vibration blurs images at long exposures, excites MEMS sensors (a mounting resonance near the gyro's drive frequency is a classic integration bug) and slowly loosens mountings, so extrinsic calibration drifts over the car's life and online calibration has to track it. Ageing of image sensors (hot pixels), laser diodes (output power) and plastic covers (yellowing, scratches) slowly reduces performance and must be part of the range budget at the end of life, not only at start of production.

8.6Other cars: interference

Active sensors share their medium with every other car. Radars in the same band receive each other's chirps (Section 3.9), lidars can see each other's pulses, ultrasonic sensors hear the neighbour's parking sensors. All three are handled by detection and suppression of interfered samples, randomised timing and coded signals, and none is a showstopper today, but they scale with the number of equipped cars, and a robust system must expect them. Passive sensors have their own version: headlights and high beams dazzling cameras.

10Choosing a sensor set: selection, placement, redundancy and cost

A sensor set is not chosen by picking the best sensors; it is derived from the functions the car must perform, the conditions it must perform them in (its operational design domain), the safety argument and a cost target. This chapter walks through that derivation for the running example and shows why most L2+ cars end up with similar sets.

10.1From functions to sensor requirements

Each function sets requirements on range, field of view, accuracy and latency, and simple kinematics turns them into numbers. The stopping distance at speed \(v\) with a total reaction time \(t_r\) (sensing, processing, actuation) and deceleration \(a\) is

\[ d = v\,t_r + \frac{v^2}{2a} . \tag{10.1}\]

Emergency braking for a pedestrian at 40 km/h with 0.6 s of total delay and 9 m/s² of full braking needs 13.5 m; at 60 km/h, 25 m. The perception must confirm the object one or two seconds earlier than that (a track needs several consistent measurements, and the function must distinguish a crossing pedestrian from one who will stop), so detection and classification of pedestrians are needed at 40–60 m across a wide field of view, especially for pedestrians stepping out from the side. Adaptive cruise at 130 km/h must react to a stationary end of a traffic jam: with 1 s of reaction and a comfortable 3.5 m/s², 222 m; with 5 m/s², 167 m. So the forward sensors need vehicle detection, and stationary-object handling, at 200 m and beyond, in the narrow forward cone. Lane change at motorway speed must see a car approaching from behind 25 m/s faster with a four-second margin: 100 m to the rear and side. Cross traffic at an intersection or when reversing out of a parking space: a car at 50 km/h, 3 s away, is 42 m to the side, at up to ±90°. Parking needs centimetres at 0.2–5 m all around.

Accuracy requirements follow the same way. To assign a car at 200 m to the correct lane, its lateral position must be good to about half a lane (1.75 m), an angle of 0.5°, which a conventional radar's resolution cannot guarantee when two cars are side by side, but which a camera does easily. Time to collision needs range rate, best from radar. And the functions must keep working, or degrade predictably, in the conditions of the operational design domain: if the highway assist is meant to work at night in rain, its sensor set must do so.

10.3Placement

Every position is a compromise between what the sensor sees, how it is protected and cleaned, how it looks and what it costs to install. The front camera sits behind the windscreen near the rear-view mirror: high enough to see over the car ahead and to look down on lane markings, inside the wiper area, protected and heated, and close to the driver's eye point, which is what traffic-sign and light recognition assume; the price is the windscreen's optical quality, reflections from the dashboard (hence a light shield) and recalibration after every windscreen replacement. The front radar sits behind the bumper or the brand badge, at bumper height, which is ideal for car rears but sees under trailers; the badge or bumper becomes part of its antenna system and must be designed with it. Corner radars hide behind the bumper corners at about ±45° and ±135°. Surround cameras sit in the grille, the tailgate and the side mirrors, low and close to the car, where they see the ground around it; mirror cameras move when the mirror folds. Ultrasonic sensors are distributed along the bumpers at about 50 cm height, spaced for overlap. A lidar on the roof sees furthest and over other cars, and is the best position technically, but it changes the car's silhouette and drag; production cars put it at the top of the windscreen, in the roof edge or in the grille, each with cleaning and heating. Trucks and buses have very different mounting options and needs, covered in the commercial-vehicle chapter.

Research and data-collection cars show what the sensors look like when styling does not matter: everything is stacked on a roof rack, where the view is clearest and the sensors are easy to swap and calibrate (Figure 10.1). Production cars hide the same functions in the bumper, the grille, the windscreen and the mirrors, and accept the compromises described above.

Pitfall

Treating the cover as somebody else's problem. A radar behind a newly styled bumper with a metallic paint, a camera behind a windscreen with a new heating wire pattern, or a lidar behind a slightly curved window can lose a large part of its performance or get distorted angles. The bumper, badge, windscreen and lidar window must be specified and tested together with the sensor from the start.

10.4Redundancy and graceful degradation

Redundancy is required where a function's safety depends on a sensor that can fail or be fooled. For emergency braking, the classic argument is diverse redundancy: the camera and the radar use different physical principles, so a condition that fools one (a shadow, a manhole cover) rarely fools the other in the same way, and requiring both to agree before strong braking reduces unjustified braking, while either one alone can trigger warning or partial braking (Functional safety 4.3, and ASIL decomposition in 3.3). For an eyes-off L3 function, the car must also continue safely for several seconds after one sensor fails, until the driver takes over, which is a strong argument for three modalities in the forward cone and for redundant power, compute and actuation.

Redundancy works only if the sensors' failures are independent, and common causes undermine it: a single dirty spot that covers both a camera and a lidar behind the same window, one wiring harness or power supply for two sensors, a software component shared by both channels, or a weather condition that degrades both (heavy spray affects camera and lidar together). The dependent-failure analysis looks for exactly these (Functional safety 5.5). And when sensors are lost, the system must degrade gracefully: reduce speed, disable lane changes, hand back to the driver with a warning, rather than continue at full capability with half its view (Calibration and fusion 11.4).

10.6Putting it together: why the L2+ set looks the way it does

Walking through the running example once more shows how the requirements produce the set. The forward cone needs vehicles at 200 m and beyond, pedestrians at 40–60 m with classification, lane markings, signs and lights, in rain and at night, with two independent sensors for braking: a front camera for semantics and lateral accuracy, a front radar for range, closing speed and weather. The sides and rear need fast cars at about 100 m and cross traffic at ±90°, in any weather, at low cost: four corner radars. The near field needs centimetres around the whole car and a picture for the driver: four fisheye cameras and twelve ultrasonic sensors. Ego-motion and position come from the wheels, the ESC's yaw-rate sensor, an IMU and GNSS, already partly in the car. The result is the 1V5R + 4V + 12U set of Figure 1.2, and its weak points are known in advance: stationary objects at long range rely on the camera and on radar elevation if available; pedestrians at night rely on the camera's low-light performance and the radar's sensitivity; lane-level position in tunnels relies on lane markings. Adding a lidar or 4D radar, more cameras or a thermal camera is the answer when the function list or the operational design domain grows beyond what that set can argue for.

Braking at urban speeds needs pedestrians at about 50 metres, cruise control on the motorway needs stopped cars at about 200 metres, lane change needs fast cars 100 metres behind, parking needs centimetres all around. 11.3One sentence each

  • Camera: measures direction and appearance with fine resolution, the only sensor that reads the road and classifies well, but its range error grows with distance and it needs light and a clean window.
  • Stereo camera: measures dense depth directly and finds unknown obstacles, but its depth error grows with the square of range.
  • Thermal camera: sees emitted heat, so it finds people and animals at night through glare, but cannot sit behind glass and has no colour.
  • Radar: converts range into a beat frequency and velocity into a phase rotation, precise in range and speed in any weather, coarse in angle, confused by stationary objects and multipath.
  • 4D imaging radar: hundreds of virtual channels give elevation and about one-degree resolution, which tells a stopped car from a bridge.
  • Lidar: times laser echoes to give a 3D point cloud with centimetre accuracy day and night, at a cost, with weak semantics and trouble in fog, snow and with dirt.
  • FMCW lidar: the radar principle with light, giving velocity per point and immunity to sunlight and other lidars.
  • Ultrasonic: times sound echoes at parking range for a few euros, sees glass and poles, and is limited to a few metres and walking speed.
  • GNSS: absolute position and time from satellite clocks, metres alone and centimetres with RTK, unreliable in cities and absent in tunnels.
  • IMU: rotation and acceleration at a kilohertz that never drops out, but integration makes its errors grow with time squared and cubed.
  • Wheel odometry: distance travelled from wheel ticks, good at low speed, with a scale error of about half a per cent and poor heading.
  • Sensor set: derived from functions, conditions, safety and cost, built on a camera–radar pair whose weaknesses do not overlap.

QSelf-check questions

Core questions with short model answers; the chapter sections hold the depth. The second group checks understanding with "why" and "what if" questions rather than definitions.

Q.1Core questions

Why do cars use several types of sensors instead of the best one?Basic

Because each sensor exploits one physical effect that answers some questions well and others badly, and fails in different conditions. Cameras classify and measure angles but not distance and need light; radar measures range and velocity in any weather but is coarse in angle; lidar gives 3D geometry but suffers in fog and costs more; ultrasonic sensors cover the last metres cheaply. Combining sensors with different physics improves accuracy (precise angle plus precise range), robustness (diverse failure modes) and coverage, which the safety argument needs.

Explain how an FMCW radar measures range.Basic

It transmits a chirp whose frequency rises linearly with slope S and mixes the echo with the signal it is currently transmitting. Because the echo is a delayed copy of the ramp, the frequency difference is constant during the chirp: the beat frequency f_b = 2RS/c. Sampling the beat signal and taking an FFT turns each target into a peak at a frequency proportional to its range, so several targets are measured at once.

What determines the range resolution of an FMCW radar?Basic

Only the swept bandwidth: ΔR = c/2B, 15 cm for 1 GHz and 3.75 cm for 4 GHz. Two targets closer than that fall into one FFT bin. Chirp duration and carrier frequency do not matter for resolution; the maximum range, however, is set by the IF bandwidth divided by the slope, so for a fixed IF bandwidth and chirp time more bandwidth means finer resolution but shorter maximum range.

How does the radar measure velocity, and what limits it?Intermediate

From the phase of the beat signal across consecutive chirps: between chirps the round trip changes by 2vT_r, rotating the phase by 4πvT_r/λ. An FFT across the N chirps of a frame gives the velocity. The phase is ambiguous beyond ±180°, so v_max = λ/4T_r (about ±32 m/s for 30 µs at 77 GHz), and the resolution is λ/2NT_r, set by the frame duration. Higher speeds fold back and are resolved with multiple chirp periods or tracking.

How does a radar measure angle, and what is a virtual array?Intermediate

A wave from angle θ reaches neighbouring antennas d apart with a phase difference 2πd·sinθ/λ; an FFT across the antennas gives the angle spectrum. Resolution is about 2/N radians for N antennas at λ/2. MIMO radars use M transmitters and K receivers arranged so that each pair has a distinct path, synthesising an array of M·K virtual elements: 3×4 = 12 gives about 10°, cascaded chips with 12×16 = 192 give about 0.6°. Transmitters are separated in time, phase or frequency.

Walk through radar signal processing from ADC to point cloud.Intermediate

The ADC samples the beat signal into a cube of samples × chirps × channels. A range FFT per chirp gives range bins; a Doppler FFT per range bin across chirps gives velocity; magnitudes are summed over channels into a range–Doppler map; a CFAR detector finds cells above the local noise; an angle FFT across channels for each detection gives azimuth (and elevation). The result is a detection list with range, radial velocity, angle, RCS and SNR, which is clustered and tracked inside the radar or on a central computer.

What is CFAR and why is it used?Intermediate

Constant false-alarm rate detection compares each cell of the range–Doppler map with an estimate of the local noise from neighbouring training cells (excluding guard cells) and declares a detection when it exceeds that estimate by a factor. A fixed threshold would produce a flood of false alarms when the noise floor rises, for example under interference or rain clutter, and miss targets when it falls; CFAR keeps the false-alarm probability roughly constant. Variants (cell-averaging, ordered-statistic) handle clutter edges and multiple targets differently.

Why did radar-based ACC historically not brake for stationary vehicles?Intermediate

For a moving car nearly everything is stationary, and a conventional radar without elevation and with a few degrees of angular resolution cannot reliably distinguish a stopped car in the lane from an overhead sign, a bridge, a manhole cover or a guardrail. Braking for all of them would cause unacceptable false braking, so stationary objects never seen moving were suppressed. Camera confirmation, better angular resolution and 4D radars with elevation address this.

What is radar cross-section and how does it affect detection range?Basic

RCS is the equivalent area of an isotropic reflector that would return the same power; about 10 m² for a car, 0.1–1 m² for a pedestrian, and it varies strongly with aspect. By the radar equation received power is proportional to σ/R⁴, so detection range scales with the fourth root of RCS: a radar that sees a car at 200 m sees a 0.5 m² pedestrian at about 95 m.

How does a lidar measure distance, and how does it scan?Basic

A pulsed lidar emits nanosecond laser pulses and times the echo, R = ct/2: one nanosecond is 15 cm. To cover the field of view it either spins a column of lasers (360°, robotaxis), steers one or a few beams with a MEMS or polygon mirror (forward 120° × 25°, most automotive units), or floods the scene with one flash onto a SPAD array (no moving parts, short range). FMCW lidars chirp a continuous laser and mix the echo, like radar.

905 nm versus 1550 nm lidar: what is the trade-off?Intermediate

905 nm uses cheap silicon lasers and detectors but reaches the retina, so eye safety limits the pulse energy and therefore range. 1550 nm is absorbed in the front of the eye, allowing much more energy and longer range, but needs InGaAs detectors and fibre lasers that cost more, and water absorbs it more, which hurts with wet windows and spray. Both are Class 1 eye-safe in their designs.

What does "200 m at 10% reflectivity" mean, and why does it matter?Basic

It is the range at which the lidar still detects a diffuse target that reflects 10% of the light at its wavelength, typically with a stated probability. For extended targets the received power falls with R², so range scales with the square root of reflectivity: about 140 m on a 5% black car. A headline range quoted at 80% reflectivity corresponds to a much shorter range on dark targets.

How does an ultrasonic parking sensor work and what are its limits?Basic

A piezo-driven membrane sends a short burst at 40–58 kHz and listens for the echo; R = c_s·t/2 with c_s ≈ 343 m/s at 20 °C, so 1 m is about 5.8 ms. Several sensors and cross echoes locate the object by trilateration. Limits: a dead zone of 15–20 cm while the membrane rings, a range of a few metres, a wide beam with no direct angle, slow cycles that limit it to low speed, dependence on air temperature, and blindness when covered by snow, ice or mud.

Why does GNSS need four satellites, and what limits its accuracy?Basic

The receiver measures travel times, but its clock is not synchronised with the satellites; the clock offset is a fourth unknown next to x, y and z, so at least four pseudoranges are needed. Accuracy is limited by ionospheric delay (removed by dual-frequency receivers), troposphere, orbit and clock errors, satellite geometry, and above all in cities by multipath and non-line-of-sight reception. Standalone accuracy is 2–5 m; corrections (SBAS, PPP, RTK) bring it to metres, decimetres or centimetres.

What is RTK and why is it so much more accurate?Advanced

Real-time kinematic positioning measures the phase of the carrier wave (19 cm wavelength on L1) instead of only the code timing, and uses corrections from a nearby reference station or network to cancel common errors. Once the integer number of carrier cycles between satellite and receiver is resolved, the range is known to millimetres, and the position to 1–2 cm. It needs a correction link, a clear sky and some time to fix the ambiguities, and loses the fix after obstructions.

What are the main error sources of a MEMS IMU?Intermediate

Bias (a constant offset, different at each power-on), bias instability and temperature dependence, scale-factor errors, axis misalignment, and white noise (angle and velocity random walk); also vibration sensitivity. Integrated, a gyro bias gives a heading error growing linearly and a lateral position error growing with t², an accelerometer bias a position error ½b_a t², and gyro errors in roll and pitch make gravity leak into horizontal acceleration, growing with t³.

How is a typical L2+ sensor set chosen?Intermediate

From the functions and their operational design domain: compute range, field of view and accuracy needs (AEB pedestrians at 40–60 m, ACC stationary vehicles at about 200 m, lane change 100 m behind, cross traffic at ±90°, parking centimetres), then cover each with a sensor that is good at it, and the safety-relevant forward cone with two diverse sensors. The result is usually a front camera and front radar, four corner radars, four fisheye cameras and twelve ultrasonic sensors, plus GNSS, IMU and wheel sensors; lidar or 4D radar are added for L3 or demanding urban functions. Cost includes compute, cleaning, calibration and validation, not only the sensor price.

What is the difference between resolution and accuracy?Basic

Resolution is the smallest separation at which two objects are reported separately; accuracy is how close one measurement of one object is to the truth. A radar with 8° angular resolution can locate a single target to 0.3°, because a strong peak's centre is far better defined than its width. Resolution matters when objects are close together (a pedestrian beside a car), accuracy when estimating position or time to collision for one object.

Q.2Why and what if

Why does doubling the radar's bandwidth not double its maximum range?Why

Bandwidth sets resolution, c/2B. Maximum range is set by the highest beat frequency the IF chain can sample, f_IF·c/2S; doubling B at the same chirp time doubles the slope and therefore halves the maximum range. And whether a target is actually detected at a given range is a separate question of transmitted power, antenna gain, RCS and integration time, by the radar equation.

What happens to a target closing at 50 m/s if the radar's v_max is 32 m/s?What if

Its phase step per chirp exceeds 180° and wraps, so the Doppler FFT places it at −50 + 2·32.4 = +14.9 m/s: apparently moving away. Without disambiguation the tracker would get an impossible velocity that contradicts the range change. Radars avoid this with alternating chirp periods (the true velocity is the one consistent with both), by tracking the range rate over frames, or by designing v_max above the expected relative speeds.

Why can a crossing pedestrian be hard for radar even when it is clearly in the field of view?Why

Its motion is perpendicular to the line of sight, so its radial velocity is almost the same as that of the stationary world at the same angle; it does not separate from the background in Doppler. It has a small RCS, often next to strong reflectors (parked cars, poles) that raise the local CFAR threshold, and the radar's coarse angle may merge it with them. Micro-Doppler from the legs, range separation and camera fusion help.

Why does a radar often see a car's two corners as one point, and why is that dangerous?Why

Both corners are at the same range and velocity and only a degree or two apart, far below a conventional radar's angular resolution, so their echoes fall in one range–Doppler cell and the angle estimator reports one angle in between. For object extent this loses the width; worse, a car next to a guardrail or a motorcycle next to a truck can be merged into a point that belongs to neither, at a wrong lateral position, which can put an object in the wrong lane.

A lidar's datasheet says 250 m. Why might it detect a black car at only 90 m?What if

If the 250 m is quoted at 80% reflectivity, the range on a 10% target is 250·√(0.1/0.8) ≈ 88 m, because received power on an extended target falls with R² and range scales with the square root of reflectivity. Black car paint can be 5% or less in the near infrared. Add sunlight background, a dirty window and the edge of the field of view, and the effective range shrinks further.

Why does a lidar sweep need the car's motion to be correct?Why

The points of one sweep are measured over 50–100 ms from a moving car: at 30 m/s the car moves 3 m during a 10 Hz sweep, and a turn rotates it by degrees. Treating all points as taken from one pose bends and doubles walls and shifts objects. Each point must be transformed with the ego pose at its own timestamp (deskewing), which needs good IMU and wheel data and synchronised clocks.

Why does fog hurt lidar and cameras but hardly radar?Why

Fog droplets are 1–20 µm, much larger than the 0.5–1.5 µm wavelengths of cameras and lidars, so they scatter that light strongly: contrast and signal fall exponentially with distance, and lidar gets false returns from droplets near the sensor. For a 3.9 mm radar wave the droplets are tiny, and scattering falls with the fourth power of size over wavelength, so it is negligible. Thermal cameras at 10 µm are in between.

What if the outside temperature signal is missing and the parking ECU assumes 20 °C at −20 °C?What if

Sound is 7.6% slower than assumed (319 versus 343 m/s), so every echo arrives later and every distance is reported 7.6% too long: an obstacle at 30 cm appears at 32 cm. The stop warning comes late by a couple of centimetres at the closest distances and proportionally more further out; in automated parking, the planned stopping point is too close. This is why the temperature signal is an input with a defined fallback.

Why is a single camera's distance error proportional to the square of distance?Why

Distance is inferred from an angle that shrinks with distance: a ground point at distance d is seen at about h/d below the horizon. A fixed angle error δα therefore changes the distance by d²δα/h. Stereo has the same law because disparity fB/Z also shrinks as 1/Z. Radar and lidar measure time of flight, whose error does not grow with range.

A GNSS position jumps 15 m sideways in a city street. What is the likely cause and how should the system react?What if

Non-line-of-sight reception or multipath: a satellite seen only by reflection gives a range too long by the detour, and the solution moves. The fusion should notice the jump as inconsistent with the IMU and wheel odometry (an innovation far outside its covariance), down-weight or reject the GNSS update, and rely on dead reckoning and map matching; integrity monitoring should flag the GNSS solution as untrustworthy.

Why is wheel odometry plus a gyro better than an IMU alone for a long tunnel?Why

Wheels measure distance directly, so their along-track error grows only linearly (scale error × distance), and the gyro's heading error causes a lateral error growing with t². An IMU alone must integrate acceleration twice, so a 1 mg bias gives 18 m in a minute, and gyro tilt errors leak gravity into the horizontal axes, growing with t³. With typical residual errors, wheels and gyro are about 11 m off after a minute at 90 km/h, the IMU alone about 80 m.

Why is a thermal camera not simply placed behind the windscreen like the front camera?Why

Ordinary glass is opaque in the 8–14 µm band the camera needs; it transmits only up to about 2.7 µm. The camera would see the windscreen's own temperature. Thermal cameras need germanium or chalcogenide optics and windows and are mounted in the grille or bumper, with their own heating and cleaning, which adds cost and exposure to dirt.

Camera and radar agree on an obstacle in the lane that does not exist. How can two diverse sensors both be wrong?What if

Through a common triggering condition: a metal plate or manhole cover gives a strong stationary radar return and looks like a dark object to the camera; a billboard with a person on it has metal structure for the radar and a real-looking person for the camera. Diversity reduces but does not eliminate common causes. Mitigations are radar elevation, camera height and texture cues, temporal consistency, and explicitly testing these scenarios in SOTIF validation.

What if you add a fifth radar in the same band to a car that already has four?What if

Besides cost and wiring, the radars must not interfere with each other: their chirps must be separated in time, frequency or code, or each must detect and suppress interference. Each also needs alignment at end of line and in service, a clear radome and a place in the bumper design, its detections must be time-synchronised and fused, and it adds data to the central computer. The benefit must be a coverage gap or a function that justifies all of that.

Why do most L2+ cars rely on camera plus radar rather than camera plus lidar?Why

Cost, maturity and weather. A radar costs a fraction of a lidar, has decades of automotive qualification, fits invisibly behind the bumper, measures closing speed directly and works in fog and rain, which complements the camera's weaknesses in exactly the conditions where the camera fails. Lidar is geometrically stronger but shares the camera's weakness in fog, spray and dirt, needs cleaning and a visible mounting. It is added when measured geometry is needed for eyes-off functions, and as prices fall.

Accuracy
How close a single measurement is to the true value, including systematic errors; compare resolution and precision.
Angle FFT
Fourier transform across a radar's (virtual) antenna channels that turns the phase progression of an echo into an angle spectrum.
Beat frequency
Frequency of the mixer output of an FMCW sensor, the difference between transmitted and received chirp, proportional to range: f_b = 2RS/c.
Bias instability
Slow random wander of an inertial sensor's offset, the floor of its Allan deviation, in °/h for gyros or mg for accelerometers.
CFAR
Constant false-alarm rate detection: a cell is a detection if it exceeds a multiple of the noise estimated from surrounding cells.
Chirp
A signal whose frequency changes linearly with time; the basic waveform of FMCW radar and lidar.
Dead reckoning
Estimating position by integrating measured motion (wheel distance, heading, acceleration) from a known start; its error grows with time.
Doppler FFT
Fourier transform across the chirps of a radar frame, per range bin, that turns the chirp-to-chirp phase rotation into radial velocity.
FMCW
Frequency-modulated continuous wave: a ranging method that transmits chirps and measures the frequency difference to the echo, used in radar and coherent lidar.
GNSS
Global navigation satellite systems: GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou; position and time from satellite signal travel times.
IMU
Inertial measurement unit: three accelerometers and three gyroscopes measuring specific force and rotation rate.
Integrity
For positioning, the ability to bound the error with a known probability (protection level) and to warn in time when the position cannot be trusted.
Microbolometer
Uncooled thermal detector pixel: a suspended membrane whose electrical resistance changes as absorbed infrared radiation warms it.
Micro-Doppler
Spread of radial velocities around a target's body speed caused by moving parts such as legs or wheels; a radar classification cue.
MIMO radar
Radar with several transmitters and receivers whose signals are separable, so that each transmitter–receiver pair acts as one element of a larger virtual array.
NETD
Noise-equivalent temperature difference: the temperature difference a thermal camera can just distinguish from its noise, typically below 50 mK.
Pseudorange
Satellite-to-receiver distance computed from signal travel time with the receiver's unsynchronised clock; true range plus clock offset times c, plus errors.
Radar cube
One frame of raw radar data: ADC samples × chirps × virtual channels, transformed into range, velocity and angle by three FFTs.
Radar cross-section (RCS)
Equivalent area of an isotropic reflector returning the same power as a target, in m² or dBsm; about 10 m² for a car, 0.1–1 m² for a pedestrian.
Range FFT
Fourier transform of the beat signal of one chirp; each frequency bin corresponds to a range bin of c/2B.
Resolution
The smallest separation in range, angle or velocity at which two objects are reported as two.
RTK
Real-time kinematic GNSS: carrier-phase positioning with corrections from a reference station or network, accurate to 1–2 cm.
SPAD
Single-photon avalanche diode: a detector biased so that one photon triggers a measurable avalanche; used in arrays and SiPMs in lidars.
Time of flight
Ranging by measuring the round-trip time of a pulse: R = ct/2 for light, R = c_s·t/2 for sound.
Trilateration
Locating a point from its distances to several known points, as the intersection of circles or spheres; used by ultrasonic sensors and GNSS.
Virtual array
The set of M·K effective antenna positions created by M transmitters and K receivers in MIMO radar; its size sets the angular resolution.
