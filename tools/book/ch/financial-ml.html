Financial machine learning and high-frequency trading

How electronic markets work from the order book up, why prices are so hard to predict, how a trading idea becomes an honest backtest, and how a trading system turns a market-data packet into an order in about a microsecond. Two journeys run through the chapter: one order, followed from a market-data packet through our strategy to the exchange's matching engine with the time spent at each step; and one strategy, a pairs trade, followed from the idea to a backtest that tells the truth.

1The big picture: what markets do and where speed and statistics fit

A market is a machine for two jobs: letting people who want to hold risk find people who want to get rid of it, and turning everyone's information into a single number, the price. Trading firms make money by doing one of those jobs a little better or faster than others: providing liquidity, connecting prices that should agree, or predicting where prices go next. Speed and statistics are the two tools; this chapter is about both.

1.1Markets exist to move risk and to discover prices

A pension fund receives contributions every month and must buy shares; a company's founder wants to sell some of hers; an airline wants to lock in the price of fuel. None of them wants to wait until exactly the right counterparty shows up. A market gives them a place where buy and sell interest from thousands of participants meets continuously, so that anyone can trade at almost any time at a price close to what everyone collectively believes the asset is worth. The two services are liquidity, the ability to trade a reasonable amount quickly without moving the price much, and price discovery, the process by which new information (an earnings report, a central bank decision, a large seller) is reflected in the price.

Today almost all of this is electronic. A stock exchange is a computer, the matching engine, that keeps a list of buy and sell orders for each instrument and matches them by fixed rules. Participants talk to it through network protocols, and it broadcasts every change to everyone who subscribes. Everything else in this chapter, from the statistics of returns to kernel bypass, follows from that fact: the market is a shared data structure that many programs update concurrently, and the rules of that data structure decide who wins.

Why it matters

Seen from an engineer's chair, a modern exchange is a distributed system with one strict serialisation point per instrument. Who reaches that point first, with which information, decides the outcome. That is why a field built on economics ends up caring about cable lengths, cache lines and clock synchronisation.

1.2Who trades, and why some need speed and others need statistics

The participants differ in what they need (Figure 1.1). Investors (pension funds, asset managers, retail) want to hold positions for months or years; for them trading is a cost, and they hand large orders to brokers whose execution algorithms cut them into many small child orders over hours. Market makers stand in the middle: they post buy and sell orders all day, earn the difference between the two prices (the spread) and carry the risk of the inventory they accumulate. Arbitrageurs keep related prices consistent: an index future and the stocks in the index, an exchange-traded fund and its basket, the same share on two venues. Statistical traders (quant funds) forecast returns over minutes to months from data and hold many small, diversified bets.

Speed matters most to market makers and arbitrageurs. A market maker's quote is a promise to trade at a price; when the price moves, the old quote becomes a gift to whoever hits it first, so the maker must cancel faster than the fastest taker can trade against it. An arbitrageur earns money only if he is first to see that two prices disagree. Statistics matter most to quant funds, whose edge is a forecast with a correlation to future returns of a few percent. High-frequency trading (HFT) is the overlap: short-horizon statistical signals (seconds to minutes, often milliseconds) executed by systems where every microsecond counts.

Interesting detail

Every trade has a maker and a taker. The maker had an order resting in the book; the taker sent an order that crossed the spread and traded with it immediately. Makers provide liquidity and are usually paid a small rebate; takers consume it and pay a fee. The same firm can be a maker in one trade and a taker a millisecond later.

1.4Why finance is an unusually hard machine-learning problem

If you come from computer vision, the first surprise is how little signal there is. A cat is a cat in every frame; the label is stable, the input contains the answer, and a good model reaches 95 % accuracy. A stock's return tomorrow is mostly noise: if prices could be predicted from public data, traders would buy until the predictable part was gone. What is left is a signal with a correlation of 0.01 to 0.1 to the target, buried in noise that changes its own character over time. A model that predicts the sign of the next return correctly 51 % of the time can be a good business; a model that claims 60 % on daily data is almost certainly leaking information from the future (Chapter 7).

Three more properties make it hard. The data are non-stationary: the relationship between features and returns changes with regimes (low and high volatility, rising and falling rates, new regulation, new competitors). The environment is adversarial: when a pattern becomes known and traded, the trading removes it, so success destroys its own signal (alpha decay). And there is only one history: you cannot collect another 2008 to test a model on, so every test you run on the same past data uses up a little of its power to tell you the truth (Chapter 8).

Pitfall

Habits from vision and NLP transfer badly: shuffled K-fold cross-validation, big models with millions of parameters, accuracy as the metric, and "more epochs until the validation loss is low". In finance each of these produces a model that looks excellent and loses money. The fixes (purged walk-forward validation, small models, P&L after costs as the metric) are in Chapters 5, 7 and 8.

2Exchanges and the limit order book

The limit order book is the data structure at the centre of every electronic market. Understanding what an order is, how the book is kept and how the matching engine decides who trades with whom explains most of what follows: why spreads exist, why queue position is valuable, and why everyone wants to be fast.

2.1Orders: what you can ask an exchange to do

An order says: side (buy or sell), quantity, instrument, and how the price is set. A limit order names the worst price you accept: "buy 500 at 100.02 or lower". If someone is already selling at that price or better, the order trades immediately (it is marketable); otherwise it rests in the book and waits. A market order has no price limit and trades against whatever is available, which is convenient and dangerous in a thin book. Most professional flow uses limit orders, because a limit is the only protection against trading at a terrible price.

A time-in-force says how long the order lives: day orders rest until the close; IOC (immediate or cancel) trades whatever it can now and cancels the rest; FOK (fill or kill) trades the whole quantity now or nothing. Further flags shape behaviour: post-only orders are rejected (or repriced) if they would trade immediately, which guarantees maker status; iceberg orders show only part of their size; hidden orders show nothing. Finally, participants cancel and modify orders constantly. On a typical stock most orders are cancelled rather than executed; the ratio of messages to trades is commonly in the tens, and for some participants much higher.

In practice

A modify that reduces quantity usually keeps the order's place in the queue; a modify that changes the price or increases the quantity is treated as cancel plus new order and loses its place. The exact rules differ between venues and are part of the specification every trading system must implement.

2.2The limit order book and price-time priority

The book (Figure 2.1) holds all resting limit orders for one instrument, buys (bids) on one side and sells (asks or offers) on the other. The highest bid and the lowest ask are the best bid and offer (BBO, also "top of book" or "the touch"); their difference is the spread, and the average of the two is the mid price. Each price is a level; the quantity resting at a level is its depth. Prices are not continuous: they move in steps of the tick size, one cent for most US stocks.

When orders conflict, the matching engine applies price-time priority (also FIFO): better price first; at the same price, the order that arrived first trades first. Some markets, notably several interest-rate futures, use pro-rata matching at a level instead, sharing each fill in proportion to resting size, which rewards size rather than speed. Price-time priority is the rule that turns latency into money: if many participants want to buy at 100.01, the one whose order reached the engine first will be filled first, and the one at the back of the queue may never be filled at all, or only when the price is about to move against him.

Why it matters

A resting order at the front of the queue is valuable: it trades when the market wants to trade at that price, and it is first to trade while the price is still fair. An order at the back is filled mostly when a large aggressive order sweeps the whole level, which is exactly when the price is about to move through it. Much of market-making technology exists to get to the front of queues and to leave them quickly.

2.3Inside the matching engine: one incoming order, step by step

The matching engine runs a simple loop per instrument: take the next incoming message in arrival order, apply it to the book, emit execution reports and market-data updates. It is single-threaded per book on purpose; one sequence of events per instrument makes the result deterministic and auditable. Figure 2.2 steps through a buy limit order for 900 shares at 100.03 arriving at the book from Figure 2.1.

The order is checked (instrument, quantity, price within the allowed band, participant limits) and compared with the opposite side. The best ask, 100.02, is at or below the limit 100.03, so the order is marketable.

The engine fills against the best level first, and within the level against the oldest order: 200 against A1, then 100 against A2. Each fill happens at the resting order's price, 100.02. The buyer was willing to pay 100.03 and gets price improvement.

The 100.02 level is empty, so the engine moves to 100.03, still within the limit, and fills A3 (300) and A4 (200). The order has now bought 800 shares; 100 are left.

The next ask is 100.04, above the limit. The remaining 100 shares rest as a new bid at 100.03, which becomes the best bid. The spread is now one tick, 100.03 to 100.04. The engine publishes the trades and the book changes as market data; the buyer's average price is 100.02625.

Two details matter for the rest of the chapter. Every step is published: other participants see the trades and the new book within microseconds, and their programs react to them. And the engine does not care how the order was meant: whether it came from a pension fund's algorithm or from a market maker's mistake, a marketable order trades. Protection against mistakes lives outside the engine, in price bands, risk checks and the kill switch (Section 11.4).

Talk track

"The matching engine is a single-threaded state machine per instrument with price-time priority. An incoming order first trades against the opposite side, best price first and oldest order first within a price, always at the resting order's price, and any remainder rests at its limit. Because every event is sequenced and published, the whole market is effectively a replicated log of order events."

2.4Market data: L1, L2, L3 and how a feed is delivered

Market data comes in three levels of detail. Level 1 is the top of book and the last trade. Level 2 is aggregated depth: the total quantity at each of the best N prices. Level 3, also called order-by-order or market-by-order, is every individual order event: add, execute, cancel, replace, each with an order id. From an L3 feed a participant can rebuild the exact book the exchange holds, including queue positions; that is what HFT systems consume. Nasdaq's TotalView-ITCH is a typical L3 protocol: binary, fixed-layout, big-endian messages. An "Add Order" message is 36 bytes (type, stock locate, tracking number, a 6-byte timestamp in nanoseconds since midnight, an 8-byte order reference, side, shares, an 8-character symbol and a 4-byte price with four implied decimals).

The feed is sent by UDP multicast: one packet from the exchange reaches every subscriber at the same time, without any per-client connection. UDP can lose packets, so each packet carries a sequence number, and exchanges publish the same stream twice, on an A and a B feed over separate network paths (Figure 2.3). The feed handler takes whichever copy of each sequence number arrives first and drops the duplicate (A/B arbitration). If a number is missing on both feeds, the handler has a gap: the book can no longer be trusted, and it must recover from a retransmission service or from a periodic snapshot, while the strategy stops quoting on that instrument. Order entry is different: it uses TCP sessions (protocols such as OUCH or FIX), because orders must never be lost or reordered.

Pitfall

A book built from an incomplete feed is silently wrong: a missed cancel leaves a phantom order at the top of the book, and a strategy that trusts it will quote against a price that does not exist. Gap detection must stop trading on that instrument immediately; "probably fine" is how firms lose money on a quiet afternoon.

2.5Many venues: fragmentation, best price and dark pools

A US stock trades on more than a dozen exchanges and many more off-exchange venues; a European stock on its home exchange and several multilateral trading facilities. The same instrument therefore has many books. Regulation ties them together: in the US, Regulation NMS requires that orders are not executed at prices worse than the best displayed quote on another exchange (the order protection rule), and a consolidated feed, the SIP, publishes the national best bid and offer (NBBO). The SIP is slower than the exchanges' own direct feeds, because it collects quotes from all venues in one place and redistributes them. A firm that reads the direct feeds sees price changes earlier than one that reads the SIP; that difference is one of the latency races of Chapter 9.

Dark pools are venues that do not display orders; trades typically happen at the mid price of the lit market, which suits large investors who do not want to reveal their size. Auctions run at the open and the close: orders collect for a while, then the engine finds the single price that maximises traded volume and executes everything crossing at that price. On many markets the closing auction is now a large share of daily volume, because index funds trade at the closing price.

2.6Tick size, fees and rebates

The tick size is a design decision with real consequences. If the tick is large relative to the price, the spread is stuck at one tick, queues at each level are long, and queue position becomes the main competitive variable. If the tick is small, participants can step in front of each other for a trivial price improvement, and queues matter less. Regulators adjust tick sizes for this reason: the EU sets them by tables that depend on price and liquidity.

Fees shape behaviour as well. Under the common maker-taker model the taker pays a fee and the maker receives a rebate, each a fraction of a cent per share in the US. For a market maker trading a stock with a one-cent spread, a rebate of a few tenths of a cent is a large part of the profit; it is why maker strategies care so much about which venue to rest an order on. Some venues invert the model (taker-maker), paying takers and charging makers, and attract different flow. A backtest that ignores fees and rebates gets the sign of a high-frequency strategy wrong as often as not.

Numbers

One cent on a 100-dollar stock is 1 basis point (bp, 0.01 %). The spread of a liquid large-cap US stock is typically one tick, around 1 bp; small caps and less liquid stocks have spreads of 10 to 100 bp. Fees and rebates of 0.1 to 0.3 cents per share are 0.1 to 0.3 bp on that 100-dollar stock: small, but the same order of magnitude as a high-frequency strategy's whole edge.

3Market microstructure: spread, liquidity, slippage and impact

Microstructure is the study of how the mechanics of trading shape prices: why there is a spread at all, what a trade costs, and why prices move when you trade. These costs decide whether a strategy that predicts correctly can make money, so they come before any forecasting.

3.1Why a spread exists

Someone who posts both a bid and an ask offers a service: immediacy. The spread is the price of that service, and it covers three costs. The order-processing cost is the cost of running the business: technology, fees, capital. The inventory cost is the risk of holding a position the market maker did not choose: after buying from a seller, the maker holds shares whose price may fall before a buyer comes. The third and most interesting is adverse selection. Some of the people trading against the maker know more than the maker: they have just read the news, or they see a price move on another venue a few microseconds earlier. Against them the maker always loses, because they buy only before the price rises and sell only before it falls.

The Glosten-Milgrom model (1985) shows the consequence. If a fraction of traders is informed, the maker must set the ask above the expected value given that someone buys, because a buy is itself evidence that the price is going up. The spread is then wide enough that the profit from uninformed traders pays for the losses to informed ones. More informed flow, wider spreads; that is why spreads widen before earnings announcements and in fast markets.

Talk track

"The spread pays the market maker for three things: processing costs, inventory risk and adverse selection. Adverse selection dominates: some traders know more than the maker, a trade against them is a loss, and the spread must be wide enough that the uninformed flow pays for it. That is why spreads widen around news."

3.2Mid, microprice and order-book imbalance

The mid price is the simplest estimate of the "fair" price, but it ignores the quantities. In Figure 2.1 the best bid holds 400 shares and the best ask 300; if the ask held only 50, everyone would expect the next tick to be upward, because a small trade would clear the ask. The order-book imbalance captures this: \(I = (Q_b - Q_a)/(Q_b + Q_a)\), from -1 (all quantity on the ask) to +1 (all on the bid). The microprice weights the two prices by the opposite side's quantity, \(P_\text{micro} = (P_b Q_a + P_a Q_b)/(Q_b + Q_a)\), so it sits closer to the side that is about to be consumed. Stoikov (2018) shows a refined version that is a better short-term predictor of the next mid price than either.

Imbalance and its time-series cousin, order-flow imbalance (the net quantity added to the bid minus the ask over a short window; Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov 2014), are the most robust short-horizon signals in equities and futures. They predict the next few ticks with real but small skill, and almost every HFT strategy uses some version of them. Our latency experiment in Chapter 10 uses imbalance as its toy strategy for that reason.

3.3Walking the book: what a market order really costs

A small market buy order pays the best ask: half the spread above the mid. A larger order consumes the best level and continues to the next, paying a worse price for each share beyond the first level's depth. This is slippage: the difference between the price you expected (often the mid or the price when you decided) and the average price you got. Figure 3.1 lets you push a market buy order into a book and watch what it costs.

In the normal book, 500 shares cost exactly the half-spread, 1 bp. 5,000 shares take four levels and cost 2.74 bp above the mid; 10,000 take six levels and cost 4.02 bp. In the thin book, which is what a liquid stock looks like for a few seconds after a surprise, 3,000 shares already cost 4.43 bp and 5,000 shares exhaust everything visible. The cost curve is concave in the visible book (each new level is a bit worse), but in reality it is worse than the picture: as soon as the first levels are taken, other participants cancel their remaining asks or move them up, because a large buyer is evidence that the price will rise. The visible book is a snapshot, not a promise.

Pitfall

Backtests often assume fills at the mid or at the last trade price. For any strategy that trades more than the top-of-book quantity, or trades when everyone else does (at news, at the open), that assumption overstates profits by the whole slippage, which for a short-horizon strategy can be larger than its edge.

3.4Market impact and the square-root law

Slippage against the visible book is the immediate cost of one order. Market impact is the broader effect: a large order executed over hours as many small child orders still moves the price, because other participants infer from the persistent buying that someone knows something or needs to buy. Impact is usually split into a temporary part, which decays after the trading stops (the liquidity was simply used up and gets replenished), and a permanent part, which stays because the market updated its view of the value.

A remarkably stable empirical regularity describes the total: the square-root law. The average impact of a metaorder of size \(Q\) is roughly \(\Delta P / P \approx Y \sigma \sqrt{Q/V}\), where \(\sigma\) is the daily volatility, \(V\) the daily volume and \(Y\) a constant of order one. It has been measured across stocks, futures, options and even cryptocurrencies. Two consequences: impact grows much more slowly than size (four times the size costs only twice as much per share), and the total cost grows like \(Q^{3/2}\), which is what limits the capacity of any strategy (Section 6.5).

Numbers

A stock with 2 % daily volatility: buying 1 % of the daily volume moves the price by about \(2\% \times \sqrt{0.01} = 0.2\%\), 20 bp, for \(Y=1\). Buying 10 % of daily volume costs about 63 bp. A strategy that expects to earn 30 bp per trade can therefore only trade a small fraction of daily volume before impact eats the whole edge.

3.5Adverse selection, seen through markouts

How does a market maker know whether its fills are good? It measures markouts: for each fill, the profit if the position had been closed at the mid price some time after the fill, averaged over many fills. A fill against an uninformed trader keeps the half-spread; a fill against an informed one starts with the half-spread and then loses as the price moves. Figure 3.2 shows markouts from our market-making simulation, where one fill in six is against a trader who knows that the price is about to move by 5 cents.

Real market makers look at exactly these curves, split by venue, counterparty type, time of day and order type. A curve that falls steeply in the first milliseconds means the maker is being picked off by faster traders (the quote was stale); a curve that falls over minutes means the flow carries real information. The response differs: the first calls for faster cancellation, the second for wider quotes or avoiding that flow. "Toxic flow" is the industry's word for fills with bad markouts.

Interesting detail

Retail order flow in the US is valuable precisely because its markouts are good: individual investors trading a few hundred shares rarely know where the price is going in the next second. Wholesalers pay brokers for the right to execute it (payment for order flow), a practice that is restricted or banned in some other jurisdictions.

3.6Measuring liquidity and execution quality

The quoted spread is the easy number, but trades often happen inside or outside it, so practitioners use measures based on actual trades. The effective spread is twice the distance between the trade price and the mid at the time of the trade; it is the round-trip cost a taker really paid. The realised spread is twice the distance between the trade price and the mid some time later (for example five seconds); it is what the maker really kept. Their difference is the price impact of the trade, the part lost to adverse selection. The relation is the markout of Section 3.5 written as a formula.

Depth (shares within some distance of the mid), resilience (how fast the book refills after a large trade) and the cost of trading a given size (as in Figure 3.1) complete the picture. Liquidity is not one number; a stock can have a one-tick spread and very little depth behind it, which looks cheap for small orders and is expensive for large ones.

4Time series: what returns look like and what can be forecast

Before building a model, look at the data. Financial returns have a handful of properties so consistent across markets and decades that they are called stylised facts: almost no linear predictability, fat tails, and volatility that comes in clusters. Each one tells you something about what kind of model can work and how to evaluate it.

4.1Prices, returns and stationarity

Prices themselves are useless as model inputs. A price of 100 means nothing without knowing yesterday's; the series wanders without a fixed mean (it is non-stationary), so any statistic computed on one period does not describe another. Models therefore work with returns, the relative change from one time to the next. The log return \(r_t = \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})\) is the standard choice: log returns add up over time (the return over a week is the sum of the daily ones), they are close to the simple return for small moves, and a price that can never go below zero maps to a return that can be any real number.

A series is (weakly) stationary if its mean, variance and autocorrelations do not depend on time. Returns are much closer to stationary than prices, but not completely: their variance changes from calm to turbulent periods (Section 4.2), and the relationships between them change with regimes (Section 8.4). Statistical tests such as the augmented Dickey-Fuller test check for a unit root, the property that makes a price series wander forever; a price fails it, a return series passes it, and a well-built pairs spread (Chapter 6) should pass it too.

Why it matters

Almost every machine-learning method assumes that the training data and the future come from the same distribution. Making inputs stationary (returns instead of prices, z-scores instead of levels, ratios instead of amounts) is the cheapest way to get closer to that assumption. It is the time-series equivalent of normalising images.

4.2Stylised facts, measured on real data

Our experiment (experiments/stylized.py) measures the facts on two public datasets: the Shiller S&P 500 monthly series since 1871 and two years of daily Apple prices. Figure 4.1 shows the two most important ones.

Fat tails. The kurtosis of monthly S&P returns since 1950 is 7.8, against 3 for a normal distribution; over the full history since 1871 it is 14.2. In 920 months a normal distribution expects 2.5 months beyond three standard deviations and 0.06 beyond four; the data have 12 and 3. The daily Apple returns show the same: kurtosis 6.1, and four days beyond four standard deviations in two years where a normal distribution expects 0.03. Any risk model that assumes normality underestimates the frequency of large losses by orders of magnitude.

No linear predictability. The autocorrelation of Apple's daily returns at lags 1 to 3 is +0.015, -0.029 and -0.039, all well inside the noise band of ±0.087 for 505 days. Yesterday's return tells you essentially nothing about today's sign. This is not a property of Apple: it is what you expect when many participants trade any obvious pattern away.

Volatility clustering. The absolute returns, a simple measure of volatility, are clearly correlated: +0.14 at lag 1 for Apple's daily returns, and for the monthly S&P still +0.145 at a lag of twelve months. Big moves follow big moves, quiet follows quiet. Apple's rolling 21-day annualised volatility ranged from 8 % to 47 % within two years. Volatility is therefore forecastable even though the direction is not, which is why volatility models (GARCH, Section 4.3) work and why risk limits should scale with current volatility.

Pitfall

The monthly S&P returns have a lag-1 autocorrelation of +0.27, far outside the noise band. A naive analyst would build a momentum strategy on it. But the Shiller series is the monthly average of daily prices, and averaging a random walk produces exactly this: our simulation of monthly averages of a pure random walk gives +0.24 (Working, 1960, derived the theoretical value of 0.25). You cannot trade at a monthly average price, so the "signal" is untradeable. Always ask how a price in a dataset was produced: average, close, last trade, mid, or bid.

4.3AR, ARIMA and GARCH: what the classical models capture

The classical time-series toolkit models a series as a function of its own past. An AR(p) model predicts the next value as a weighted sum of the last p values plus noise: \(r_t = c + \sum_i \phi_i r_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t\). An MA(q) model uses the last q noise terms; ARMA combines both; ARIMA adds differencing (the I, "integrated") to handle a non-stationary level such as a price. For returns these models find what the autocorrelation already showed: coefficients close to zero. They are more useful for series that are genuinely persistent, such as a pairs spread (an AR(1) with \(\phi\) below 1 is the discrete version of the mean-reverting process of Section 6.1), volumes, spreads and volatility itself.

The model that works on returns is about the variance, not the mean. ARCH (Engle, 1982) and GARCH (Bollerslev, 1986) let today's variance depend on yesterday's squared return and yesterday's variance: \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2\). With typical daily estimates of \(\alpha \approx 0.05{-}0.1\) and \(\beta \approx 0.9\), a shock raises volatility, which then decays slowly back to its long-run level, reproducing the clustering of Figure 4.1. GARCH forecasts feed risk limits, position sizing (smaller positions when volatility is high) and option pricing.

In practice

Few trading firms forecast returns with ARIMA. They use the classical models as baselines and as feature generators: a GARCH volatility estimate as an input, an AR(1) half-life to choose a holding period, a Kalman filter (Mathematics: the Kalman filter) to track a slowly changing hedge ratio. The return forecasts themselves come from many weak features combined, as in Chapter 5.

4.4Signal to noise: why a correlation of 0.05 can be a business

The quality of a forecast is usually measured by the information coefficient (IC): the correlation between the forecast and the realised return, often computed across many stocks each day. An IC of 0.05 sounds worthless; it means the forecast explains 0.25 % of the variance. Yet it can support a profitable strategy, because it is applied many times. Grinold's fundamental law of active management states it compactly: the information ratio (a Sharpe ratio relative to a benchmark) is approximately \(IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{\text{breadth}}\), where breadth is the number of independent bets per year. An IC of 0.05 applied to 500 stocks once a month gives about 6,000 bets a year and an IR of about 3.9, before costs. The same IC applied to one index once a month gives 0.17.

This explains the shape of the industry. Statistical strategies spread a weak edge over many instruments and many decisions; HFT applies an even weaker edge per trade millions of times a day. Both live from breadth, and both are destroyed by costs, because the cost is paid on every one of those bets while the edge per bet is tiny. The law also has a warning built in: it assumes independent bets. Five hundred stocks that all move with the market are not five hundred independent bets, and the true breadth is often much smaller than the count.

Numbers

Sign accuracy and IC are related: for a forecast with correlation \(\rho\) to a symmetric return, the probability of getting the sign right is about \(1/2 + \arcsin(\rho)/\pi\). An IC of 0.05 corresponds to 51.6 % hit rate, an IC of 0.1 to 53.2 %. A daily model that reports 60 % should make you look for leakage before you celebrate.

4.5Which clock: time bars, volume bars and event time

Data must be sampled before it can be modelled, and the choice of clock matters. Time bars (one-minute, daily) are the default, but markets do not process information at a constant rate: the first and last half hours of the day carry far more trades than lunchtime, so time bars mix very different amounts of information. Volume bars (a new bar every N shares) and dollar bars (every N dollars traded) sample by activity instead; their returns are closer to normal and more homoscedastic, which makes many models behave better. López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning (2018) popularised these alternatives.

At the highest frequencies there are no bars at all: the system reacts to each event (order add, cancel, trade) as it arrives, and features are defined in event time, for example "imbalance over the last 50 book updates" rather than "over the last second". Event time adapts automatically to busy and quiet periods, which is the same reason an automotive tracker updates per measurement rather than on a fixed timer.

5Features and statistical learning

A financial model is only as honest as its inputs. The two disciplines that matter most are temporal: each feature must use only information that was available at the moment of the decision, and each label must be defined so that it measures what the strategy would actually earn. Model choice comes after, and simpler usually wins.

5.1Point in time: a feature may only use what was known

Every data point has two timestamps: the event time it describes and the knowledge time at which it became available to you (Figure 5.1). A quarterly earnings figure describes the quarter ending 30 September, is published weeks later, and is often revised months after that. A trade happens at the exchange and is visible in your system some microseconds later. A backtest that joins data on event time uses information before it existed: the earnings are known on 30 September, the revised figure is used in place of the original, the trade is acted on before it could have been seen. All three make results look better than reality.

The remedy is a point-in-time database that stores every version of every value with the time it became known, and an "as-of" join that, for each decision time, picks the latest version known at that time. Commercial datasets sold as point-in-time exist for exactly this reason; a dataset of today's company fundamentals with history attached is not one.

Pitfall

Survivorship bias is the same mistake applied to the universe: testing a strategy on today's index members means testing only on companies that survived and grew enough to be in the index. The ones that went bankrupt, were delisted or shrank are missing, and they are exactly the ones a long-only strategy would have lost money on.

5.2Feature families

Features come in a few families. Price and volatility features: returns over several lookbacks (the past minute, day, month), moving-average distances, realised volatility, the high-low range. Order-book and flow features, mostly for short horizons: the imbalance and microprice of Section 3.2, order-flow imbalance, trade sign (whether trades hit the bid or lift the ask), queue sizes, cancellation rates, the time since the last trade. Cross-sectional features compare an instrument with its peers: return relative to its sector, rank of momentum among all stocks, residual after removing the market factor. Cross-asset features use related markets: index futures lead individual stocks by milliseconds; bond yields move bank stocks. Fundamental and alternative data serve longer horizons: earnings, analyst revisions, and less traditional sources such as news text, satellite images or card transactions.

The common thread is relative information. A raw volume of 2 million shares means nothing; volume twice its 20-day average means something. A return of 1 % is large for a utility stock and small for a biotech; the same return divided by the stock's volatility is comparable across both.

5.3Making features stationary and comparable

Three transformations do most of the work. The rolling z-score subtracts a rolling mean and divides by a rolling standard deviation, computed only from the past window: it turns a level into "how unusual is this now". Cross-sectional ranks replace each stock's value by its rank among all stocks on the same day, which removes outliers and market-wide shifts in one step. Volatility scaling divides returns by a current volatility estimate so that calm and turbulent periods look alike to the model.

A subtler tool is fractional differentiation. Taking returns (a first difference) makes a price series stationary but throws away its memory: the level information that a slow trend or a valuation signal needs. Differencing with a non-integer order between 0 and 1 removes just enough of the trend to pass a stationarity test while keeping as much memory as possible. It is a niche technique, but it illustrates the trade-off well: every transformation that makes a feature better behaved also removes some information.

Pitfall

Normalising with statistics computed on the whole dataset (scikit-learn's StandardScaler fitted on all rows, for example) leaks the future mean and variance into every past sample. In vision this is a minor sin; in finance, where the signal is a few percent, it can create the entire edge. Fit every scaler on the training window only, or use rolling statistics.

5.4Labels: fixed horizon, triple barrier and meta-labelling

The label defines what the model learns, and the simplest choice is often wrong. A fixed-horizon label, the sign of the return over the next h bars, ignores the path: a trade that would have hit its stop-loss on the way and been closed counts as a win if the price recovers by the horizon. It also ignores that the same 0.5 % move is large on a quiet day and noise on a volatile one.

The triple-barrier method (Figure 5.2) labels each potential trade the way a trader would close it: an upper barrier for taking profit, a lower barrier for stopping the loss, both set as multiples of current volatility, and a vertical barrier for the maximum holding time. The label is +1, -1 or 0 depending on which barrier is touched first. Labels then match how the strategy actually exits, and they mean the same in calm and wild markets.

Meta-labelling splits the problem in two. A primary model, often a simple rule (a pairs z-score crossing 2, a trend signal), decides the side of a trade. A secondary model predicts whether the primary signal will be profitable this time, and its output sets the position size, including zero. The secondary model sees features the primary rule ignores (volatility, time of day, recent performance of the signal) and turns a high-recall, low-precision rule into a strategy that skips its worst trades. This structure is popular because it keeps an interpretable rule at the core and uses machine learning where it is strongest: filtering and sizing.

5.5Models: linear first, trees next, deep networks last

With a correlation of a few percent between features and target, the main enemy is variance, not bias: a flexible model fits the noise long before it finds the signal. That makes the usual ranking of model families almost the reverse of computer vision. Linear models with regularisation (ridge, lasso; Classical ML 3.2) are the workhorse, especially at high frequency, where they also have the advantage of evaluating in nanoseconds. Gradient-boosted trees (Classical ML 4.3) are the default for mid-frequency tabular problems with many heterogeneous features and some interactions; they need heavy regularisation (shallow trees, strong shrinkage, many samples per leaf). Deep networks earn their place where the input really is high-dimensional and structured: raw order-book sequences (DeepLOB, Zhang, Zohren and Roberts 2019, combines convolutions over price levels with an LSTM), news text, or large cross-sections with learned embeddings.

Whatever the model, the training set is small in the sense that matters. Ten years of daily data are 2,500 samples per stock, heavily autocorrelated; even a large cross-section has far fewer independent observations than its row count suggests. Ensembles of simple models, strong priors (for example, constraining a momentum coefficient to be positive), and features designed by people who understand the mechanism usually beat a bigger model.

Talk track

"In finance the signal-to-noise ratio is so low that variance, not bias, is the enemy. I start with regularised linear models, move to boosted trees with strong regularisation if there are real interactions, and use deep models only for raw high-dimensional inputs like order-book sequences or text. And I judge every model by out-of-sample P&L after costs, not by accuracy."

5.6From prediction to position: accuracy is not money

A model's output is not a trade. Between the two sit three decisions. Sizing: how much to hold given the forecast and its uncertainty; a forecast twice as strong should usually mean a larger position, not the same one. Costs: a trade is worth making only if the expected gain exceeds the expected cost of entering and leaving, which for short horizons is the spread plus fees plus impact; the optimal policy therefore trades less often than the forecast changes sign, and holds positions through small reversals. Risk: positions are combined into a portfolio whose exposure to common factors (the market, sectors, interest rates) is controlled, so that the bet is the one the model makes and not an accidental bet on the market.

This is why accuracy and even log-loss are poor guides. A model that is right 55 % of the time on small moves and wrong on the large ones loses money; a model right 48 % of the time with large wins and small losses can make money. The metric that matters is the P&L of the whole pipeline (forecast, sizing, costs) on out-of-sample data, and the metrics that describe it (Sharpe ratio, drawdown, turnover) are in Section 8.1.

Pitfall

A classifier that predicts "up" for every sample in a rising market reaches high accuracy and has no skill at all. Compare every result with the trivial baselines (always long, always the majority class, yesterday's sign) on the same period; in our leakage experiment, the majority class alone scores 0.56.

6Quantitative strategies: mean reversion, momentum and market making

Most systematic strategies are variations of a few ideas: prices that have moved apart come back together (mean reversion), prices that have moved keep moving (momentum), and someone who stands ready to trade both ways earns a fee for it (market making). Each idea exists because of a real economic mechanism, and knowing the mechanism tells you when the strategy will stop working.

6.1Mean reversion and the pairs trade

Mean reversion is a bet that a deviation is temporary. The cleanest version is the pairs trade: two stocks whose prices are driven by the same economics (two oil producers, two share classes of the same company, an ETF and its basket) wander together. If one rises relative to the other without news, the gap is more likely to close than to widen, because arbitrageurs, investors rebalancing and the shared fundamentals all push it back. The strategy sells the expensive one, buys the cheap one, and closes when the gap is back to normal. It earns from the relative move while being largely neutral to the market as a whole.

Formally, the two log prices should be cointegrated: each is non-stationary, but a linear combination, the spread \(s_t = \ln P^B_t - \beta \ln P^A_t\), is stationary. The hedge ratio \(\beta\) comes from a regression of one on the other (Engle-Granger) or from a Kalman filter that lets it drift. The spread is then modelled as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, \(ds = \kappa(\mu - s)\,dt + \sigma\,dW\), which pulls back to its mean \(\mu\) with speed \(\kappa\). Its half-life \(\ln 2 / \kappa\) says how long a typical deviation takes to halve, and sets the natural holding period and the lookback of the z-score.

The rules are simple: compute the z-score of the spread over a rolling window, enter when it passes ±2, exit at 0. Our synthetic pair (experiments/pairs_backtest.py) is built so that the truth is known: B is 1.3 times A plus a spread with a half-life of 8 days for six years, after which the relationship weakens to a half-life of 120 days for the last two years, and each closing price carries a little independent noise. Chapter 7 backtests this strategy, step by step from naive to honest.

Pitfall

Searching thousands of stock pairs for the ones that were cointegrated in the past is a multiple-testing machine: at a 5 % significance level, one in twenty unrelated pairs passes by chance. Pairs chosen for an economic reason (same industry, same supply chain, same underlying) survive out of sample much more often than pairs chosen by statistics alone.

6.2Momentum and trend following

Momentum is the opposite bet, and it works on different horizons. Cross-sectional momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993) buys the stocks that performed best over the past twelve months, usually skipping the most recent month, and sells the worst; it has been found in most stock markets and asset classes. Time-series momentum or trend following (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012) goes long an asset whose own past return is positive and short when negative, across futures on equities, bonds, currencies and commodities; it is the core of the managed-futures industry.

The explanations are behavioural and structural: investors under-react to news and then catch up gradually; large investors spread their trades over days and weeks, which turns their buying into a trend; risk-management rules force selling after losses. The same horizon distinction appears everywhere: at very short horizons (one month, one day, and within the day around the bid-ask bounce) prices tend to reverse; at intermediate horizons (months to a year) they tend to continue; at long horizons (three to five years) they reverse again.

Why it matters

Mean reversion and momentum are not contradictory; they live on different time scales and in different instruments. A strategy designer's first question is therefore "on what horizon, and why would the mechanism hold there?", not "which model fits best?".

Interesting detail

Momentum has a famous failure mode, the momentum crash: after a long market decline, the past losers are the most beaten-down, highest-risk stocks, and when the market rebounds sharply they rise fastest. A short-losers book then loses heavily in weeks, as in spring 2009. The strategy's return distribution has a fat left tail that a Sharpe ratio does not show.

6.3Market making: quoting both sides and managing inventory

A market maker posts a bid and an ask and earns the spread whenever both are filled. If the order flow were perfectly balanced and uninformed, this would be riskless. In reality fills arrive randomly: after three buys in a row against its ask, the maker is short three units and loses if the price rises. And some of the fills come from informed traders (Section 3.5). A market-making strategy is therefore mainly an inventory and adverse-selection controller, and its classic model is Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008).

In their model the maker does not quote around the mid price \(s\) but around a reservation price, the price at which it is indifferent to holding one more unit given its current inventory \(q\): \(r = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T - t)\), where \(\gamma\) is risk aversion, \(\sigma\) volatility and \(T - t\) the time left. A long maker quotes lower: its ask is more attractive, its bid less, so the next fill is more likely to reduce the position. The optimal total spread is \(\gamma\sigma^2(T-t) + \frac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1 + \gamma/k)\), where \(k\) describes how quickly the fill rate drops as a quote moves away from the mid (fills arrive at rate \(A e^{-k\delta}\) for a quote at distance \(\delta\)). Figure 6.2 shows the quotes and the fill rates.

Our simulation (experiments/mm_sim.py) reproduces the paper's setting with 1,000 paths. With inventory-skewed quotes the maker earns 65.0 on average with a standard deviation of 6.5, and ends the day with inventory spread ±3.0. Quoting the same spread symmetrically around the mid earns slightly more on average, 68.3, but with twice the standard deviation, 13.0, and inventory spread ±8.7. The skew buys a halving of risk for a 5 % reduction in mean profit: a very good trade, and the reason every real market maker skews its quotes with inventory.

In practice

Production market makers extend the model in three directions: the fair price is not the mid but a short-term forecast (microprice, imbalance, the futures price), the spread widens when markouts deteriorate or volatility jumps, and inventory limits are hard caps enforced by the risk gate. The hardest part is not the formula but speed: requoting within microseconds when the fair price moves, so that stale quotes are not picked off.

6.4Arbitrage and latency-sensitive strategies

Statistical arbitrage generalises the pairs trade to hundreds of stocks: each stock's return is regressed on common factors (the market, sectors, style factors), and the strategy trades the residuals, expecting them to revert. Index and ETF arbitrage keeps an index future or ETF in line with its constituents: when the basket is cheaper than the ETF, buy the basket and sell the ETF, and vice versa; the creation and redemption mechanism of ETFs anchors the relationship. Latency arbitrage is the purest speed game: when a futures contract in Chicago moves, the related stocks in New Jersey will move a few milliseconds later, and whoever trades first against the stale quotes earns the difference.

Latency arbitrage is controversial because it transfers money from slower liquidity providers to the fastest takers without improving prices much. Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015) argued that the continuous limit order book makes this arms race inevitable and proposed frequent batch auctions as a remedy; IEX added a 350-microsecond delay (38 miles of coiled fibre) to incoming orders so that its own price-protecting logic can update first. The point for an engineer: some strategies' entire edge is a latency advantage of microseconds, and it vanishes the moment a competitor is faster.

6.5Capacity, crowding and alpha decay

Every strategy has a capacity: the amount of capital it can run before its own trading costs, by the square-root law of Section 3.4, eat the edge. HFT strategies often have small capacity (tens of millions) and very high Sharpe ratios; trend-following strategies have large capacity (tens of billions) and modest Sharpe ratios. This is a law of the field, not a coincidence: high-Sharpe opportunities are small, or they would have been arbitraged away.

Successful strategies attract imitators, and crowding creates a new risk: when many funds hold the same positions, an unrelated loss that forces one of them to sell pushes the others' positions down, which forces more selling. The August 2007 "quant quake" was such an event: market-neutral equity strategies lost heavily over a few days while the market barely moved. Over time, a published or widely known signal decays: studies of published anomalies find that returns fall substantially after publication. Alpha decay is not a failure of a particular model; it is the market doing its job (Section 8.4).

7Backtesting honestly

A backtest replays history to estimate how a strategy would have performed. It is the most important tool in quantitative trading and the most dangerous, because every mistake in it points the same way: towards results that look better than reality. This chapter lists the mistakes, measures two of them, and walks the pairs strategy from a naive backtest to an honest one.

7.1What a backtester is: vectorised and event-driven

The simplest backtest is vectorised: compute a position for every bar from the signal, shift it by one bar so that it is held from the next bar on, multiply by returns and subtract a cost per unit traded. It runs in milliseconds over decades and is adequate for daily strategies that trade small sizes and cross the spread. It cannot express passive orders, queue position, partial fills, latency or the strategy's own effect on the book.

An event-driven backtester (Figure 7.1) replays recorded market data event by event, in the order and with the timestamps at which the strategy would have received it, into the same strategy code that runs live. Orders go to a simulated exchange that delays them by a latency model, places passive orders at the back of the queue at their price level, fills them only when the queue ahead of them has traded or cancelled, applies fees and rebates, and returns fills as events. This is the trading equivalent of resimulating recorded drives through the production software: same code, recorded inputs, a model of the parts you cannot record.

Why it matters

Running the production strategy code in the backtester removes a whole class of errors: the backtest and the live system cannot disagree about how a signal is computed, and the backtester doubles as an integration test. Firms that maintain separate research and production implementations spend much of their time explaining why the two disagree.

7.2The leakage zoo

Leakage is any way in which the backtest uses information that the strategy would not have had (Classical ML 9.2 covers the general idea). In finance it has many forms. Look-ahead bias: using the close price to compute a signal and trading at the same close; using a day's high and low before the day is over; normalising with full-sample statistics. Point-in-time violations: joining fundamentals on the period they describe rather than their publication date; using restated values (Section 5.1). Survivorship bias: testing on today's index members only. Corporate actions: splits and dividends handled with back-adjusted prices are fine for returns, but signals based on price levels (a "stock under 5 dollars" filter) then use a price that never existed on that day. Timestamp sloppiness: aligning two feeds by exchange timestamp when your system would have seen one of them later.

A practical test for leakage is the "too good" check. Daily strategies with Sharpe ratios above 3, models with sign accuracy above 55 % on daily returns, or equity curves without visible drawdowns are almost always leaking. The second test is to shift: delay every signal by one more bar and see whether the performance collapses; real signals degrade gradually, leaks fall off a cliff.

7.3Overlapping labels, purging and embargo

Financial labels usually span time: the return over the next 20 days, or the triple-barrier outcome. Two samples a day apart share 19 of their 20 label days and have almost identical features. Shuffled K-fold cross-validation then puts near-copies of each test sample into the training set, and a flexible model simply remembers them. Our experiment (experiments/leakage_cv.py) shows how strong the effect is. The prices are a pure random walk, so there is nothing to predict; the label is the sign of the next 20-day return; the model is a random forest on seven ordinary price features.

Pitfall

The shuffled-K-fold result in the table is not an exotic corner case: it is what scikit-learn's default cross_val_score with shuffle=True produces on any time series with overlapping labels. The model is not overfitting in the usual sense; the validation is measuring memory instead of prediction.

7.4Walk-forward evaluation

Walk-forward evaluation (Figure 7.2, bottom) simulates what would really have happened: at each step, the strategy is fitted and its parameters chosen using only data up to that point, then traded on the next block; the window moves forward and the process repeats. The concatenated test blocks form one out-of-sample track record in which no decision used future data. The training window can be expanding (all history so far) or rolling (the last N years); a rolling window adapts to regime changes and forgets old regimes, an expanding one is more stable. Either way, the walk-forward result is the closest a backtest gets to paper trading.

Walk-forward has a cost: there is only one path through history, so the estimate is noisy, and early test blocks have little training data. Combinatorial purged cross-validation (López de Prado) creates many train/test splits from the same history and reports a distribution of out-of-sample results, which gives a sense of how much of the result is luck.

7.5Simulating fills: latency, queues and costs

For strategies that cross the spread, the fill model is straightforward: pay the ask (or bid) at the time the order would have arrived, plus fees, plus the slippage of walking the book for larger sizes. The important detail is the time: the decision is made when the data arrived, and the order reaches the exchange one latency later, at a price that may have moved. A strategy that is profitable at zero latency and unprofitable at the firm's real latency is not a strategy.

For passive strategies, the fill model is the hardest part of the backtest. A resting order joins the back of the queue at its price; it is filled only after all orders ahead of it have traded or cancelled. An L3 feed allows tracking the queue ahead exactly; with L2 data the position must be estimated, typically by assuming cancellations come uniformly from the queue. The optimistic assumption, "filled whenever the price touches my level", is badly wrong: the fills it adds are exactly the ones that real queues deny you when the price touches and bounces, and it omits the adverse ones where the level is swept and the price continues through. Optimistic passive fill models are the most common reason that market-making backtests look wonderful.

In practice

Firms calibrate their fill models against their own live fills: for orders actually sent, compare the simulated fill (time, price, probability) with what happened, and tune queue and latency assumptions until the simulator reproduces live performance on past days. A simulator that cannot reproduce yesterday's real P&L should not be trusted to predict tomorrow's.

8Overfitting, regime change and realistic evaluation

Even a backtest without leakage can lie, because of the way it was found. Try enough variations and one of them will look excellent by chance. This chapter explains how to measure performance, how to correct for the number of things you tried, and how to get from a backtest to live trading without fooling yourself.

8.1Measuring performance: Sharpe, drawdown and turnover

The Sharpe ratio is the mean excess return divided by its standard deviation, annualised: for daily returns, \(SR = \sqrt{252}\,\bar r / s_r\). It measures return per unit of risk, and it is scale-free: leverage doubles both mean and volatility and leaves the Sharpe unchanged. Rules of thumb: a long-only stock index has had a Sharpe around 0.3 to 0.5 over long periods, a good diversified quant strategy 1 to 2 after costs, an HFT market-making business can show 5 or more because it makes many small, nearly independent profits every day. The Sharpe ratio has an estimation error: its standard error is approximately \(\sqrt{(1 + SR^2/2)/T}\) with \(T\) in years, so five years of data pin down a Sharpe of 1 only to about ±0.5.

Other numbers complete the picture. Maximum drawdown, the largest fall from a peak, is what investors and risk managers actually feel; a strategy with Sharpe 1 will typically see drawdowns of one to two years' expected return. Turnover, how much of the book is traded per day, decides how sensitive the strategy is to cost assumptions. Hit rate and win/loss ratio explain how the money is made. Skewness and tail behaviour reveal strategies that earn small amounts steadily and lose large amounts rarely (selling options, momentum before a crash, carry trades), whose Sharpe ratio flatters them until the tail arrives.

Numbers

Annualise by the square root of the number of periods: daily ×√252 ≈ 15.9, monthly ×√12 ≈ 3.5. This assumes independent returns; for strategies with autocorrelated returns (smoothed prices, illiquid holdings) the naive annualisation overstates the Sharpe ratio, sometimes by a factor of two.

8.2Selection bias: the best of many noise strategies

Suppose you test N strategies that have no skill at all. Each backtest Sharpe ratio is a random number around zero with a standard deviation of about \(1/\sqrt{T}\). The best of them is not around zero: it is the maximum of N random numbers, and its expected value grows like \(\sqrt{2\ln N}/\sqrt{T}\). Our experiment (experiments/overfit.py) shows the effect in its most familiar form. On 200 random walks of ten years each, a researcher tries 144 moving-average crossover rules (fast and slow lengths, trend or contrarian) on the first five years and picks the best. Its in-sample Sharpe ratio averages 1.06, and is above 0.70 in nine runs out of ten. Traded on the next five years, the same rule averages -0.02. The rule was never good; it was the luckiest of 144.

With 100 tries on five years of data, the best zero-skill strategy is expected at a Sharpe of 1.13, and a backtest needs to exceed 1.47 to be significant at 5 % after accounting for the search; a single honest trial would need only 0.74. With 1,000 tries, a backtest Sharpe of 1.5 has a one-in-three chance of being pure noise. The formula behind the figure was checked against simulation: for 10, 100 and 1,000 independent trials on five years it predicts 0.70, 1.13 and 1.46, and the simulation gives 0.70, 1.12 and 1.45.

Pitfall

The number of trials includes everything you looked at, not only the final grid search: the features you tried and dropped, the universes you tested, the periods you excluded because "they were special", and the strategies your colleagues tried on the same data. Researchers rarely count these, so the real N is almost always larger than the reported one.

8.3Deflated Sharpe, PBO and higher hurdles

Several tools correct for selection. The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey and López de Prado, 2014) computes the probability that the observed Sharpe ratio exceeds the best one expected from N trials of noise, also adjusting for the sample length and for non-normal returns (skewness and fat tails widen the noise distribution). The probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) splits the history into many in-sample and out-of-sample combinations and measures how often the strategy that is best in-sample ranks below the median out-of-sample; a PBO above 0.5 means the selection process is picking noise. In academic finance, Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argued that after hundreds of published factors, a new one should need a t-statistic above about 3 rather than 2.

The cheapest correction is procedural: decide the hypothesis and the test before looking at the data, keep a final hold-out period that is touched once, record every trial, and prefer strategies with an economic reason to work. A strategy that is a small family of variations of a sensible idea, all of which work somewhat, is far more credible than the single best cell of a large grid.

Talk track

"A backtest Sharpe ratio has to be judged against how many things were tried. With a hundred variants on five years of data, pure noise delivers a best Sharpe of about 1.1, so a 1.2 means nothing. I keep a log of trials, use the deflated Sharpe ratio or PBO to account for them, and I trust a strategy more when its neighbouring parameter values also work."

8.4Regime change and alpha decay

Markets change their behaviour. Volatility regimes shift (2017 was one of the calmest years in decades, 2020 contained one of the fastest crashes), correlations change (stocks and bonds moved in opposite directions for twenty years, then together in 2022), market structure changes (decimalisation, the rise of HFT, new rules on tick sizes and fees), and competitors arrive. A strategy fitted on one regime may simply stop working in the next, as the pairs trade of Section 7.6 did when the relationship weakened.

Defences come in three kinds. Robustness by design: strategies with an economic mechanism that should hold across regimes, tested over several distinct periods, with performance broken down by volatility regime and year. Adaptation: rolling windows, regime indicators (volatility level, trend strength) as model inputs, models whose parameters are re-estimated regularly. Monitoring: live performance compared with the backtest's expected distribution, with an agreed rule for when a strategy is scaled down or stopped. The last is the most important, because no amount of research predicts the next regime; what you can control is how long you keep trading a broken strategy.

Why it matters

In vision, a model that works on this year's images will usually work on next year's. In trading, a model that works this year is being studied by competitors and traded away. Plan for every strategy to decay, and measure the decay rather than hoping it does not happen.

8.5From backtest to live: paper, shadow and small size

The path to production adds reality one layer at a time. Paper trading runs the strategy on live data and records the orders it would have sent, testing the data pipeline and the timing on data the researcher has never seen. Shadow trading runs it in the production environment alongside the real systems. Small live size sends real orders and measures real fills, latencies and costs; comparing them with the simulator's predictions for the same days validates (or invalidates) the fill model. Only then is capital increased, gradually, watching whether costs grow with size as the impact model predicted.

The gap between backtest and live performance has a name, implementation shortfall at the strategy level, and it is normal for it to be large: live Sharpe ratios around half of the backtest's are common even for careful firms. A team that tracks this gap for each strategy learns where its simulator is optimistic and fixes it, which is the only way backtests get better over time.

9HFT system architecture: one order from packet to match

This chapter follows the first journey: one market-data packet in, one order out, and the exchange's matching engine at the end. Each component exists to remove time from that path, and each step has a typical cost in nanoseconds. The numbers are what makes the architecture make sense.

9.1Why speed matters, and how much

Speed matters for two reasons that follow from price-time priority. A maker needs to cancel or move its quotes before faster takers trade against them when the fair price changes; a quote that is stale for 5 µs is free money for anyone who sees the change within those 5 µs. A taker going after a mispriced quote needs to arrive before everyone else trying to take the same quote; usually only the first one gets it. In both cases the race is relative: being fast in absolute terms is worthless if a competitor is faster.

The races are won by margins of nanoseconds to microseconds. The industry measures tick-to-trade latency, the time from the last bit of a market-data packet arriving at the server's network port to the first bit of the resulting order leaving it, usually with a passive network tap and hardware timestamps so that the measurement itself adds nothing. Competitive software systems are in the low single-digit microseconds, FPGA systems in the tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. The relevant percentile is not only the median: a system that is fast on average but slow in the moments that matter (busy markets, which are exactly when the opportunities are) loses the races that pay.

9.2The path of one order

Figure 9.1 steps through the path in a colocation data centre, the exchange's own building where trading firms rent rack space next to the matching engine. Each step names the component and a typical time; Section 9.8 adds them up.

Someone else's order trades in the matching engine. The exchange's market-data publisher turns the change into a sequenced ITCH-style message, packs it into a UDP packet and multicasts it. Inside the exchange this takes microseconds, the same for every subscriber.

The packet crosses the exchange's distribution network and a cross-connect into our rack switch. Colocation providers equalise cable lengths so that every customer in a given hall is the same distance away; tens of metres of fibre cost about 5 ns per metre. A layer-1 switch adds a few nanoseconds, a conventional low-latency switch a few hundred.

Our network card deserialises the frame, timestamps it in hardware and writes it by DMA into a receive ring in host memory. With kernel bypass, a thread busy-polling that ring sees the new descriptor within tens of nanoseconds; no interrupt, no system call. NIC plus PCIe typically cost a few hundred nanoseconds.

The feed handler checks the sequence number, decodes the binary message and applies it to the order book: an add, an execution or a cancel. In our measurement on 2 million synthetic messages this takes 9 ns to parse and about 25 ns in total per message with a flat-array book.

The strategy sees the updated book, recomputes its signal (imbalance, microprice, a linear model over a few dozen features) and decides whether to quote, cancel or take. Simple decisions take tens of nanoseconds, model evaluation hundreds.

The pre-trade risk gate checks quantity, price collar, position, message rate and duplicate orders in tens of nanoseconds. The order gateway writes the order into a pre-built message template, filling only the fields that change (price, quantity, sequence, checksum).

The gateway hands the bytes to the NIC, often by writing them straight into the card's memory rather than letting the card fetch them by DMA, saving a PCIe round trip. The frame leaves the port and travels back through the switch to the exchange.

The exchange's order gateway terminates the TCP session, validates the order and applies its own risk checks, then forwards it to the matching engine, which sequences it with everyone else's orders and matches it (Section 2.3). Exchange order-entry latencies are typically several to tens of microseconds, and they are the same for every participant.

Why it matters

The exchange's own latency is large compared with ours, but it is the same for everyone, so it does not decide the race. What decides it is the part that differs between firms: the wire, the NIC, and the software in between. That is why firms optimise a 1 µs path while the round trip to the matching engine takes tens of microseconds.

9.3Colocation, the wire and geography

Inside one data centre, the speed of light is the floor: light in optical fibre travels at about two thirds of its vacuum speed, roughly 5 µs per kilometre or 5 ns per metre. Between data centres, the floor becomes geography. The classic route is between the futures exchange's data centre near Chicago and the equity exchanges' data centres in northern New Jersey, about 1,200 km apart. A straight fibre route takes roughly 13 ms for a round trip; microwave and millimetre-wave radio links, which travel through air at almost the vacuum speed of light and along straighter paths, bring it to around 8 ms, within a few percent of the physical limit. Firms have built tower networks, and later shortwave radio across oceans, for these few milliseconds, because the futures market usually moves first and the stocks follow.

Inside the rack, the network is engineered for latency rather than throughput. Layer-1 switches copy bits from one port to others electrically or optically, without parsing the frame, in a few nanoseconds; they are ideal for fanning out market data. Cut-through switches start forwarding a frame after reading its header, costing hundreds of nanoseconds instead of the microseconds a store-and-forward switch needs for a full frame. Link speed matters too: a 64-byte frame takes 67 ns on the wire at 10 Gbit/s including preamble and gap, 27 ns at 25 Gbit/s.

Numbers

Fibre: about 5 µs per km. Radio through air: about 3.3 µs per km. Chicago to New Jersey: around 13 ms round trip over fibre, around 8 ms over microwave. A 64-byte frame at 10 GbE: 67 ns. Layer-1 switch: a few ns. Cut-through switch: a few hundred ns.

9.4The NIC and kernel bypass

The normal Linux network path is designed for throughput and fairness, not for microseconds (Figure 9.2). A packet raises an interrupt, the driver and the NAPI softirq process it, the IP and UDP layers deliver it into a socket buffer, the scheduler wakes the waiting thread, and the recv() system call copies it into user memory. Our measurement on loopback, which skips the NIC entirely, puts the one-way cost of this path at 14 µs at the median with a blocking receive, and 2.1 µs with busy polling in a non-blocking loop (p99 53 µs and 7.8 µs). On bare-metal servers with tuned kernels the numbers are lower, but the structure is the same: interrupts, copies and wakeups cost microseconds and add jitter.

Kernel bypass removes the kernel from the data path. The NIC's receive and transmit rings are mapped into the application's address space, and a thread pinned to a dedicated core polls them in a tight loop. Vendors provide several layers: AMD Solarflare's Onload accelerates ordinary socket calls transparently, while their ef_vi and TCPDirect APIs give raw access to the rings; DPDK is the vendor-neutral framework; NVIDIA (Mellanox) offers VMA and raw verbs. The price is a core spent at 100 % even when nothing happens, and a loss of the kernel's conveniences: no tcpdump on that interface, the application must handle its own TCP for order sessions (or use the vendor's user-space stack), and debugging is harder. The general mechanism is described in Networking 5.4.

In practice

Low-latency NICs also timestamp every packet in hardware, at the moment the frame arrives at the port, with clocks disciplined by PTP. Those timestamps are the basis of every latency measurement, of the order of events across feeds, and of regulatory record keeping (Section 10.5).

9.5Feed handler and book builder

The feed handler is the first piece of software on the path, and it must be both fast and paranoid. Fast, because every message passes through it, including the 90 % or more that are adds and cancels far from the touch; paranoid, because a single lost or misapplied message corrupts the book. It performs A/B arbitration and gap detection (Section 2.4), decodes the binary messages (byte-swapping big-endian fields, scaling prices, mapping symbol locate codes to internal instrument ids) and applies each to the book. On a busy day a single US equity feed carries billions of messages; bursts at the open and around news reach millions per second, and the handler must keep up with the burst, not only the average.

The book builder keeps, per instrument, the order-by-order state needed to process executes and cancels (which arrive with only an order id) and the aggregated per-level view the strategy uses. Most firms build only the books for instruments they trade, filter everything else as early as possible (sometimes in the NIC or an FPGA), and hand the strategy not the whole book but the few numbers it needs: best prices, sizes, a handful of levels, derived signals.

9.6Strategy, risk gate and order gateway

The strategy component is usually small on the hot path. It updates a handful of features incrementally (an imbalance is two numbers; an exponentially weighted moving average is one multiply-add), evaluates a linear model or a lookup table, and compares the result with thresholds. Heavier work, such as re-fitting a model, recomputing a fair-value curve or optimising a portfolio, runs on other threads or other machines and hands parameters to the hot path through lock-free structures. The hot path then reads only precomputed values.

The risk gate sits between strategy and network and is not optional: regulations in the US (SEC Rule 15c3-5, the market access rule) and the EU (MiFID II, RTS 6) require pre-trade controls on every order. Typical checks are maximum order size and notional, a price collar around a reference price, position and exposure limits, message and order rate limits, duplicate-order detection and self-trade prevention. They are simple comparisons, and in our pipeline cost tens of nanoseconds; the design rule is that the gate is independent of the strategy, so that a bug in one is caught by the other. The order gateway keeps the exchange sessions (logon, heartbeats, sequence numbers), encodes orders into pre-built templates, and processes acknowledgements and fills, which update the strategy's position and the risk gate's limits.

9.7FPGAs and hardware

When software reaches about a microsecond, the remaining time is mostly the NIC and PCIe. The next step is to move the decision onto the network card itself. An FPGA (field-programmable gate array) on the NIC can parse the market-data packet as its bytes arrive, update a small book, evaluate a simple trigger, and start sending a pre-built order before the incoming packet has even been fully received. Tick-to-trade times are in the tens to low hundreds of nanoseconds. The software on the host does not disappear: it computes the parameters (fair price, thresholds, sizes, which orders to arm) and writes them into the FPGA, and it handles everything that is not time-critical.

FPGA development is hardware development: logic written in Verilog, VHDL or high-level synthesis tools, timing closure, long build times, and the discipline that every feature costs area and latency. Firms use FPGAs for the narrow, extremely latency-sensitive parts (feed filtering, triggered orders, risk checks) and keep the intelligence in software. Some go further to custom chips, which trade even more flexibility for speed.

Talk track

"Tick-to-trade is wire-in to wire-out at our server. Through the kernel it is tens of microseconds, dominated by interrupts, copies, wakeups and thread hand-offs. With kernel bypass and a single busy-polling thread it is around a microsecond, where the NIC and PCIe cost more than all our parsing, book and strategy code. Below that you move the trigger onto an FPGA at around a hundred nanoseconds and keep software for setting its parameters."

10Low-latency C++ in practice

The hot path of a trading system is ordinary C++ written under unusual rules: nothing may allocate, block, wait for the kernel or touch memory that is not already in cache. This chapter turns those rules into measured numbers from our own pipeline benchmark, and covers the measurement and clock discipline that keeps them honest. General C++ and lock-free fundamentals are in their own chapters; this one is about how trading applies them.

10.1The rules of the hot path

The hot path is the code between a packet arriving and an order leaving. Everything on it is judged by its worst case in a busy market, and a few rules follow. No dynamic allocation: memory is reserved before the market opens, from pools and pre-sized arrays, because malloc can take microseconds when it needs to fetch pages from the operating system. No system calls and no locks: anything that can enter the kernel or sleep (I/O, mutexes, condition variables, even some clock functions) moves off the hot path. No exceptions or virtual dispatch on the hot path where it matters: not because they are slow when nothing happens, but because they make the cost and the code layout harder to predict; templates and compile-time configuration (C++ chapter, performance) replace runtime polymorphism. Warm caches: if the market is quiet for a second, the code and data of the hot path may be evicted, and the next packet pays dozens of cache misses; systems run the path continuously on synthetic or real data and discard the result ("cache warming") so that the real event finds everything hot.

The rules apply to the logging and monitoring too, which is where they are most often broken. A snprintf and a std::string in the order path look harmless; Section 10.2 measures what they cost.

Why it matters

In most software, the average case is what users feel. On a trading hot path, the cases that matter are the busy ones, where caches are under pressure, queues are full and the opportunities are largest. Code that is fast "usually" is slow exactly when it should be fast.

10.2The order book data structure, measured

The textbook order book is a balanced tree of price levels (std::map), each level holding a linked list of orders, plus a hash map from order id to the order's position. It is correct and general, and every operation chases pointers through separately allocated nodes. The low-latency version uses the fact that prices live on a grid: an array of levels indexed by price in ticks relative to a base price, an index of the best level that is updated incrementally, and a vector of orders indexed directly by order id (exchanges assign ids densely enough to make this work, or a fast open-addressing hash map stands in) (Figure 10.1).

struct FlatBook {                                   // one instrument, one side shown
  static constexpr int N = 8192;                    // price range in ticks, sized before the open
  std::vector<int64_t> bid_qty = std::vector<int64_t>(N);
  std::vector<Order>   orders;                      // indexed by exchange order id
  int best_bid = -1;

  void add(uint64_t id, int px_idx, uint32_t qty) {
    orders[id] = {px_idx, qty};
    bid_qty[px_idx] += qty;
    if (px_idx > best_bid) best_bid = px_idx;       // new best price
  }
  void reduce(uint64_t id, uint32_t qty) {          // execute or cancel
    Order& o = orders[id];
    o.qty -= qty; bid_qty[o.px_idx] -= qty;
    while (best_bid >= 0 && bid_qty[best_bid] == 0) --best_bid;   // usually 0-2 steps
  }
};

Logging is the most common hot-path regression. 10.3Threads, cores and lock-free pipelines

The fastest design is one thread doing everything from receive to send: no hand-offs at all. When the work does not fit one core, the pipeline is split into stages pinned to dedicated cores and connected by single-producer single-consumer (SPSC) rings (Concurrency 5.2), each stage busy-polling its input (Figure 10.2). Our measurement of a one-way hop between two pinned threads: 290 ns at the median through an SPSC ring with busy polling (p99 0.47 µs), against 13.1 µs through a mutex-and-condition-variable queue where the consumer sleeps (p99 60 µs). The VM inflates the SPSC number; on bare metal a hop between cores on the same socket is closer to 50 to 100 ns, the cost of moving one cache line between cores. The sleeping queue's cost is the operating system's wakeup, and no hardware fixes it.

The supporting configuration is part of the design. Core isolation (isolcpus, nohz_full, interrupt affinity) keeps the scheduler, timer ticks and device interrupts off the hot cores. NUMA locality places the hot threads and their memory on the socket whose PCIe lanes host the NIC, because crossing the inter-socket link costs about 100 ns per access. Huge pages (2 MB or 1 GB) cut TLB misses for the book and the rings. Power management is disabled (no deep C-states, fixed frequency), because waking a core from a sleep state takes microseconds. False sharing (Concurrency 5.4) is avoided by aligning the producer and consumer indices of each ring to separate cache lines, as in the ring we measured.

10.4Measuring latency: timestamps, percentiles and tails

Latency work starts with measurement, and measurement in nanoseconds has its own traps. Inside the process, the CPU's time-stamp counter (rdtsc, invariant on modern x86) is the cheapest clock: our measured cost of a fenced read pair is 33 ns, against 70 ns for clock_gettime through the vDSO. Both are too expensive to call many times per message, so systems timestamp a few points (arrival, decision, send) and compute the stages offline. Outside the process, the reference is the NIC's hardware timestamps and, best of all, a passive tap on the wire with a capture card that timestamps packets on both sides of the server: that measures true tick-to-trade without trusting any software clock.

Results are reported as distributions: median, p99, p99.9, maximum, and a histogram. A mean hides the tails and a maximum is dominated by one-off events, so the percentiles are the working numbers. Tails have physical causes that must be hunted individually: an interrupt on the hot core, a page fault on first touch of a buffer, a cache miss after an idle period, a branch the predictor got wrong for a rare message type, a log ring that was full, a frequency change. The tools are those of Linux 9.4 and of the performance chapters: pin, isolate, pre-touch, measure again.

Pitfall

Measuring with too few samples or only in quiet markets underestimates the tail badly. Our flat book's p99.9 is 360 ns but its maximum over 2 million messages was 181 µs, a single preemption by the virtual machine's host. Production systems record every timestamp, and the tail statistics come from busy periods, not from the average day.

10.5Clock synchronisation and regulatory timestamps

Timestamps from different machines can be compared only if their clocks agree. Trading firms run the Precision Time Protocol (IEEE 1588, Calibration and fusion 4.4) from GPS-disciplined grandmaster clocks, with hardware timestamping in NICs and PTP-aware switches, to keep server clocks within tens to hundreds of nanoseconds of each other and of UTC. The automotive analogy is close: gPTP aligns sensor clocks for fusion; here PTP aligns the clocks that decide which of two events came first.

In finance timestamps are also legal records. In the EU, MiFID II (RTS 25) requires firms using high-frequency trading techniques to keep business clocks within 100 µs of UTC and to record events with a granularity of 1 µs or better, with documented traceability. Regulators use these timestamps to reconstruct the sequence of events across firms after an incident. A firm that cannot prove its clocks were in sync cannot prove what it did.

10.6Determinism, replay and testing

A trading system that receives the same sequence of messages should take the same decisions. This determinism is what makes the event-driven backtester (Section 7.1) and post-incident analysis possible: record every input message with its hardware timestamp, replay it into the same binary, and reproduce the decision. It requires discipline: no dependence on wall-clock time except through the recorded timestamps, no uninitialised memory, deterministic iteration order (no hash maps whose order depends on addresses), and the same floating-point code path in research and production.

Testing then has three layers. Unit tests for parsers and the book against exchange specification examples. Replay tests of whole days of recorded data, compared against the exchange's own end-of-day state and against the previous release's decisions; any difference must be explained. Exchange certification and test environments for order entry, where the exchange checks that the firm's gateway handles rejects, disconnects and recovery correctly. Releases are staged, with the old version ready to come back; the most expensive failure in the industry's history was a deployment.

Interesting detail

On 1 August 2012, Knight Capital deployed new order-routing code to seven of its eight servers; the eighth still contained an old, unused function that a reused configuration flag reactivated. In 45 minutes that server sent millions of orders and accumulated positions worth billions of dollars, and the firm lost about 440 million dollars. The regulator's findings centred on the absence of adequate pre-trade controls and deployment procedures, not on the code itself.

11Execution, transaction costs and risk

A strategy's forecast is turned into profit only after the trades are executed at some cost and the positions survive the risks they carry. Execution and risk management are where most of the money that backtests promise is lost, and where good firms earn back the most.

11.1The anatomy of trading cost

The standard way to measure what trading cost is implementation shortfall (Perold, 1988): the difference between a paper portfolio that traded everything instantly at the price when the decision was made, and the real portfolio. It decomposes into four parts (Figure 11.1). The delay cost is the price movement between decision and the moment the order reaches the market. The execution cost is the difference between that arrival price and the average fill price: spread plus market impact. Fees are explicit. The opportunity cost is what was lost on the part of the order that was never filled, because the price ran away.

The decomposition shows a real trade-off. Trading faster reduces delay and opportunity cost but increases impact; trading slower does the opposite. The optimal speed depends on how urgent the forecast is: a short-lived signal must be traded quickly even at high impact, a slow valuation signal can be traded over days. Every execution algorithm is an answer to this trade-off.

11.2Execution algorithms and the Almgren-Chriss trade-off

Brokers and trading firms offer a few standard algorithms. TWAP (time-weighted average price) slices the order evenly over a time window. VWAP (volume-weighted average price) slices it in proportion to the expected volume curve of the day, trading more at the open and close when liquidity is high, and is judged against the day's VWAP. POV (percentage of volume) participates at a fixed share of the observed volume, adapting automatically to how busy the market is. Implementation shortfall algorithms minimise the expected cost against the arrival price plus a penalty for risk, which leads them to trade faster early on.

The theory behind the last one is Almgren and Chriss (2000). With a temporary impact proportional to the trading rate and price risk proportional to the remaining position, the optimal schedule for selling \(X\) shares over time \(T\) is \(x(t) = X \sinh(\kappa(T-t)) / \sinh(\kappa T)\), where \(\kappa = \sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\) combines the risk aversion \(\lambda\), the volatility \(\sigma\) and the impact coefficient \(\eta\). For \(\kappa \to 0\) this is TWAP; the larger \(\kappa\), the more front-loaded the schedule (Figure 11.2). The model's lesson is general: the right execution schedule is set by the ratio of price risk to impact cost.

Below the schedule sits the order placement layer: for each child order, decide whether to post passively (cheaper, may not fill, exposed to adverse selection) or cross the spread (certain, costs the half-spread), at which price, and on which venue. This is where the microstructure of Chapters 2 and 3 meets the execution problem, and where short-horizon signals such as imbalance pay off for an execution desk: a buy order should be passive when the book is leaning down and aggressive when it is leaning up.

11.3Smart order routing and venue choice

With liquidity spread over many venues (Section 2.5), a smart order router decides where each child order goes. It considers displayed prices and sizes on each venue, the fees and rebates, the probability of hidden liquidity in dark pools, the historical fill rates and markouts per venue, and the latency to each venue. To take liquidity across several venues at once, the router must time the orders so that they arrive simultaneously; otherwise the first fill on the nearest venue warns fast participants, who cancel their quotes on the farther ones before the rest arrive. Routers therefore delay the orders to near venues by the latency difference to far ones.

11.4Pre-trade risk checks and kill switches

Risk control is layered, from nanoseconds to days (Figure 11.3). Inside the strategy, limits on quote sizes and inventory and a rule to stop quoting on stale or gapped data. In the trading process, the pre-trade risk gate of Section 9.6. At the broker or market-access level, credit and capital limits required by regulation. At the exchange, price bands that reject orders far from the last price, limit-up/limit-down mechanisms and market-wide circuit breakers that pause trading after extreme moves, and cancel-on-disconnect, which removes a firm's orders if its session drops. After the trade, monitoring compares P&L, positions, fills and message rates with expectations, and can trigger the kill switch.

A kill switch is one action that cancels all resting orders and blocks new ones, triggered by a person or automatically (loss limit, message-rate spike, position breach, lost market data). It must work when everything else is broken, which means it should not depend on the strategy process being healthy, and it must be tested regularly in production conditions. The automotive analogy is a safety monitor with a minimal-risk condition: independent of the function it supervises, simple enough to be trusted, and exercised rather than assumed.

// pre-trade gate: plain comparisons, independent of the strategy that produced the order
bool RiskGate::check(const Order& o, int64_t now_ns) {
  if (o.qty == 0 || o.qty > max_qty)                    return reject(TooLarge);
  if (o.qty * o.px > max_notional)                      return reject(Notional);
  if (std::abs(int64_t(o.px) - ref_px) > collar)        return reject(PriceCollar);
  if (std::abs(position + o.signed_qty()) > max_pos)    return reject(Position);
  if (!rate.allow(now_ns))                              return reject(Rate);
  if (killed.load(std::memory_order_relaxed))           return reject(KillSwitch);
  return true;
}

11.5Portfolio risk: VaR, expected shortfall and position sizing

At the portfolio level, risk is the distribution of possible losses. Value at risk (VaR) is a quantile of that distribution: a one-day 99 % VaR of 1 million means that on 99 days out of 100 the loss should be smaller than 1 million. It says nothing about how bad the other day is, which is what expected shortfall (ES, the average loss beyond the VaR) measures; bank regulation moved from VaR to ES for that reason. Both depend on a model of the return distribution, and Section 4.2 showed why a normal model understates tails: risk systems use historical simulation, fat-tailed distributions and stress scenarios (2008, March 2020, a sudden move in rates) alongside the model.

Position sizing turns forecasts and risk into amounts. Volatility targeting scales positions inversely to current volatility so that each position contributes a similar risk, which also reduces exposure automatically when markets become turbulent. The Kelly criterion gives the growth-optimal fraction of capital to bet, \(f^* = \mu / \sigma^2\) for a continuous bet with expected excess return \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\); because \(\mu\) is estimated with large error and over-betting is far more damaging than under-betting, practitioners use a fraction of Kelly (often half or less). Drawdown limits reduce or stop a strategy after a defined loss, which protects against the regime changes of Section 8.4 at the cost of sometimes stopping a strategy at its worst point.

Pitfall

Correlations rise in crises. Strategies and assets that were diversifying in normal times fall together when everyone deleverages at once, so a portfolio's risk measured in calm periods understates its risk in exactly the periods that matter. Stress tests with crisis correlations are a necessary complement to any model-based number.

11.6Measuring execution quality

Transaction cost analysis (TCA) closes the loop: for every order, compare the fills with benchmarks (arrival price, interval VWAP, close), decompose the shortfall as in Figure 11.1, and aggregate by algorithm, venue, broker, order size, time of day and volatility. For passive fills, add markouts (Section 3.5) to see adverse selection. The results feed back into the impact model used by the backtester and the portfolio optimiser, into the router's venue preferences and into the choice of algorithm. A firm that measures its costs carefully can often improve a strategy's net performance more by reducing costs than by improving its forecasts, because the cost reduction applies to every trade with certainty, while forecast improvements are uncertain and decay.

Talk track

"I measure execution with implementation shortfall against the decision price, split into delay, spread and impact, fees and opportunity cost, and for passive fills I look at markouts. The trade-off is urgency against impact: Almgren-Chriss formalises it, VWAP and POV are practical answers, and TCA tells me whether the impact model my backtests assume matches what I actually pay."

QSelf-check questions

Q.1Core questions

Explain how a limit order book and price-time priority work.Basic

The book holds resting limit orders for one instrument, bids sorted from highest price down and asks from lowest up. An incoming order that crosses the best opposite price trades immediately against the best price first and, within a price, against the oldest order first, at the resting orders' prices; any remainder rests at its limit price at the back of the queue for that price. Cancels remove orders; reducing quantity usually keeps queue position, changing price loses it. The engine is single-threaded per instrument so that every event has one total order.

Why is there a bid-ask spread?Basic

The market maker provides immediacy and must be paid for order-processing costs, for the risk of holding unwanted inventory, and for adverse selection: some counterparties know more and trade only when the price is about to move against the maker. In Glosten-Milgrom the spread is set so that profits from uninformed flow cover losses to informed flow, so more informed flow means wider spreads, as before news.

What is the difference between slippage and market impact?Basic

Slippage is the difference between the expected price (mid, arrival or decision price) and the average fill price of an order, including the half-spread and walking the visible book. Market impact is the price change caused by trading, including the reaction of other participants over time; it has a temporary part that decays and a permanent part that stays. Empirically the total impact of a large order scales roughly with the square root of its size relative to daily volume.

What are the stylised facts of asset returns?Basic

Returns are nearly uncorrelated (no linear predictability), heavy-tailed (kurtosis well above 3; monthly S&P 500 since 1950 shows 7.8), and volatility clusters (absolute returns are positively autocorrelated for long lags). Also, correlations rise in crises, and there is an asymmetry: volatility rises more after falls than after rises. Practical consequences: normal-distribution risk models understate tails, volatility is forecastable, direction is not.

Why use log returns rather than prices as model inputs?Basic

Prices are non-stationary: their level wanders and statistics from one period do not describe another. Returns are close to stationary. Log returns add over time, map positive prices to the whole real line, and equal simple returns for small moves. Features are further normalised (rolling z-scores, ranks, volatility scaling) so that different instruments and periods are comparable.

What is data leakage in a backtest? Give three examples.Intermediate

Using information the strategy would not have had at decision time. Examples: computing a signal from the close and trading at that same close; joining earnings data on the quarter end instead of the publication date, or using restated numbers; testing only on today's index members (survivorship); normalising features with full-sample statistics; shuffled K-fold cross-validation with overlapping labels. All bias results upwards.

Why does shuffled K-fold cross-validation fail on financial time series, and what replaces it?Intermediate

Labels such as 20-day forward returns overlap, and features are autocorrelated, so a test sample's near-duplicates sit in the training folds and a flexible model memorises them. In my experiment on a pure random walk, shuffled 5-fold gave 69 % accuracy. Replacements: contiguous folds with purging (drop training samples whose label window overlaps the test block) and an embargo after the test block, or walk-forward evaluation; both gave about 50 %, the truth.

Walk me through making a backtest honest.Intermediate

Point-in-time data and the full historical universe; signals from data available at decision time; fills one latency later at realistic prices (cross the spread, walk the book, queue model for passive orders); fees and impact; parameters chosen walk-forward or with purged CV; count the number of variants tried and deflate the result; check by delaying signals one more bar, by period and regime, and against trivial baselines. On my synthetic pairs trade these steps moved the Sharpe from 2.92 to 0.14.

How do you correct a backtest Sharpe ratio for the number of strategies tried?Advanced

The best of N zero-skill strategies has an expected Sharpe of about the expected maximum of N standard normals times the Sharpe's standard error, roughly √(2 ln N)/√T. The deflated Sharpe ratio tests whether the observed Sharpe exceeds that benchmark, adjusting for sample length, skewness and kurtosis; PBO estimates how often the in-sample winner is below median out of sample. Practically: log every trial, keep a final hold-out, and prefer families of variants that all work.

Explain a pairs trade and what can go wrong.Intermediate

Two cointegrated prices have a stationary spread s = ln PB − β ln PA. Trade its rolling z-score: short the spread above +2, long below −2, exit near 0; the half-life of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck fit sets the lookback and holding period. Risks: the relationship breaks (merger, divergent fundamentals), so the spread trends instead of reverting; hedge ratio drift; spurious pairs from data mining; costs, since the strategy trades often; crowding.

How does a market maker manage inventory?Intermediate

It skews its quotes: in Avellaneda-Stoikov it quotes around a reservation price r = s − qγσ²(T−t), so a long position lowers both bid and ask, making the next fill more likely to sell. Spread widens with volatility and risk aversion. In my simulation the skew halved the P&L standard deviation (6.5 vs 13.0) for a 5 % lower mean. In practice also hard inventory limits, hedging in correlated instruments, and wider quotes when markouts deteriorate.

What are markouts and what do they tell you?Intermediate

The P&L of each fill marked to the mid price at fixed times after it (1 ms, 1 s, 1 min), averaged. A passive fill starts with the half-spread; if the price then moves against the position, the flow was informed (adverse selection). A steep drop within milliseconds means stale quotes being picked off, which calls for faster cancellation; a slow drift means informed flow, which calls for wider quotes or avoiding that flow.

Walk through tick-to-trade for a software HFT system and where the time goes.Intermediate

Packet arrives at the NIC (hardware timestamp), DMA into a ring, busy-polling thread picks it up (few hundred ns for NIC and PCIe), feed handler checks sequence and parses (about 10 ns), book update (tens of ns), strategy (tens to hundreds of ns), risk gate (tens of ns), encode into template and write to NIC, NIC transmits (few hundred ns). About 1-2 µs total with kernel bypass, dominated by NIC and PCIe. Through kernel sockets it is tens of µs, dominated by interrupts, copies, wakeups and thread hand-offs.

What is kernel bypass and what does it cost you?Intermediate

The NIC's receive and transmit rings are mapped into user space and a pinned thread polls them, so packets reach the application without interrupts, system calls, copies or wakeups (Onload, ef_vi, DPDK, VMA). Costs: a core at 100 % permanently, a user-space TCP stack for order sessions, loss of standard tools (tcpdump, iptables) on that path, vendor lock-in, harder debugging.

Design a low-latency order book.Advanced

Per instrument, an array of price levels indexed by (price − base)/tick, holding aggregate quantity and, if queue position matters, an intrusive FIFO of orders; an incrementally maintained best-price index; orders in a vector indexed by exchange order id (or open addressing). All memory preallocated before the open, no allocation on add, no pointer chasing beyond one level. Handle recentring if prices leave the range. In my benchmark this was 24 ns per message against 178 ns for a std::map plus std::list book, and 10x better at p99.9.

How do threads communicate on the hot path, and why not with a mutex?Intermediate

Through single-producer single-consumer ring buffers with acquire/release indices on separate cache lines, consumers busy-polling on pinned isolated cores. A mutex with a condition variable puts the consumer to sleep, and waking it costs the scheduler's latency: 13 µs median in my measurement versus 0.29 µs for the SPSC hop. Ideally the hot path has no hand-off at all.

How do you measure latency correctly?Intermediate

End to end on the wire with a passive tap and a capture card that hardware-timestamps both directions; inside the process with the TSC at a few points per message. Report percentiles (p50, p99, p99.9, max) and histograms, not means; measure during busy periods; record every event. Know the overhead of the clock (33 ns per rdtsc pair in my test). Hunt tails by cause: interrupts, page faults, cache misses after idle periods, logging.

What pre-trade risk checks would you put in front of an algorithm?Basic

Maximum order quantity and notional, price collar around a reference price, position and exposure limits, order and message rate limits, duplicate-order detection, self-trade prevention, credit limits, and a kill switch check, implemented independently of the strategy. They are required by regulation (SEC 15c3-5, MiFID II RTS 6) and cost tens of nanoseconds.

What is implementation shortfall?Basic

The difference between a paper portfolio that traded at the decision price and the real one. It decomposes into delay cost (decision to arrival), execution cost (arrival to average fill: spread and impact), fees, and opportunity cost on unfilled shares. It is the standard measure for comparing execution algorithms and brokers.

TWAP, VWAP, POV, implementation shortfall: when would you use each?Intermediate

TWAP for simple even slicing when volume patterns are unreliable; VWAP when judged against the day's VWAP or to blend into normal volume; POV to adapt participation to actual market activity and cap footprint; IS algorithms when there is an alpha decay or risk reason to trade early, trading off impact against price risk as in Almgren-Chriss (front-loaded when risk-averse).

Why do simple models often beat deep learning for return prediction?Intermediate

The signal-to-noise ratio is tiny (ICs of a few percent), the data are non-stationary, and the effective number of independent samples is small, so variance dominates and flexible models fit noise. Regularised linear models and shallow boosted trees generalise better, are cheaper to evaluate (important at high frequency) and easier to understand. Deep models help where inputs are raw and high-dimensional: order-book sequences, text.

Why does PTP matter in trading?Basic

To compare timestamps across machines and feeds (which event came first, true tick-to-trade, latency to each venue) and to meet regulatory clock requirements: MiFID II RTS 25 requires HFT firms to stay within 100 µs of UTC with 1 µs granularity. PTP with hardware timestamping in NICs and switches, from GPS-disciplined grandmasters, achieves sub-microsecond agreement.

Q.2Why and what if

Why is queue position valuable, and why is the back of the queue dangerous?Why

At the front you trade whenever there is interest at that price, mostly against small uninformed orders while the price is still fair. At the back you are filled mainly when a large aggressive order sweeps the whole level, which is exactly when the price is about to move through your level, so your fills carry adverse selection. Price-time priority turns speed into queue position.

Why can a model with 51 % hit rate make money and one with 60 % lose it?Why

Profit depends on the size of wins and losses and on costs, not on the count. A 51 % model applied to thousands of independent bets with costs below its edge accumulates profit (fundamental law). A 60 % model may win small moves and lose large ones, trade too often for its costs, or, on daily data, owe its 60 % to leakage.

What if your backtest fills passive orders whenever the price touches your level?What if

It will overstate profits badly. In reality you are behind a queue and are filled only if the level is traded through or the queue ahead cancels; the touches where you would not be filled are the good ones (price bounces), and the fills you do get are disproportionately the ones where the price continues through you. Use a queue-position model and calibrate it against live fills.

Why did the monthly S&P series show strong lag-1 autocorrelation, and what does it teach?Why

The Shiller series is a monthly average of daily prices; averaging a random walk induces a lag-1 autocorrelation of about 0.25 (Working 1960; 0.24 in my simulation). The series measures something you cannot trade at. Always know how a price was produced (average, close, mid, last trade) before believing a pattern in it.

What if you try 1,000 strategy variants and the best has a 5-year Sharpe of 1.5?What if

Pure noise would produce a best Sharpe of about 1.46 on average, and a 1.5 or better about one time in three. The result is not evidence of skill. You would need either many more years, far fewer variants, an out-of-sample confirmation on untouched data, or an economic reason plus robustness across neighbouring parameters.

Why does kernel bypass make the NIC the biggest item in the budget?Why

Bypass removes the microseconds of kernel processing, leaving fixed hardware costs: serialisation, MAC/PHY, and PCIe DMA in both directions, a few hundred nanoseconds each, against tens of nanoseconds for well-written application code. That is what motivates writing orders directly into NIC memory and, further, moving the trigger onto an FPGA on the NIC.

What if a market-data gap is detected on both A and B feeds?What if

The book for the affected instruments can no longer be trusted. The strategy must immediately stop quoting and cancel resting orders there (or at least stop new ones), while the handler recovers via retransmission or snapshot and replays buffered messages. Only once the book is consistent again does trading resume. Continuing on a possibly wrong book risks trading against phantom prices.

Why would a market maker accept lower average profit to skew quotes?Why

Because risk is the binding constraint, not mean profit: halving the P&L standard deviation (13.0 to 6.5 in my simulation) for a 5 % lower mean raises the Sharpe ratio sharply and allows more capital or tighter quotes under the same risk limits. Uncontrolled inventory also exposes the maker to large losses on big moves.

What if your strategy's live Sharpe is half the backtest's?What if

That is common. Decompose the gap: compare simulated and real fills for the same days (latency, queue position, fill rates), compare assumed and real costs (impact at your size, fees), check for leakage or selection bias in the research, and check whether the regime changed. The fill and cost comparisons improve the simulator; the rest decides whether to keep trading.

Why does adding a one-day delay hurt the naive pairs backtest so much?Why

Closing prices contain noise that reverts the next day (closing-print noise, bid-ask bounce). Computing the z-score from a close and trading at the same close captures that reversal, which nobody can trade because the close is only known after it happens. One day of delay removes this false profit: Sharpe 2.92 to 1.16 on my synthetic pair.

What if two strategies on the same data each have Sharpe 1, are they diversifying?What if

Only if their returns are weakly correlated, and only if that remains true in stress. Strategies built on the same features and universe are often highly correlated; crowded strategies across firms become correlated precisely when deleveraging starts (August 2007). Measure correlations, including in drawdowns, and stress-test with crisis correlations.

Why is the exchange's latency less important than yours, even though it is larger?Why

The exchange's gateway and matching latency is the same for all participants and so does not change who arrives first; the race is decided by the parts that differ between firms: distance, network, NIC and software. Jitter in the exchange's latency can matter, which is why firms study its distribution, but a constant offset does not.

What if the hedge ratio of a pair changes slowly over time?What if

A fixed β leaves a trending component in the spread, so the z-score drifts and the strategy accumulates losing positions. Estimate β on a rolling window or with a Kalman filter that treats β as a slowly varying state; check that the residual spread remains stationary; and widen or stop trading when the estimate becomes unstable.

Why log binary records rather than formatted strings on the hot path?Why

Formatting and string allocation cost hundreds of nanoseconds and occasionally microseconds (reallocation, page faults); in my benchmark a log line per order raised the order-path p99 from 215 ns to 6.8 µs and the p99.9 from 0.4 µs to 60 µs. A fixed-size binary record pushed into an SPSC ring costs a few nanoseconds; a logger thread on another core formats it.

What if a strategy's markouts turn sharply negative within the first millisecond?What if

Faster participants are picking off stale quotes: the fair price moved and they traded before the strategy could cancel. Remedies: faster cancellation (latency work, cancel on signals from leading instruments), wider quotes or smaller sizes when the fair price is moving, and avoiding venues or times where this happens. If the drop is over minutes instead, the flow is informed, and the answer is pricing, not speed.
