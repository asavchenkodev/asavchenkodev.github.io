Bird's-eye view and 3D perception

How a set of cameras, and optionally lidar and radar, becomes one metric top-down picture of the world around a vehicle: BEV coordinates and representations, inverse perspective mapping and its assumptions, learned view transforms from Lift-Splat to BEVFormer and sparse queries, temporal fusion and depth reasoning, point-cloud networks and camera–lidar fusion, 3D boxes, occupancy and vector maps, tracking and free space, what changes on an automotive SoC with fisheye surround cameras, and the ways all of it fails.

1The big picture: why perceive the world from above

A camera measures directions, not positions. Every pixel says "something is along this ray", and a perception system that wants to tell a planner "a car is 31 m ahead and 0.4 m left of the lane centre" has to supply the missing distance itself. Bird's-eye-view (BEV) perception is the family of methods that do this inside the network: they take the images of all cameras, and the points of lidar and radar if present, and produce one metric grid around the vehicle in which objects, lanes and free space are represented once, in the frame in which the vehicle moves. This chapter explains how that grid is built, what it costs, and where it breaks.

1.1Why BEV exists

The classic camera pipeline detects objects in each image and then converts every detection into 3D on its own: a 2D box, a depth guess from the box's lower edge or its size, and a transform with the camera's extrinsics into the vehicle frame. A fusion module then merges the per-camera objects. The deep-learning chapter lists the weaknesses of this late fusion: an object that straddles two cameras is detected twice with two different distances, each camera's depth error is fixed before anything can correct it, and every camera needs its own post-processing. There is a deeper problem too. The image is a poor place to reason about space. Size changes with distance, a car 50 m away is 20 pixels wide, a lane that is straight on the road is a curve in a fisheye image, and the planner cannot use any of it until it has been converted to metres.

BEV moves the conversion into the network. Image features from all cameras are transferred into one shared top-down grid, for example 200 × 200 cells of 0.5 m around the vehicle, and every task head works on that grid. Three things become easy that were hard before. Objects seen by two cameras are represented once, in one cell, so the overlap between cameras helps instead of creating duplicates. Other sensors join naturally, because lidar and radar points already live in metric 3D space and can be rasterised into the same grid. And time becomes easy: last frame's grid can be shifted by the vehicle's own motion and stacked with the current one, which gives the network memory and lets it measure velocities.

The price is that the network must now solve the problem that the classic pipeline solved by hand, at every pixel instead of every detection: where along its ray does this pixel's content lie? Everything in Chapters 3 to 6 is an answer to that question. Inverse perspective mapping answers it with an assumption (everything lies on a flat road), Lift-Splat answers it with a predicted depth distribution, and the transformer methods let each BEV cell look up the image features that might belong to it and learn which ones do.

Why it matters

BEV is the representation in which the rest of the driving stack already works: maps, prediction, planning and the occupancy grids of parking functions are all top-down and metric. A perception output in BEV is consumed directly, without a conversion step that hides its own errors. That is why nearly every production multi-camera stack announced since about 2021 describes some form of BEV or occupancy network, and why every perception engineer is expected to understand it.

1.2Anatomy of a BEV network

Almost every BEV network is built from the same five stages, and Figure 1.1 shows them with the tensor shapes of a typical camera-only configuration. The image encoder runs once per camera with shared weights: a backbone such as a ResNet-50 or an EfficientNet followed by a feature pyramid, producing a feature map at 1/16 (sometimes 1/8) of the input resolution. The view transformer is the stage that makes it a BEV network: it maps image features into the grid, and the families differ only here. The temporal fusion stage aligns the BEV of previous frames to the current position of the vehicle and combines them. The BEV encoder is an ordinary 2D convolutional network that runs on the grid as if it were an image, with the difference that its pixels are metres; it repairs the gaps and smears the view transformer leaves behind and gives each cell context from its neighbours. Finally, task heads read the grid: 3D boxes, a semantic map, occupancy, motion.

Two observations help to read every BEV paper. First, the expensive part in compute is usually the image encoder, because it runs on six or more high-resolution images, while the expensive part in engineering is the view transformer, because it contains the operations (scatter, gather, sampling at computed positions) that accelerators handle worst. Second, the camera calibration is an input of the view transformer, not a post-processing parameter: intrinsics and extrinsics decide where each image feature lands in the grid. A network trained on one camera rig therefore carries that rig's geometry in its weights and in its lookup tables, which has consequences for every vehicle variant (Section 6.3) and every calibration error (Section 11.3).

1.3The running examples: a research ring and a production surround rig

Most BEV methods were developed on the nuScenes dataset, and its sensor set shapes the methods more than papers admit. It has six pinhole cameras of 1600 × 900 pixels mounted on the roof, looking front, front-left, front-right, back-left, back-right and back, with horizontal fields of view of about 70° (110° for the back camera), recorded at 12 Hz. Neighbouring cameras overlap by only 10 to 20 degrees. There is also a 32-beam lidar at 20 Hz and five radars, and objects are annotated as 3D boxes twice per second. The chapter uses this research ring as its first running example, scaled as the networks use it: images resized to 704 × 256.

The second running example is a production surround rig of the kind built into most cars with a parking camera system: four fisheye cameras with fields of view around 190°, in the front bumper, the rear and the two side mirrors, mounted between about 0.6 and 1.0 m above the road and pitched down. They overlap heavily, see the ground right next to the car, and see almost nothing above a few metres of height nearby. The same BEV ideas apply, but the geometry, the useful range and the failure modes differ, and Chapter 10 collects what changes. Figure 1.2 shows both rigs from above.

Interesting detail

The nuScenes detection metric matches predictions to ground truth by the distance between box centres on the ground (0.5, 1, 2 and 4 m), not by 3D overlap. That choice made the dataset usable for camera-only methods, whose depth errors at 40 m are often more than a metre and would score almost nothing under a 3D intersection-over-union of 0.7. It is one of the reasons the camera BEV literature grew around nuScenes rather than KITTI or Waymo (Section 8.1).

2BEV coordinates and representations

Before any network can fill a grid, someone has to decide what the grid is: which frame it lives in, how far it reaches, how large a cell is, and what each cell holds. These decisions look like configuration details, and they set the accuracy ceiling, the memory budget and the sort of bugs a team will spend weeks on.

2.1The ego frame and the grid

A BEV grid is a raster laid over the ground plane of a vehicle-fixed frame, the ego frame. In the automotive convention (ISO 8855) its x axis points forward, y to the left and z up, with the origin on the ground below the middle of the rear axle; datasets use their own variants, often the frame of the top-mounted lidar, and KITTI even labels objects in a camera frame with y pointing down. The grid is defined by a range and a resolution per axis. A cell with integer indices \((i, j)\) covers the square whose centre is

\[ x_i = x_{\min} + (i + \tfrac12)\,\Delta, \qquad y_j = y_{\min} + (j + \tfrac12)\,\Delta, \]

and conversely a point \((x, y)\) falls into cell \(i = \lfloor (x - x_{\min})/\Delta \rfloor\), \(j = \lfloor (y - y_{\min})/\Delta \rfloor\). Stored as a tensor, the grid has the shape \(C \times H \times W\), one feature vector of \(C\) channels per cell, exactly like an image, which is why ordinary convolutions run on it. Height is either collapsed into the channels (a 2D BEV), kept as a few discrete layers (a 3D voxel grid of, say, 16 levels of 0.4 m), or represented implicitly by features that the heads decode into a height.

For temporal fusion a second frame is needed. The ego frame moves with the car, so last frame's grid describes the world relative to a position the car has left. A world or odometry frame, fixed to the ground and updated from wheel odometry and the IMU, provides the ego pose at every timestamp, and the relative motion between two poses is what shifts an old grid into the current one (Section 5.4). The fusion chapter describes this transform tree; for BEV it is enough to know that the ego pose must be the pose at the time the images were exposed, not the time the network ran.

Pitfall

The most common BEV bug is a grid that is transposed or mirrored with respect to the labels. A network trained with such a mismatch often still trains, because convolutions do not care which way is forward, and reaches a respectable loss; then the boxes come out mirrored left–right in the vehicle, or the temporal alignment shifts the old grid the wrong way and the velocity head never converges. The cure is cheap: draw the ground-truth boxes and the lidar points into the BEV grid as an image, and project the BEV boxes back into each camera image, before the first long training run.

2.2Range and resolution: a budget

The grid's extent follows from what the function needs to see. A city function at 50 km/h needs about ±50 m: at 14 m/s, three seconds of anticipation is 42 m. A highway function needs 150 m or more ahead, because braking from 130 km/h takes around 90 m, and much less to the sides. A parking function needs ±10 to 15 m, but at fine resolution, because a parking slot boundary or a kerb has to be located to 5 to 10 cm. The cell size follows from the smallest thing that must be separated: 0.5 m cells merge two pedestrians walking side by side, 0.1 m cells resolve a kerb.

The cost is quadratic in the ratio of range to resolution. A grid of 200 × 200 cells with 256 channels in FP16 occupies \(200 \cdot 200 \cdot 256 \cdot 2 = 20.5\) MB, and every layer of the BEV encoder reads and writes tensors of that size. Halving the cell size quadruples the memory and the compute of everything after the view transform. Production systems therefore rarely use one uniform grid: they use a coarse long-range grid for the highway, a fine near-field grid for parking, a grid that is long and narrow (for example 0 to 100 m ahead and ±25 m sideways), or a polar grid whose cells grow with range, which matches the way camera resolution falls with distance.

Numbers

Common grids: LSS 200 × 200 at 0.5 m (±50 m); BEVDet 128 × 128 at 0.8 m (±51.2 m); BEVFormer 200 × 200 at 0.512 m (±51.2 m); the Occ3D occupancy benchmark 200 × 200 × 16 voxels of 0.4 m (±40 m, −1 to 5.4 m high); a parking grid 256 × 256 at 0.1 m (±12.8 m). Memory of a 200 × 200 × 256 FP16 grid: 20.5 MB per frame; keeping four frames of history for temporal fusion: 82 MB.

2.3What a BEV cell can hold

The grid inside the network holds learned features, but what comes out of the heads is one of five representations, and they answer different questions (Figure 2.2). A semantic raster gives each cell a class such as drivable area, lane marking, pedestrian crossing or vehicle; it is easy to train with a per-cell loss and easy to visualise, and it is what early BEV papers predicted. An object list of 3D boxes (position, size, heading, velocity, class) is what tracking, prediction and emergency braking consume; it compresses the scene into a few dozen entries but can only describe things that are box-shaped and belong to a known class. An occupancy grid marks each cell or voxel as occupied, free or unknown, optionally with a class and a motion vector; it describes arbitrary shapes and unknown objects, the debris and the overhanging load, at the price of being large and harder to evaluate. A vectorized map describes lane dividers, road boundaries and crossings as polylines and polygons with a few dozen points each; it is what a planner wants for lane-level decisions and replaces part of a pre-built HD map. Finally, sparse query methods (Section 5.3) skip the dense grid entirely and keep only a set of object queries, each a feature vector with a 3D reference point.

Talk track

"A BEV grid is an image whose pixels are metres on the ground in the ego frame. Its range follows from the speed of the function and its cell size from the smallest thing it must separate, and memory grows with the square of their ratio, so production stacks use several grids or a polar one. Inside the network the cells hold features; out of the heads come boxes for objects, occupancy for anything that blocks the way, and a semantic or vector map for the road."

3Inverse perspective mapping: BEV by assumption

The oldest way to get from a camera image to a top view does not need a network at all. If every point of interest lies on a known flat road, each pixel's ray meets that road at exactly one point, and the image can be warped into a metric map. This is inverse perspective mapping (IPM). It is exact for road markings, wrong for everything that has height, and very sensitive to the pitch of the camera, and understanding these three facts in numbers is the best preparation for understanding why learned view transforms exist.

3.1How it works, with numbers

The mathematics chapter and classic CV 6.2 derive the result: for points on the plane \(Z = 0\), the projection \(\mathbf{u} \simeq K\,[R \mid t]\,(X, Y, 0, 1)^\top\) loses the third column of the rotation and becomes a 3 × 3 homography \(H = K\,[\,r_1\; r_2\; t\,]\) from road coordinates to pixels. Its inverse maps each pixel to a point on the road. Here is the same computation done by hand for one camera, because the steps show where each error comes from.

Take a front camera with 1920 × 1080 pixels, focal length \(f = 1200\) px and principal point at the image centre (960, 540), mounted 1.5 m above the road and pitched down by 5°. Which point of the road does pixel (1300, 700) see?

  1. Pixel to ray. In the camera frame the ray direction is \(K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top = ((1300-960)/1200,\ (700-540)/1200,\ 1) = (0.283,\ 0.133,\ 1)\): a little right and a little down from the optical axis.
  2. Ray to vehicle frame. Rotating by the extrinsics (pitch 5° down, axes swapped to x forward, y left, z up) gives the direction \((0.985,\ -0.283,\ -0.220)\). The ray points forward, right and down.
  3. Intersect with the road. The camera centre is 1.5 m up, so the ray reaches \(z = 0\) after \(s = 1.5 / 0.220 = 6.82\) units of its length. The point is \((0.985,\ -0.283) \times 6.82\) = 6.71 m ahead of the camera and 1.93 m to the right.

The vertical part of this has a simpler form that is worth remembering. A pixel row \(v\) looks at an angle \(\beta = \theta + \arctan\big((v - c_y)/f\big)\) below the horizontal, where \(\theta\) is the camera pitch, and the road point is at range \(d = h / \tan\beta\). For row 700: \(\beta = 5° + 7.6° = 12.6°\) and \(d = 1.5 / \tan 12.6° = 6.71\) m. The horizon, \(\beta = 0\), is at row \(c_y - f \tan\theta = 435\).

3.2The assumptions, and what breaking each one costs

IPM rests on four assumptions: the road is a plane, the camera's height and pitch above that plane are known, the pixel's content lies on the plane, and the calibration is right. Differentiating \(d = h/\tan\beta\) shows how each error grows with range. An error \(\delta\beta\) in the viewing angle, from a wrong pitch or a road that rises, gives

\[ \delta d \approx \frac{d^2}{h}\,\delta\beta, \]

which grows with the square of the range, and at 20 m with \(h = 1.5\) m one degree (0.0175 rad) is already 4.7 m to first order and 6.1 m exactly. A height error \(\delta h\) gives \(\delta d = d\,\delta h / h\), proportional to range. A one-row error in the pixel gives \(\delta d \approx d^2/(f h)\), the same square law scaled by the focal length. The table puts numbers on all of them for the example camera.

The third assumption, that the content lies on the road, fails for everything that matters to a driving function. A point at height \(H\) above the road, at range \(d\) from a camera at height \(h\), is seen along a ray that meets the road at \(d\,h/(h - H)\). The camera geometry chapter shows the resulting smear in a top view. An object in IPM is a streak from its foot point outwards to the edge of the view.

3.3Where IPM still earns its place

For all its fragility, IPM is used every day. The surround-view display warps four fisheye images onto the ground with precomputed lookup tables, and near the car, where ranges are a few metres and the pitch sensitivity is small, the result is metrically good. Parking-slot detection runs on that top view, because slot markings are on the ground and appear in it as undistorted rectangles (camera geometry 8.3). Lane-geometry estimation in front cameras uses the same principle with an online pitch estimate. And several early learned BEV networks applied IPM to feature maps instead of pixels, warping each camera's features onto the ground plane and letting a network clean up what lies above it; it works for road layout and badly for objects.

IPM also remains the right mental model for the camera's view of the near field. A fisheye mounted at 0.8 m and pitched down sees mostly road, and any learned method that runs on such a camera will learn something close to IPM for the ground pixels, because that is what the geometry dictates. What the learned methods add is the ability to put the rest of the pixels, those showing cars, pedestrians and poles, at a depth that is not the road's.

"IPM assumes the world is a flat road and maps each pixel through a homography to the point where its ray meets that road. It is exact for markings and smears everything with height into a streak pointing away from the camera. Its range error grows with the square of distance times the pitch error, so one degree of pitch, which a braking car easily has, costs about six metres at 20 m. That is why we use it near the car and for markings, with online pitch estimation, and use learned depth for objects."

4Lift, splat: learned depth as the view transform

Lift-Splat-Shoot (LSS), published by Philion and Fidler in 2020, turned the view transform into something a network can learn end to end without giving up geometry. Each image feature is "lifted" into 3D along its ray with a predicted distribution over depth, "splatted" into the BEV cells those 3D points fall into, and the resulting grid is used for whatever comes next ("shoot" was a planning head in the original paper). BEVDet, BEVDepth, BEVFusion and most production camera BEV stacks build on this scheme, so it is worth knowing step by step, with the shapes.

4.1Lift: a depth distribution per pixel

IPM gives each pixel one depth, the road's. LSS instead lets the network say, for every position of the feature map, how likely the content is to lie in each of \(D\) depth bins along the ray, for example 59 bins of 1 m from 1 to 60 m. The image encoder's output is split by one more convolution into two parts per feature-map cell: \(D\) depth logits, turned into a probability distribution \(\alpha\) by a softmax, and a context feature vector \(c\) of \(C\) channels describing what is there. The lifted feature for depth bin \(k\) is their product,

\[ f_k = \alpha_k \, c, \qquad k = 1 \ldots D, \]

an outer product that turns one \(C\)-vector per pixel into a \(D \times C\) block: the same "what" placed at every candidate depth, weighted by how likely that depth is. If the network is sure (one \(\alpha_k\) near 1), the feature goes to one point on the ray; if it is unsure, the feature is spread along the ray; if the pixel shows something genuinely at two depths, such as a fence and the car behind it, the distribution can have two peaks. Because the operation is differentiable, the depth distribution is learned from whatever loss the BEV heads produce, without any depth label, although Section 4.4 shows that adding depth labels helps a great deal.

4.2The geometry: where each frustum point lies

Every (camera, depth bin, feature row, feature column) combination is a point in 3D, and its coordinates depend only on the calibration, not on the image content. For a pinhole camera with intrinsics \(K\), rotation \(R\) and translation \(t\) from camera to ego frame, the point of depth bin \(d_k\) at feature-map position \((u, v)\) (converted to input-image pixels) is

\[ P = R\,\big(d_k\,K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top\big) + t, \]

where \(d_k\) is the depth along the optical axis, the camera's z coordinate, as in LSS and BEVDet. The set of all these points for one camera fills a frustum, the pyramid of space the camera sees, sampled on a grid of rays times depths. Training-time augmentations must be folded into the geometry: if an image was resized, cropped or flipped before entering the network, the pixel positions have to be mapped back to the original image before applying \(K^{-1}\) (BEVDet carries this as a "post-rotation" and "post-translation" per image), and if the BEV itself is rotated or flipped for augmentation, the same transform is applied to \(P\) and to the labels. Getting one of these four transforms wrong is the second most common BEV bug after the transposed grid.

Interesting detail

Because the frustum points depend only on calibration and augmentation, at inference time they are constants. For a fixed camera rig, every frustum point's BEV cell can be computed once at start-up and stored as an index table. BEVPoolv2 exploited this and turned the splat into a precomputed gather-and-sum, several times faster than the original implementation and easy to export, which is a large part of why LSS-style networks are the most common choice on automotive SoCs (Section 10.2).

4.3Splat: pooling the frustum into the grid

The splat assigns each frustum point to the BEV cell under it and sums the features of all points that land in the same cell; points outside the grid or outside the height range are dropped. The result is a \(C \times X \times Y\) grid. This is the "voxel pooling" step, and it is a scatter-add: many inputs, computed output positions, summation where they collide. LSS implemented it by sorting the points by cell index and taking a cumulative sum ("the cumsum trick"), BEVFusion wrote a dedicated GPU kernel, and BEVPoolv2 precomputes the sorted order once per calibration. Summing rather than averaging is a deliberate choice: a cell seen by many confident pixels gets a strong feature, a cell seen by none gets zeros, and the BEV encoder learns to read both.

How many points land where is not uniform, and it explains a property of every camera BEV. Running the geometry of the research ring (experiments/lss.py) puts 130 182 of the 249 216 frustum points (52%) inside the 128 × 128 grid and its height range; the rest lie beyond 51 m or far below the road. Near the car, cells receive tens of points; beyond 30 m most cells receive one or two, and 42% of all cells receive none at all, mostly at long range and in the gaps between neighbouring cameras' rays. The far field of a camera BEV is a sparse fan of rays, and it is the BEV encoder's convolutions that fill in between them.

4.4Depth is the bottleneck: BEVDepth and its successors

When the depth distribution is learned only through the detection loss, it turns out to be poor. BEVDepth (2022) measured this: the depth predicted by an LSS-style network trained without depth supervision was far from the lidar depth at most pixels, so much of the detection quality came from the BEV encoder repairing smeared features rather than from the geometry. Its fix was simple and is now standard: project the lidar points of the training data into each image to get a sparse depth label for the feature-map cells that contain a point, and train the depth head directly with a classification loss over the bins. Two further changes made the depth head camera-aware, by feeding the intrinsics and extrinsics into it so that one network can serve cameras with different focal lengths, and added a small refinement network on the frustum. The improvement over an unsupervised depth head was several points of nuScenes detection score, which at the time was larger than the gain from most architecture changes.

The interactive figure shows why depth quality matters so much. A sharp distribution puts the feature into one or two cells; a flat one spreads it over a streak of cells along the ray, where the BEV encoder has to guess which cell was meant, and a wrong peak puts a confident feature in the wrong place. The width of the streak in metres grows with range, because the depth bins are a fixed number of metres wide while the error of a camera's depth estimate grows with distance.

Later work attacked depth from other directions. BEVStereo and SOLOFusion add temporal stereo: the vehicle moves about a metre between frames at city speed, so the previous image is a second view with a baseline, and matching features along the ray between the two gives geometric depth where the motion is not straight towards the object (Section 6.2). Others use the depth distribution of the lidar-free network only near the ground and let height-aware features handle the rest. The common thread is that a camera BEV network is, at its core, a depth estimator with a very particular output format, and most of its errors at range are depth errors.

Pitfall

Depth labels projected from lidar are sparse, measured at a different time from the camera exposure, and taken from a different viewpoint. A lidar point near an object's edge can be visible to the lidar and hidden from the camera, so it labels a foreground pixel with background depth. Using the lidar sweep closest in time, compensating ego motion between lidar and camera, and discarding points whose projections collide with closer points removes most of these false labels; skipping these steps teaches the depth head to blur edges.

Talk track

"Lift-Splat predicts, for every feature pixel, a distribution over depth bins and a context vector, and their outer product places the feature along the pixel's ray. The weak spot is depth: trained only through the detection loss it stays blurry, so BEVDepth supervises it with projected lidar, and temporal stereo adds geometric depth from the vehicle's own motion."

5Asking the images: query-based and temporal BEV

Lift-Splat pushes image features forward into the grid. The other big family reverses the direction: every BEV cell, or every object hypothesis, has a known 3D position, projects that position into the cameras, and pulls the features it finds there. No depth is predicted explicitly; attention learns which of the pulled features belong to the cell. This chapter covers BEVFormer as the dense representative, the sparse methods that skip the grid, and the temporal fusion that both families rely on.

5.1Backward projection: BEVFormer's spatial cross-attention

BEVFormer (2022) starts from a learnable grid of BEV queries, 200 × 200 vectors of 256 channels plus a positional encoding, one per 0.512 m cell. For each query it builds a vertical pillar of reference points above the cell's centre, typically four heights between a few metres below and a few metres above the ego frame's origin, because the cell's content might be a road marking, a car's bumper or a truck's side. Each reference point is projected into every camera with that camera's calibration. Most cameras miss; a point in front of the car lands in the front camera, and near a seam in two. Around each projected position, deformable attention samples the multi-scale image features at a few learned offsets with learned weights, the samples are summed, and the results from all hit cameras are averaged. Six encoder layers repeat this, each refining the query.

The pillar is how this method handles the missing depth. The cell at 31 m ahead collects the features at the four rows where its pillar projects; a cell at 25 m on the same ray collects features from the same image region, slightly lower down. If the image shows a car's bumper, both cells see it, and only the learned attention weights, the multiple heights and context from neighbouring queries and earlier frames let the network decide which cell the bumper belongs to. Section 11.2 shows the failure this leads to when the decision is wrong: duplicates along the ray.

Numbers

BEVFormer-base: 200 × 200 = 40 000 queries of 256 channels, 4 reference heights, a few sampling points per height and feature level, 6 encoder layers, image backbone ResNet-101 with deformable convolutions at 1600 × 900. Per layer that is roughly 40 000 × 4 heights × 1–2 cameras × several points × levels sampling operations, each a bilinear interpolation at a computed position, a gather, which is precisely the operation pattern accelerators like least.

5.3Sparse queries: skipping the grid

A dense grid spends most of its compute on empty road. Sparse methods keep only a set of object queries, a few hundred to about a thousand feature vectors that each stand for one possible object and carry a 3D reference point. DETR3D (2021) projects each query's reference point into the cameras, samples features there, updates the query and its reference point, and repeats; a set-prediction loss with Hungarian matching assigns queries to ground-truth boxes, as in DETR, so no non-maximum suppression is needed. PETR (2022) avoids the projection step altogether: it computes, for each image feature, the 3D coordinates of points along its ray (the same frustum as in LSS), encodes them into a 3D position embedding added to the feature, and then lets object queries attend to all image features with ordinary global attention. The geometry is in the embedding, and the operations are plain matrix multiplications that every accelerator supports, at the price of attention over all image tokens.

The sparse family's temporal versions are among the strongest camera-only detectors. StreamPETR propagates the most confident queries of the last frame into the current one, moved by ego motion and their own predicted velocity, so that each object carries its history in its query; Sparse4D does the same with a sparse set of 3D anchors and a fixed number of sampling points per anchor. Their weakness is the flip side of their strength: there is no dense grid, so occupancy and map heads need a separate dense branch or their own query set.

5.4Temporal fusion: memory, velocity and depth from motion

A single frame cannot measure velocity, cannot see an object hidden behind a van, and has only monocular cues for depth. Adding the past fixes all three. Velocity becomes observable from displacement between frames; a pedestrian seen a second ago and now hidden can persist in the representation; and the same point seen from two positions of the moving vehicle gives a stereo baseline. On nuScenes, single-frame camera methods have velocity errors around 1 m/s or worse, and temporal methods bring this down substantially; the detection score gain from adding history is typically several points.

All temporal methods share one step: the previous representation must be moved into the current ego frame. For a dense grid, the ego motion between the two timestamps, a rotation by \(\Delta\psi\) and a translation \((\Delta x, \Delta y)\) in the ground plane, defines for every current cell the position it had in the old grid, and the old grid is resampled there (Figure 5.2). Static content then lines up; moving objects are displaced by their own motion, which the network learns to use as the velocity signal. BEVDet4D concatenates the aligned previous grid with the current one and lets convolutions find the displacement; BEVFormer's temporal self-attention lets each query sample the aligned previous grid at learned offsets; SOLOFusion keeps a long history of 16 frames for the coarse but wide-baseline view and a short one for fine matching; StreamPETR does it per object query.

In BEVFormer the temporal step is part of every encoder layer: the queries first attend to the aligned previous grid and only then, through the pillar projection of Section 5.1, to the current images (Figure 5.3). The grid that leaves the last layer is both the input of the task heads and the history of the next frame.

The alignment is only as good as the ego pose and the timestamps that go into it. A pose taken at the wrong time, a yaw-rate sign error or a transform applied in the wrong direction shifts every static object by the same mistake and teaches the network that the world moves; the symptom is a velocity head that predicts motion for parked cars. The fusion chapter describes ego-motion compensation at the measurement time, and it applies here unchanged.

Sparse methods like DETR3D, PETR and StreamPETR keep only object queries and skip the grid. 6Multi-camera aggregation and depth reasoning

Whatever the view transform, a camera-only BEV network must decide what to do where cameras overlap, and must find depth somewhere. This chapter looks at where camera depth actually comes from, how accurate it can be, and why the calibration of the rig has become part of the network.

6.1Overlaps and seams

In the research ring, neighbouring cameras overlap by only 10 to 20 degrees, so most of the scene is seen by exactly one camera and an object at a seam is cut in two. In a forward method, the two halves splat into the same cells if the calibration is right, and their features simply add; in a backward method, a query whose reference points hit both cameras averages the two. Either way the object is represented once, which is the main advantage over late fusion. In the surround rig the overlaps are large, and every ground point near the corners of the car is seen by two fisheyes from very different directions. That is useful redundancy, but it also means that any disagreement between the two cameras' calibrations shows up directly as a doubled or smeared object (Section 11.3), the learned equivalent of the stitching seam of the top-view display.

Images of different cameras are also different photometrically: one faces the sun, one a shadow, and their ISPs may expose differently. A network trained with consistent exposure across the rig can mistake a brightness step at the seam for an object edge. Production stacks either harmonise exposure across cameras (camera geometry 7.4) or train with independent photometric augmentation per camera so that the network does not rely on consistency.

6.2Where a camera-only network finds depth

There are four sources of depth for a camera network, and each has a characteristic error law.

The ground contact point. An object standing on the road touches it at a pixel row, and that row gives range through the IPM formula. This is the strongest monocular cue for vehicles and pedestrians, and it inherits IPM's square law: one pixel of row error costs \(d^2/(f h)\), and a degree of pitch or slope costs \(d^2/h\) times the angle. With the research ring's network resolution (focal length about 560 px at 704 × 256) and a camera at 1.5 m, one feature-map row of 16 pixels at 30 m is about 17 m of range; the network has to interpolate within cells, and it does, but the cue is coarse at range.

Size priors. A car is about 1.5 m tall and 1.8 m wide, an adult about 1.7 m. Apparent size gives range \(d = f H / h_{\text{px}}\), with an error proportional to range: 10% uncertainty in the true size gives 10% in range, 3 m at 30 m. This cue does not depend on pitch or slope, which is why networks lean on it at long range, and it fails for classes with large size variation (trucks, children) and for objects cut by the image border.

Temporal stereo. Between two frames at 12 Hz and 50 km/h the camera moves 1.2 m. For a point off to the side of the direction of travel, this is a stereo baseline, and the depth error follows the stereo law \(\delta d = d^2 \delta p / (f b)\) with disparity error \(\delta p\). For an object straight ahead, along the direction of travel, there is almost no parallax: it only grows slightly in the image. Temporal stereo therefore helps most for objects to the side and for slow or parked scenes, and least for the car directly ahead, which is the one that matters most for braking.

Spatial stereo in overlaps. Two cameras that see the same point form a stereo pair with the distance between them as baseline. In the research ring the overlaps are too narrow to matter; in the surround rig they are wide, but the cameras are far apart and look from very different directions, so matching is hard. Learned networks exploit it implicitly at best.

Numbers

Ground-contact range error for one pixel of row error (f = 1200 px, h = 1.5 m): 0.06 m at 10 m, 0.23 m at 20 m, 0.9 m at 40 m. Size prior with 10% size uncertainty: 1 m at 10 m, 4 m at 40 m. Temporal stereo with 1.2 m baseline, f = 560 px and 0.5 px disparity error, for a point off to the side: 0.7 m at 30 m, 1.9 m at 50 m. Typical result on nuScenes: the best camera-only detectors report mean translation errors around half a metre, lidar detectors about half that.

6.3Calibration inside the network

In a classic pipeline, a calibration error moves the output; in a BEV network, it moves the features, before the network has had a chance to reason about them. If the front-left camera's yaw is off by half a degree, its features land 0.26 m beside where the front camera puts the same object at 30 m, and the BEV encoder sees two half-objects. The network can learn to tolerate a range of calibration errors, but only if it saw them during training. Three practices follow.

First, calibration augmentation: during training, perturb each camera's extrinsics by small random rotations and translations while keeping the labels fixed, so that the network learns not to rely on perfect alignment. The perturbation should match the expected real error, a fraction of a degree, not more, because larger noise blurs the geometry the network can learn. Second, camera-aware networks: feed the intrinsics and extrinsics to the depth head or encode them into position embeddings, so that one network can serve vehicle variants whose cameras sit at different heights or have different lenses; without this, a network trained on one rig must be retrained or fine-tuned for each variant. Third, calibration monitoring and online calibration: the extrinsics used at runtime must be the current ones, from end-of-line calibration updated by online estimation as the vehicle ages and its load changes (camera geometry 6.4, calibration and fusion 3.5), and their validity must be checked, because the network cannot tell that its geometry is wrong.

In practice

When a BEV network trained on one vehicle line is deployed to a second line with the cameras 15 cm higher, expect a systematic range error for the ground-contact cue (a height error of 10% gives a range error of 10%) and degraded depth at the seams, even if the new calibration is fed correctly to the geometry. Budget a fine-tuning round with data from the new rig, or train camera-aware from the start with several rigs in the data.

7Lidar, radar and point-cloud networks

A lidar measures what a camera has to guess: the distance along each ray. Its data, an unordered set of 3D points, does not fit the grid-shaped tools of image networks, and a whole family of architectures exists to deal with that. This chapter explains the main ones (PointNet, voxel and pillar encoders, CenterPoint), then how camera and lidar are fused inside a network, and what radar adds to a BEV. The physics of the sensors is left to the sensor chapter; the fusion chapter describes fusion levels from the filter side.

7.1What a point cloud looks like to a network

A spinning lidar returns a list of points, each with x, y, z, an intensity and often a timestamp or ring index. A 32-beam sensor at 20 Hz produces around 30 000 points per sweep, a 128-beam sensor over 200 000. Three properties make this data awkward. It is unordered: the same scene can be listed in any order, so a network must give the same answer for every permutation. It is sparse: almost all of the space around the car is empty. And its density falls steeply with range, because the beams fan out: in a simulated 32-beam sweep of a street scene (experiments/pillars.py, 34 560 rays, 25 001 returns), 73% of the returns lie within 10 m of the sensor, mostly on the road, and only 4% beyond 40 m. A car at 50 m is hit by a handful of points; a pedestrian at 50 m by one or two.

Figure 7.1 shows one real sweep of a 128-beam sensor mounted about 1.9 m above a city street. A spinning lidar fires a fixed grid of beams and angles, so its raw output is also an image in which every pixel carries a measured distance (panels a and b); only after conversion to points, seen from above in panel c, does it become the sparse, uneven set described here, with dense facades, rings on the road and a shadow behind every object.

There are two broad ways to cope. Point-based networks operate on the list directly with operations that do not depend on order. Grid-based networks first quantise the points into voxels or pillars and then use convolutions, sparse ones for 3D voxels and ordinary ones for the 2D BEV. Production systems are almost all grid-based, for the same reason camera BEV networks are: dense 2D convolutions are what accelerators do best.

7.2PointNet: learning on sets

PointNet (2017) is built on one observation: a function of a set that does not depend on order can be written as a shared function applied to every element, followed by a symmetric aggregation. PointNet applies the same small multilayer perceptron to every point independently, lifting its coordinates to, say, 64 or 1024 features, and then takes the element-wise maximum over all points. Max is symmetric, so the result is permutation-invariant; with enough channels, the network can approximate any continuous set function, and each channel learns to respond to a particular spatial pattern, with the maximum picking the point that shows it most. PointNet++ (2017) adds locality, which PointNet lacks: it samples centre points with farthest-point sampling, groups the neighbours within a radius, and runs a small PointNet on each group, hierarchically, like the receptive fields of a CNN.

PointNet is rarely used on its own for driving scenes, because neighbour search and sampling over 100 000 points are expensive and irregular. Its importance today is as a building block: the encoders of VoxelNet and PointPillars are tiny PointNets that summarise the points inside one voxel or pillar.

7.3Voxels and pillars: from points to a grid

VoxelNet (2018) divided space into 3D voxels, encoded the points of each non-empty voxel with a small PointNet, and ran 3D convolutions over the voxel grid. 3D convolutions over mostly empty space waste almost all their compute, so SECOND (2018) replaced them with sparse convolutions, which compute outputs only at active voxels and keep a hash table of where they are; "submanifold" sparse convolutions even keep the set of active sites fixed, so sparsity does not dilate layer by layer. Sparse-convolution backbones are the accuracy reference for lidar detection, and their irregular memory access is a problem on most embedded accelerators.

PointPillars (2019) made lidar detection fast with one simplification: no vertical voxelisation at all. Space is divided into vertical columns, pillars, on a 2D grid. Each point inside a pillar is decorated to 9 values: its x, y, z and intensity, its offset from the mean of the points in its pillar, and its offset from the pillar's centre. A PointNet (one linear layer with batch normalisation and ReLU) lifts each point to 64 features and a max over the pillar's points gives one 64-vector per pillar. These vectors are scattered back to their grid positions, producing a dense 64-channel "pseudo-image", and from there everything is an ordinary 2D CNN and detection head. 7.4CenterPoint: objects as points in the BEV

Early lidar detectors placed anchor boxes at every BEV cell, at several fixed orientations, and regressed offsets from them, which fits poorly with objects that can be rotated arbitrarily. CenterPoint (2021) represents each object by its centre instead. A head on the BEV feature map predicts, for each class, a heatmap that is trained to peak at object centres (a Gaussian bump whose width depends on object size); further heads regress, at each cell, the sub-cell offset of the centre, its height above the ground, the box size (as logarithms), the heading (as sine and cosine, so that the angle wraps smoothly) and the velocity in x and y. Detection is reading local maxima of the heatmap, and because a centre has no orientation, the representation is rotation-invariant by construction. A second stage can sample features at the box's faces to refine it.

The velocity head makes tracking almost trivial. Each detected centre is moved back by its predicted velocity times the frame interval, and greedily matched to the nearest track centre of the previous frame; unmatched detections start new tracks. This simple scheme was competitive with far more elaborate trackers on nuScenes and Waymo. The CenterPoint head is also used on camera BEV grids, which is why the head design of camera and lidar BEV systems is often identical.

7.5Camera–lidar fusion networks

Camera and lidar complement each other almost perfectly: the lidar gives geometry that the camera can only infer, the camera gives semantics, colour and resolution at range that the lidar lacks. Where in the network they meet defines four families (Figure 7.2).

Point decoration projects every lidar point into the camera images and appends what the image says there. PointPainting (2020) appends the per-pixel class scores of a 2D segmentation network; MVX-Net appends image features. The decorated points then enter an ordinary lidar network. It is simple and effective, and brittle: a small calibration or time offset paints a pedestrian's class onto the wall behind her, and a lidar point that is visible to the lidar but occluded in the camera gets the wrong colour. Proposal-level fusion (MV3D, AVOD, Frustum PointNets) generates candidate boxes in one modality and refines them with features cropped from the other; Frustum PointNets, for example, run a 2D detector and then a PointNet on the lidar points inside each detection's frustum. It depends heavily on the first modality's recall. BEV-level fusion turns each modality into its own BEV grid in the same frame, with a Lift-Splat camera branch and a pillar or voxel lidar branch, concatenates the two grids and fuses them with a few convolutions. BEVFusion (2022) made this the reference design: it is modular, each branch can be trained and replaced separately, the convolutional fuser absorbs small misalignments, and if one sensor fails, the other's grid is still meaningful. Query-level fusion (TransFusion, 2022; CMT; DeepFusion) uses lidar features to initialise object queries, for instance from the peaks of a lidar heatmap, and lets the queries attend to image features to refine class and box, which handles calibration errors gracefully because attention can look around the projected position.

Pitfall

A fusion network trained with both sensors always present learns to rely on the stronger one, usually the lidar, and its camera branch atrophies. When the lidar is blocked by dirt or fails, the network does not fall back to a camera-quality result; it collapses. Train with modality dropout (randomly zero one branch's BEV for a fraction of the batches) and measure the single-sensor performance of the fused network as an explicit requirement.

7.6Radar in the BEV

Automotive radar is the sensor that production cars actually have in large numbers, and it brings two things neither camera nor lidar can: a direct measurement of radial velocity from the Doppler shift, and robustness to rain, fog, darkness and low sun. Its points are few (from tens to a few hundred per sensor and cycle for conventional radars, several thousand for 4D imaging radars), noisy in angle, weak or absent in elevation, and polluted by multipath reflections from guard rails and ghost targets. A BEV network uses radar by rasterising its detections into pillars or cells with features such as radar cross-section and compensated radial velocity, accumulating several cycles to get density, and fusing that grid with the camera BEV. Because radar points have no reliable height, they are best used where height does not matter: to fix the range and velocity of objects whose class and extent the camera provides. CenterFusion associates radar points with camera detections, and CRN (2023) uses radar occupancy to guide the camera's view transform, so that camera features are lifted to the depths at which the radar saw something.

8Reading the grid: 3D boxes, occupancy and maps

Once the BEV grid exists, the heads that read it are where the tasks of a driving stack become explicit. They decide what the network is trained to say and how its quality is measured. The three main outputs are 3D boxes for objects, occupancy for space, and maps for the road.

8.13D boxes and how they are scored

A 3D box in the ego frame is usually parametrised by its centre \((x, y, z)\), its size \((l, w, h)\), its heading \(\theta\) around the vertical axis, and for driving datasets its velocity \((v_x, v_y)\) on the ground. Each parameter is encoded so that it is easy to regress. The centre as a sub-cell offset from the cell that predicts it; the size as the logarithm of its ratio to a class mean, so that errors are relative; the heading as \((\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), because regressing an angle directly has a discontinuity at \(\pm\pi\), and the front and back of a car must not be confused. Heads are either dense, CenterPoint-style heatmaps followed by non-maximum suppression or peak extraction, or set-prediction heads in the DETR style, whose object queries are matched one-to-one to the ground truth by the Hungarian algorithm and need no NMS. When NMS is used in BEV, it is done with rotated-box IoU or simply by centre distance.

The benchmarks score boxes differently, and the difference matters when comparing numbers. KITTI and Waymo match detections to ground truth by 3D IoU, typically 0.7 for vehicles and 0.5 for pedestrians, and Waymo adds APH, which weights each true positive by how well its heading matches. nuScenes matches by the distance between centres on the ground, at thresholds of 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 m, and averages AP over these thresholds and ten classes to get mAP. It then measures, for the matched true positives, five error types: translation (ATE, metres), scale (ASE, 1 − IoU after aligning centre and heading), orientation (AOE, radians), velocity (AVE, m/s) and attribute (AAE). The nuScenes detection score combines them:

\[ \mathrm{NDS} = \frac{1}{10}\Big[\,5\,\mathrm{mAP} + \sum_{e \in \{\text{ATE, ASE, AOE, AVE, AAE}\}} \big(1 - \min(1, \mathrm{mTP}_e)\big)\Big]. \]

Half of NDS is mAP, the other half rewards good boxes, and the velocity term is the one single-frame methods lose. For camera-only methods on Waymo, a longitudinal-error-tolerant variant (LET-3D-AP) accepts depth errors along the line of sight up to a fraction of the range, because a strict 3D IoU would score a camera network near zero at 50 m.

Pitfall

A centre-distance threshold of 2 or 4 m is generous for a pedestrian: a detection 1.9 m off in range is a true positive in nuScenes mAP, and would be a miss in any emergency-braking evaluation. A BEV network's benchmark score says little about whether it meets a function's accuracy requirement; evaluate per range band, per class and with the matching tolerance the function needs (the training chapter covers slices and operating points).

8.2Occupancy: describing space instead of objects

Boxes assume that everything relevant is a cuboid of a known class. Occupancy drops that assumption: the head predicts, for each voxel of a 3D grid around the car, whether it is occupied, usually with a semantic class and sometimes with a motion vector (occupancy flow). Overhanging loads, open doors, fallen trees, construction equipment and debris are all just occupied voxels. The deep-learning chapter introduced the idea; here are the parts that matter in practice.

The standard camera benchmark, Occ3D-nuScenes, uses a 200 × 200 × 16 grid of 0.4 m voxels from −40 to 40 m and −1 to 5.4 m in height, with 17 semantic classes plus free. Its labels are made by accumulating lidar sweeps over a sequence, separating static scenery from moving objects, and filling surfaces. Crucially, each voxel also carries a visibility mask: voxels that no camera could see, such as the inside of a building or the space behind a truck, are excluded from the evaluation, because no network could know what is there. The metric is mean IoU over classes. Because voxel IoU can be improved by predicting thick surfaces, a ray-based IoU (RayIoU) that compares the first occupied voxel along query rays was proposed as a fairer alternative.

A camera occupancy network lifts features into a 3D volume instead of a flat grid. Some keep an explicit voxel grid (a Lift-Splat with height bins, or BEVFormer-style queries per voxel), some use three orthogonal planes (tri-perspective view, TPV), and deployment-oriented designs such as FlashOcc keep a 2D BEV and reshape its channels into height at the end (channel-to-height), so that all heavy layers stay 2D. The memory argument is direct: 200 × 200 × 16 voxels with 64 channels is 41 M values, 16 times a 2D grid with the same channels.

Why it matters

For safety argumentation, occupancy is attractive because it does not depend on the class list: an object that was never labelled can still block the way. In SOTIF terms it reduces the "unknown hazardous" space for objects of unusual shape. The catch is evaluation: a voxel-level mIoU of 40% says nothing about whether the one voxel column that matters, the tow bar at bumper height, was detected. Per-region and per-height requirements are needed, exactly as for boxes.

8.3Maps: from semantic rasters to vectors

The first BEV map heads predicted a semantic raster: for each cell, the probability of drivable area, lane divider, road boundary or pedestrian crossing, trained with a per-cell loss and scored by IoU. HDMapNet (2022) turned such rasters into vector elements with post-processing. A planner, however, needs the structure: which lane dividers bound the ego lane, where the stop line is, how lanes connect. Vectorized map heads predict that directly. MapTR (2023) represents every map element as a fixed number of ordered points, for example 20 per polyline, predicted by a DETR-style decoder with one query per element and one per point. A polyline has two equivalent point orders (either end can be the start) and a closed polygon has as many as twice its number of points (any start, either direction), so MapTR matches predictions to ground truth over all equivalent orderings, which removes an ambiguity that otherwise makes training unstable. Map elements are scored by average precision with a Chamfer-distance threshold (0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 m).

Online vector maps are what lets a car drive lane-level without a pre-built HD map, or check that the HD map still matches the road. Newer work predicts lane centrelines and their connectivity as a graph (lane topology), and propagates the map over time (StreamMapNet) so that elements stay consistent as the car moves.

8.4Several heads on one grid

A production BEV network has several heads on one shared grid: detection, map, occupancy, sometimes motion prediction. Sharing saves the cost of the image encoder and the view transform, which dominate, and the tasks help each other: a network that knows where the lanes are places cars better. The price is training complexity. Losses on very different scales compete for the shared trunk and must be weighted, tasks converge at different speeds, and a change for one head can degrade another, so every head needs its own regression tests. End-to-end research systems such as UniAD (2023) chain detection, tracking, mapping, motion prediction and planning in one network with queries passed between stages; production stacks so far keep explicit interfaces between perception and planning, because those interfaces are where requirements, tests and safety arguments attach.

9Objects over time and the space between them: tracking and free space

A box in one frame is a measurement; a function needs objects with identities, histories and velocities, and it needs to know where the car can go. This chapter covers how tracking works on BEV outputs and how free space is derived, with the distinction between "free" and "not seen" that functions most often get wrong.

9.1Tracking in the BEV

Tracking in BEV is easier than in the image in every respect that matters: positions are metric, ego motion can be removed exactly with the vehicle's pose, motion models are physical (constant velocity, constant turn rate), and objects do not change scale with distance. The standard approach is tracking by detection, with the loop the deep-learning chapter and the fusion chapter describe: predict each track to the current time, associate detections to tracks by centre distance or IoU with gating, update the matched tracks with a Kalman filter, and manage births and deaths. BEV detectors that regress velocity simplify the association, as in CenterPoint's greedy matching of back-projected centres.

Query-based networks can track inside the network. In MOTR-style trackers and in StreamPETR, a query that detected an object in one frame is carried into the next, moved by ego motion, and continues to represent the same object, so identity is a property of the query and association is learned; new queries detect newcomers. This removes hand-tuned association logic but makes the tracker's behaviour harder to specify and test. Tracking quality is scored on nuScenes with AMOTA (multi-object tracking accuracy averaged over recall thresholds), AMOTP and the number of identity switches.

9.2Free space: free, occupied and unknown

Free space is the part of the ground that is known to be drivable and known to be empty. Both halves matter. Drivable area from a map head is not free space, because a parked car stands on drivable area; and an empty-looking region is not free space if no sensor could see it. The correct construction keeps three states, as an occupancy grid does: occupied where something was detected, free where a sensor's line of sight passed through without hitting anything, and unknown everywhere else, including behind every obstacle and outside every field of view. In a BEV grid this is computed by casting rays from each camera's or lidar's position through the grid: cells along a ray up to the first occupied cell are free, cells beyond it are occluded.

For a function, the output is often simplified to a polygon around the car or a radial profile, the distance to the first obstacle for each of a few hundred angle bins, which is compact and easy to consume. Parking functions use a fine near-field occupancy grid from the fisheye cameras, often fused with ultrasonic sensors. Whatever the form, the free-space output must carry the unknown state through to the planner, and the planner must treat it conservatively; collapsing unknown into free is how a system drives into the pedestrian it could not see.

In practice

A learned BEV network produces plausible content in occluded regions, because it was trained on scenes where something plausible was usually there. The free-space logic must therefore not take the network's "nothing here" at face value in a region no camera could see. Either the network is trained with visibility masks and an explicit unknown class, or a geometric visibility computation from the sensor positions and the detected obstacles overrides its output.

Talk track

"Tracking in BEV is metric and ego-motion-compensated, so a constant-velocity Kalman filter with centre-distance association works well, and velocity heads make association almost trivial; query-based trackers carry identity in the query. Free space needs three states, free, occupied and unknown, computed by ray casting from the sensors; drivable area is not free space, and unknown must never be treated as free."

10On an automotive SoC with fisheye surround cameras

Almost everything in the BEV literature is developed on roof-mounted pinhole cameras and evaluated on a data-centre GPU. A production system often has four fisheye cameras low on the body and runs on an SoC with an NPU that dislikes irregular memory access. This chapter collects what changes in the geometry, in the compute and in the handling of calibration. How networks are exported, quantised and profiled in general is in the edge AI chapter.

10.1What changes with fisheye cameras

There is no single matrix K. A fisheye projects a ray at angle \(\theta\) from the optical axis to an image radius \(r(\theta)\), for example \(r = f\theta\) for the equidistant model or a polynomial in \(\theta\) for the Kannala–Brandt model (camera geometry 4.5). The lift therefore cannot use \(K^{-1}\); it needs, for every feature-map position, the unit ray of that pixel, which the inverse fisheye model gives and which is precomputed into a table. This is no burden for a Lift-Splat network, whose geometry was a table of constants anyway: the frustum points become depth times the ray from the table, and the splat is unchanged. A backward method needs the forward direction, 3D point to fisheye pixel, which is also a closed-form function of the model and can be evaluated or tabulated.

Depth must be measured along the ray. A pinhole network's depth bins are distances along the optical axis, the camera's z. A 190° fisheye sees rays more than 90° from its axis, whose z is zero or negative, so "depth along z" is undefined or negative for them. Depth bins along the ray, or bins of horizontal range on the ground, are the consistent choice. This is the first thing that breaks when pinhole BEV code is pointed at fisheye images.

Resolution varies strongly across the image. Pixels near the centre of a fisheye cover a small angle, those near the edge a larger one, and the near-field ground beside the car is imaged at very high resolution while objects 15 m to the side are tiny. The view transform has to cope with very different feature quality in different parts of the grid, and the depth distribution must be wide where the image is coarse.

The useful range is short and dominated by the ground. Cameras at 0.6 to 1.0 m height, pitched down, see the road in most of their image and little above 2 to 3 m nearby. The BEV range is accordingly ±10 to 15 m at 0.1 to 0.2 m cells, the depth bins cover perhaps 0.25 to 10 m, and IPM-like geometry is strong. The surround rig in experiments/lss.py, four 190° cameras at 640 × 480 (features 40 × 30) with 40 bins of 0.25 m, gives 4 × 40 × 30 × 40 = 192 000 frustum points and a 23 MiB FP16 frustum; cells receive 27 points on average at 2 to 4 m from the vehicle centre, 6 at 6 to 8 m, 3 at 8 to 10 m, and only 1 beyond 10 m, where 62% of the cells are empty.

The vehicle sees itself. Bumper and mirror cameras image parts of the car body, and the area under and directly beside the car is invisible. Image masks remove the body pixels from the lift, and the grid cells under the car are marked as unknown, not free.

The data is scarce. Public BEV datasets use pinhole rings; WoodScape is the main public fisheye dataset, with segmentation and detection labels rather than full 3D BEV ground truth. Fisheye BEV networks are therefore trained largely on in-house data, often with lidar-equipped recording vehicles providing the labels, and synthetic data from simulators that render true fisheye projections (rasterization versus path tracing) fills gaps. Rectifying the fisheye images to virtual pinhole views to reuse pinhole networks is possible for the central part of the image, but a pinhole cannot represent 180°, so several virtual views per camera are needed and the periphery, where the near-field side of the car is, suffers most.

10.2Compute, memory and operators

On an SoC the budget is typically a few tens of milliseconds for the whole network at 10 to 30 Hz, shared with other functions, and the costs fall into three parts. The image encoders are dense convolutions on four to eight images and usually the largest part of the compute; they are well supported by every NPU, and image resolution and backbone choice are the main levers. The view transform is small in arithmetic and large in trouble. Lift-Splat with a precomputed index table is a gather and a segmented sum, which can be expressed with standard operators or a small custom kernel; the lifted frustum is the largest intermediate tensor (30 MiB in FP16 for the research ring) unless the outer product and the pooling are fused so that it is never materialised. Backward methods need bilinear sampling at computed positions, the GridSample and deformable-attention pattern that the edge AI chapter lists among the usual deployment blockers; where the NPU lacks it, the operation falls back to the GPU or CPU, with a copy each way (edge AI 4.3). The BEV encoder and heads are dense 2D convolutions again, with a cost proportional to the number of cells.

Quantization deserves care at the view transform. The splat sums many contributions into one cell, from 1 to over 100 in the experiment's density table, so the dynamic range of the pooled features varies by two orders of magnitude across the grid; an INT8 scale chosen for the near field clips nothing at range but resolves it coarsely, and one chosen for the far field saturates near the car. Keeping the view transform in FP16, or normalising by the count, avoids most of this. Temporal fusion adds memory traffic: the previous grid (or several) is stored, warped by ego motion with a resampling operation, and read again every frame; at 20 MB per grid and 20 Hz, that is hundreds of megabytes per second of DRAM bandwidth for history alone.

In practice

A deployment-friendly camera BEV network looks like this: a moderate backbone at reduced resolution, a Lift-Splat view transform with depth supervision and a precomputed index table (BEVPoolv2 style), a 2D BEV with height encoded in channels (FlashOcc style) for occupancy, temporal fusion by concatenating one or two warped previous grids, and CenterPoint-style heads. Each choice replaces an operation accelerators handle badly with one they handle well, at a small accuracy cost relative to the best published method.

10.3Calibration at runtime

The frustum geometry depends on each vehicle's own extrinsics, measured at the end of the production line and updated by online calibration. The index table must therefore be computed on the vehicle, at start-up from the stored calibration and again whenever the online calibration publishes a new set, rather than baked into the network at export. Computing it is cheap (a few hundred thousand points, milliseconds on a CPU core), but it has to be versioned and checked like any other safety-relevant data: a corrupted or stale calibration does not crash the network, it silently moves every object. Production systems attach a calibration identifier to every frame's output, monitor the calibration's plausibility (for example the alignment of lane markings or the ground plane between overlapping cameras), and degrade the function when the calibration is invalid, as the camera geometry chapter describes for the classic views.

Talk track

"With fisheyes there is no K: the lift uses a per-pixel ray table from the fisheye model, depth bins go along the ray because rays beyond 90° have no positive optical-axis depth, the range is short and ground-dominated, the car body is masked and the area under it is unknown. On the SoC, the image encoders dominate compute but the view transform dominates engineering: a precomputed Lift-Splat gather is NPU-friendly, deformable attention often is not. The index table depends on each car's calibration, so it is built on the vehicle at start-up and on every calibration update."

11Failure modes

A BEV network fails differently from a 2D detector, because it combines geometry, learned depth and memory. Its errors are often spatially structured: streaks along rays, doubled objects at seams, static objects that drift. Knowing the patterns makes them recognisable in a visualisation and explainable to a safety engineer. Figure 11.1 shows the four that matter most.

11.1Occlusion: the network does not know what it did not see

A camera sees the first surface along each ray. Everything behind the van, behind the car ahead or behind a wall is unobserved, and a camera BEV network trained on complete labels (lidar-derived boxes that include partly hidden objects, and occupancy accumulated over a whole sequence) learns to predict content in regions it cannot see. Sometimes this is useful, because a car's hidden half is predictable from its visible half. Sometimes it produces confident hallucinations: a free road behind the van, because roads are usually free. Temporal fusion helps, since an object seen a second ago can persist in memory, and can also hurt, since a memory can keep a vehicle that has left. The defences are visibility-aware training (masks or labels that distinguish visible from inferred), explicit unknown states in the free-space output (Section 9.2), and evaluation split by visibility level, so that a good score on visible objects does not hide a poor one on occluded objects.

11.2Missing depth: smears and duplicates along the ray

Every camera BEV error that is not a classification error is, at bottom, a depth error, and it has a direction: along the ray from the camera. In a Lift-Splat network an uncertain depth distribution spreads the feature over a streak of cells, and the detector may place the box too near or too far, or produce an elongated box. In a backward network the same image feature is collected by every cell whose pillar projects onto it, so an object can be detected twice or three times at different ranges on the same ray; these "ray duplicates" are a known artefact of query-based methods, and the non-maximum suppression in BEV does not remove them because they are metres apart. Depth errors grow with range (square law for ground-contact cues, linear for size cues, Section 6.2) and are worst for unusual objects whose size the network cannot know, such as a child or a trailer, and on slopes and crests where the ground-contact cue is biased.

11.3Calibration errors

Because the calibration defines where features land, its errors move features before the network reasons about them. A yaw error \(\delta\psi\) moves content laterally by \(d\tan\delta\psi\): 0.26 m at 30 m for 0.5°, 0.52 m for 1°. A pitch error moves content in range with IPM's square law for the depth cues that depend on the ground, and shifts the projection of BEVFormer's pillars to wrong image rows. In a single-camera region the effect is a consistent offset, which the network partly absorbs if it was trained with calibration noise; in an overlap region the two cameras disagree and the object appears doubled or stretched, the learned version of a stitching seam. The quantities to know are those of the fusion chapter's error budget: lateral error grows linearly with range and angle, range error from pitch with the square of range.

11.4Synchronisation errors

BEV fusion assumes that all cameras, and the lidar and radar, saw the scene at the same moment, or that their different moments are known and compensated. Three things break this. Cameras that are not triggered together expose up to a frame period apart; an object crossing at 6 m/s moves 0.2 m in 33 ms, so two cameras disagree about where it is, and at 15 m/s relative speed the disagreement is 0.5 m. Rolling-shutter readout spreads one image over 20 to 30 ms from top to bottom. Ego rotation is the largest effect: at a yaw rate of 20°/s, a 30 ms offset rotates the world by 0.6°, a 0.31 m lateral shift at 30 m. Lidar sweeps take 50 to 100 ms per revolution, so the points of one sweep are themselves not simultaneous and must be motion-compensated before projection into an image.

Temporal fusion adds a fourth: the ego pose used to align the previous grid must be the pose at the previous exposure time. At 50 km/h a pose 50 ms off is 0.7 m. If the offset is the same for every frame it largely cancels, because only the relative motion between frames is used; if it varies, static objects appear to move between frames and the network either learns false velocities or learns to distrust its memory. The diagnosis is to plot the predicted velocity of parked cars, which should be zero. The fusion chapter covers timestamps, triggering and PTP in depth.

11.5Rig changes, sensor dropout and the long tail

Several failures are not geometric. A network trained on one camera rig degrades on another even with correct calibration, because its depth cues (the row of the ground contact, the apparent size at a given range) were learned for one height and focal length; this is the domain gap of Section 6.3. A camera that is dirty, blinded by the sun or failed leaves a sector of the grid without input; a network that never saw this in training may produce unpredictable content there instead of an honest "unknown", which is why camera-dropout augmentation and explicit sensor-health inputs belong to the training recipe, and why the function must degrade (graceful degradation). Weather, night, glare and unusual objects shift the input distribution as for any network (domain shift), with the twist that a BEV network's errors appear as spatial artefacts that a downstream tracker may smooth into a believable, wrong object.

11.6A debugging order

When a BEV network misbehaves, check the geometry before the learning. First, project the ground-truth boxes and the lidar points into every camera image with the calibration the network uses; if they do not land on the objects, the problem is calibration, axes or timestamps, not the network. Second, render the ground truth and the predictions into the BEV grid as an image, with the cameras' fields of view drawn; transposed axes, mirrored y and wrong grid origins become obvious. Third, look at the predicted depth distributions of a few pixels against lidar depth. Fourth, split the metrics by range band, by camera and by whether the object is in an overlap; an error concentrated in one camera or one seam points at its calibration, an error growing with range at depth. Only then tune the model.

Calibration errors move features: lateral error grows with range times yaw, range error with range squared times pitch, and overlaps show doubled objects. QSelf-check questions

Core questions with short model answers; the chapter sections hold the depth. The second group checks understanding with "why" and "what if" questions rather than definitions.

Q.1Core questions

What is BEV perception and why is it used?Basic

Transforming features from all cameras (and lidar or radar) into one metric top-down grid around the vehicle and running detection, mapping and occupancy heads there. It represents each object once even across camera seams, makes adding other sensors and past frames natural, and outputs in the frame planning uses, instead of fusing per-camera detections after the fact.

What is inverse perspective mapping and what are its limitations?Basic

A homography from image to a flat road plane derived from the calibration, \(H = K[r_1\, r_2\, t]\). It is exact only for points on the plane: anything with height is smeared away from the camera, and range errors grow with the square of distance times the pitch or slope error, so one degree costs about 6 m at 20 m for a camera at 1.5 m.

Explain Lift-Splat-Shoot.Intermediate

For each image feature, predict a softmax over D depth bins and a context vector; their outer product places the context at each depth with that bin's weight (lift). Each (pixel, bin) has a 3D position from intrinsics and extrinsics; points are sum-pooled into BEV cells (splat). The grid then feeds a BEV encoder and heads (the original "shoot" was a planner). It is end to end differentiable and keeps depth uncertainty explicit.

How does BEVFormer differ from Lift-Splat?Intermediate

It works backwards: learnable BEV queries, one per cell, each with a pillar of reference points at several heights, project into the cameras and sample image features by deformable attention. No depth distribution is predicted; attention weights decide what belongs to the cell. Temporal self-attention attends to the ego-motion-aligned previous BEV. It needs gather/grid-sample operations that are harder on NPUs, and can produce duplicates along rays.

Why did BEVDepth improve on LSS-style methods?Intermediate

Because depth learned only through the detection loss is poor. BEVDepth supervises the depth distribution with lidar points projected into the images, makes the depth head camera-aware by feeding intrinsics and extrinsics, and refines the frustum; better depth puts features in the right cells, which was worth several points of nuScenes score.

How is temporal information used in BEV networks?Intermediate

The previous BEV (or object queries) is warped into the current ego frame using the relative ego pose between the two exposure times, then concatenated (BEVDet4D), attended to (BEVFormer) or propagated (StreamPETR). It provides velocity, persistence through occlusion and stereo depth from the vehicle's motion. Wrong poses or timestamps make static objects appear to move.

What are the main sources of depth for a camera-only system, and how do their errors scale?Intermediate

Ground contact point: IPM-like, error grows with range squared and with pitch/slope errors. Size priors: error proportional to range and to the size uncertainty. Temporal stereo: \(d^2\delta p/(f b)\) with the baseline from ego motion, weak for objects straight ahead. Spatial stereo in overlaps: limited by overlap and viewpoint difference.

Explain PointNet.Basic

A network on point sets: the same MLP is applied to every point, then a symmetric function (max-pool) aggregates over points, so the output does not depend on the order of points. PointNet++ adds local structure by sampling centres, grouping neighbours and applying PointNets hierarchically.

How does PointPillars work, and why is it popular on embedded hardware?Intermediate

Points are grouped into vertical pillars on a 2D grid, each point decorated to 9 features, a small PointNet with max-pool gives one 64-vector per pillar, and the vectors are scattered to a dense 2D pseudo-image processed by a normal CNN and detection head. Everything heavy is dense 2D convolution, which every accelerator runs well; there are no 3D or sparse convolutions.

What is CenterPoint and why centres instead of anchors?Intermediate

A BEV detector that predicts a per-class heatmap of object centres and regresses offset, height, log-size, heading (sin, cos) and velocity at each centre. A centre has no orientation, so the method is rotation-invariant and needs no anchors at multiple angles; velocity regression makes tracking a greedy nearest-centre association.

What are the ways to fuse camera and lidar in a network?Intermediate

Point decoration (PointPainting: append image segmentation scores to lidar points), proposal-level (Frustum PointNets, MV3D), BEV-level (BEVFusion: concatenate camera BEV and lidar BEV and fuse with convolutions) and query-level (TransFusion: lidar-initialised queries attend to image features). BEV-level is modular and degrades gracefully; decoration is the most sensitive to calibration and time offsets.

What does radar add to a BEV network?Basic

Direct radial velocity via Doppler, range at long distance, and robustness to weather and darkness, at low cost. Its points are sparse, noisy in angle, lack reliable height and include multipath ghosts, so it is used to fix range and velocity of objects whose class and extent come from the camera, often by rasterising accumulated radar points into a BEV grid fused with the camera BEV.

How is 3D detection evaluated on nuScenes?Intermediate

Matching by centre distance on the ground at 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 m, AP averaged over thresholds and ten classes (mAP), plus five true-positive errors: translation, scale, orientation, velocity, attribute. NDS = (5·mAP + Σ(1 − min(1, error)))/10, so half the score rewards box quality, including velocity.

What is an occupancy network, and how is it evaluated?Intermediate

A head that predicts for each voxel around the car whether it is occupied, with a semantic class and possibly a flow vector, so arbitrary shapes and unknown objects are represented. Benchmarks such as Occ3D use 0.4 m voxels, labels accumulated from lidar, a camera visibility mask so unseen voxels are not scored, and mIoU, or ray-based IoU to avoid rewarding thick surfaces.

How do vectorized map heads like MapTR work?Advanced

Each map element (divider, boundary, crossing) is a fixed number of ordered points predicted by a DETR-style decoder with element and point queries. Because a polyline can start at either end and a polygon at any vertex in either direction, matching to ground truth takes the best over all equivalent orderings. Evaluation is AP with Chamfer-distance thresholds.

How do you derive free space from a BEV network?Basic

From occupancy or detections, cast rays from each sensor position through the grid: cells before the first obstacle are free, the obstacle cells occupied, cells beyond and outside fields of view unknown. Output as a grid or a radial distance profile; keep unknown separate, because drivable is not free and unseen is not empty.

What changes when you run BEV perception with fisheye surround cameras instead of nuScenes pinhole cameras?Advanced

Lifting uses a per-pixel ray table from the fisheye model instead of \(K^{-1}\); depth bins go along the ray because rays beyond 90° have no positive optical-axis depth; resolution varies strongly over the image; the range is short and ground-dominated, so grids are ±10–15 m at fine cells; body pixels are masked and the area under the car is unknown; public 3D training data is scarce, so in-house and synthetic data are needed.

What makes BEV networks hard to deploy on an automotive SoC?Advanced

The view transform: scatter-add or gather with computed indices, grid-sample and deformable attention are poorly supported by NPUs and can fall back to CPU/GPU. Mitigations are precomputed index tables (BEVPoolv2), height-in-channels designs, PETR-style position embeddings, keeping the view transform in FP16, and bounding temporal memory. Image backbones dominate compute but are easy.

How do calibration errors show up in a BEV network?Intermediate

They move features before the network reasons: yaw errors shift content laterally by range × angle (0.26 m at 30 m for 0.5°), pitch errors bias depth with the square of range, and in overlaps two cameras disagree, producing doubled or stretched objects. Mitigate with calibration augmentation, camera-aware inputs, online calibration and calibration monitoring.

How would you debug a BEV network whose boxes are offset or mirrored?Intermediate

Check geometry first: project ground truth and lidar into every image with the network's calibration; render ground truth and predictions into the BEV image with camera fields of view; verify axis conventions, grid origin, augmentation matrices and yaw definitions; then check depth distributions against lidar and split errors by camera, seam and range.

Q.2Why and what if

Why does an IPM range error grow with the square of distance?Basic

Range is \(d = h/\tan\beta\) for a viewing angle \(\beta\) below the horizontal, and \(\partial d/\partial\beta = -(h^2 + d^2)/h \approx -d^2/h\). Far points are seen at grazing angles, where a small change in angle slides the intersection with the road a long way.

What if the camera is mounted higher? Does IPM get better or worse?Basic

Better: the angle sensitivity \(d^2/h\) falls with height, so a camera at 2.5 m has 40% less range error per degree than one at 1.5 m, and the smear of objects shrinks because more of them are below the camera. This is one reason trucks' high cameras see further on the ground.

Why use a depth distribution rather than a single depth per pixel in Lift-Splat?Intermediate

It keeps uncertainty: an unsure pixel spreads its feature along the ray instead of committing to a possibly wrong point, a pixel at a depth discontinuity can put mass at two depths, and the softmax keeps the whole operation differentiable so depth can be learned through the task loss. The cost is a D-times larger intermediate tensor.

Why are far BEV cells sparse in a camera BEV, and who fills them?Intermediate

Rays from the feature map fan out with distance and depth bins have fixed spacing, so the number of frustum points per cell falls with range: about 120 per cell within 5 m and 3 at 40–51 m in the research ring, with half of the far cells empty. The BEV encoder's convolutions spread features between rays; the far field is effectively interpolated.

What if the depth head is perfectly sharp but biased by 10% at range?Intermediate

Features land in single, confident, wrong cells: an object at 40 m appears at 44 m with a clean box, which is worse for downstream logic than a smeared one because nothing signals the error. Temporal stereo, size priors and fusion with radar range are the usual corrections; evaluation per range band reveals the bias.

Why do backward (query) methods produce duplicates along a ray?Intermediate

Several BEV cells on the same ray project their reference pillars onto the same image region and sample the same features. Without explicit depth, only learned attention, context and temporal cues decide which cell owns the object; when they fail, two or three cells claim it at different ranges, metres apart, so BEV NMS does not merge them.

What if you change the camera height by 15 cm on a new vehicle and only update the calibration?Intermediate

The geometry of the lift is right, but the network's learned cues are not: the row of the ground contact for a given range and the apparent size relations changed, so depth estimates shift systematically, roughly in proportion to the height change for ground-based cues. Plan fine-tuning on the new rig or train camera-aware with several rigs.

Why does a single-frame camera BEV network have a large velocity error?Basic

One image contains no motion information except blur and pose cues, so velocity can only be guessed from context. Temporal fusion supplies displacement between aligned frames, which is what velocity is; nuScenes velocity errors drop substantially when history is added.

What if ego-motion alignment uses the pose at inference time instead of exposure time?Intermediate

If the latency is constant, the error largely cancels because only relative motion between frames matters; if it varies (jitter, dropped frames, variable queueing), the alignment is wrong by speed × latency difference, static objects shift between frames, and the network learns false velocities or distrusts memory. The test is that parked cars must have zero predicted velocity.

Why is PointPainting more sensitive to calibration than BEVFusion?Intermediate

PointPainting copies the image class at the exact pixel where each lidar point projects, so a fraction of a degree or a few tens of milliseconds paints labels onto the wrong surface, especially at object edges. BEVFusion fuses two grids with convolutions whose receptive fields span several cells, so small misalignments are absorbed.

What if a fusion network loses its lidar at runtime?Intermediate

If it was trained with both sensors always present, its camera branch may be underused and the output can collapse rather than degrade to camera-only quality. Training with modality dropout and testing single-sensor performance as a requirement make it degrade gracefully; the function must also know the sensor is gone and reduce its scope.

Why is "drivable area" not the same as free space?Basic

Drivable area is a property of the road (where driving is allowed), free space a property of the moment (where nothing is). A parked car stands on drivable area; a region behind a van is drivable but unknown. Free space needs occupancy and visibility, not just road segmentation.

Why does a 190° fisheye break pinhole Lift-Splat code?Advanced

The code uses \(K^{-1}\) and depth along the optical axis. A fisheye has no linear K, and rays more than 90° from the axis have zero or negative z, so "depth along z" cannot place them; points end up behind the camera or at infinity. The fix is a per-pixel unit-ray table from the fisheye model and depth along the ray.

Why can INT8 quantization of the splat output be tricky?Advanced

The pooled feature is a sum over a variable number of points, from one at range to over a hundred near the car, so its magnitude varies by two orders of magnitude across the grid. One INT8 scale either saturates the near field or resolves the far field coarsely. Keep the view transform in FP16 or normalise by the count before quantizing.

What if two overlapping fisheyes disagree by 1° in yaw?Intermediate

At 5 m their contributions to the same object differ by about 9 cm, tolerable; at 10 m by 17 cm. In the near field this mostly blurs edges, but it produces doubled kerbs and parking-slot lines in the overlap, which is exactly where parking functions need precision; overlap consistency is also a good online calibration signal.

Why might a BEV network with excellent nuScenes NDS still be unsuitable for emergency braking?Intermediate

NDS matches pedestrians within up to 4 m of centre distance, averages over classes, ranges and thresholds, and includes terms irrelevant to braking. Braking needs high recall and accurate range for objects in the ego corridor at a specific operating point, with tight tolerances, per range band and condition; those must be measured separately.

Why do BEV networks hallucinate in occluded regions, and is it always bad?Intermediate

They are trained on labels that include partly hidden objects and on scenes where occluded space is usually free, so they learn priors for what is there. Completing a half-visible car is useful; declaring the space behind a van free is dangerous. Visibility-aware training, explicit unknown states and evaluation by visibility separate the two.

AMOTA
Average multi-object tracking accuracy over recall thresholds; the main nuScenes tracking metric.
Bird's-eye view (BEV)
A top-down metric grid in the ego frame in which perception outputs and intermediate features are represented.
BEVDepth
Lift-Splat-style detector whose depth distribution is supervised with projected lidar and conditioned on calibration.
BEVFormer
Backward-projection BEV network: BEV queries sample image features by deformable spatial cross-attention and attend to the aligned previous BEV.
BEVFusion
Camera–lidar fusion by concatenating a camera BEV and a lidar BEV and fusing them with convolutions.
BEV pooling (voxel pooling)
The splat: summing the features of all frustum points that fall into each BEV cell; BEVPoolv2 precomputes the indices.
CenterPoint
BEV detector that finds objects as heatmap peaks at their centres and regresses size, heading and velocity.
Deformable attention
Attention that samples a few learned offsets around a reference point instead of attending to all positions.
Ego frame
Vehicle-fixed coordinate frame; in ISO 8855 x forward, y left, z up, origin below the rear axle.
Free space
Ground that is known to be drivable and known to be empty; requires distinguishing free from unknown.
Frustum
The pyramid of space a camera sees; in Lift-Splat, the set of 3D points of all pixels times depth bins.
Inverse perspective mapping (IPM)
Mapping image pixels to a ground plane with a homography under a flat-world assumption.
Lift-Splat-Shoot (LSS)
View transform that lifts features with a per-pixel depth distribution and sum-pools them into BEV cells.
MapTR
Vectorized online map network representing elements as ordered point sets with permutation-equivalent matching.
NDS
nuScenes detection score: half mAP (centre-distance matching), half true-positive error terms.
Occupancy grid / network
Per-cell or per-voxel prediction of occupied, free (and unknown), optionally with class and flow.
PETR
Sparse-query detector that adds 3D position embeddings of frustum points to image features and uses global attention.
Pillar
A vertical column of a 2D grid; in PointPillars, the unit whose points are encoded by a small PointNet.
PointNet
Network on point sets: shared per-point MLP followed by a symmetric max-pool.
PointPainting
Fusion by appending image segmentation scores to lidar points projected into the image.
Pseudo-image
Dense 2D BEV feature map produced by scattering pillar features to their grid positions.
BEV / object query
Learnable feature vector tied to a BEV cell or a candidate object that gathers information by attention.
Ray duplicates
False detections at several ranges along one camera ray, typical of backward-projection methods.
Sparse convolution
Convolution computed only at active voxels; submanifold variants keep the active set fixed.
StreamPETR
Temporal sparse-query detector that propagates object queries from frame to frame.
Temporal fusion
Combining the current BEV or queries with past ones aligned by ego motion.
Tri-perspective view (TPV)
3D scene representation as three orthogonal feature planes instead of a voxel grid.
View transform
The network stage that maps perspective image features into the BEV grid.
Visibility mask
Marks which voxels or cells a sensor could observe, so unobservable regions are not trained or scored as known.

A cell of a 3D grid; voxel encoders (VoxelNet, SECOND) quantise point clouds into such cells.
